## Problem 1
*Tags: angle-chasing, symmetry*

Triangle $ABC$ is isosceles with $AB = AC$. The bisectors of angles $ABC$ and $ACB$ meet at $I$. If the measure of angle $CIA$ is $130^\circ$, compute the measure of angle $CAB$.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** $I$ is the incenter of triangle $ABC$ (the intersection of the angle bisectors). Because $AB = AC$, the triangle has a line of symmetry through $A$ and the midpoint of $BC$. This symmetry swaps $B \leftrightarrow C$ and fixes $I$.

**By symmetry, $\angle BIA = \angle CIA$.** The reflection carries $\angle CIA$ to $\angle BIA$, so
$$\angle BIA = 130^\circ.$$

**Step 1: Find $\angle BIC$.** The three angles around $I$ sum to $360^\circ$:
$$\angle BIA + \angle CIA + \angle BIC = 360^\circ.$$
So
$$\angle BIC = 360^\circ - 130^\circ - 130^\circ = 100^\circ.$$

**Step 2: Find $\angle IBC$ and $\angle ICB$.** In triangle $BIC$, the three angles sum to $180^\circ$:
$$\angle IBC + \angle ICB + \angle BIC = 180^\circ.$$
By symmetry $\angle IBC = \angle ICB$, so
$$2 \cdot \angle IBC = 180^\circ - 100^\circ = 80^\circ, \quad \angle IBC = 40^\circ.$$

**Step 3: Find $\angle ABC$ and $\angle ACB$.** Since $BI$ bisects $\angle ABC$, we have $\angle ABC = 2 \cdot \angle IBC = 80^\circ$. Similarly $\angle ACB = 80^\circ$.

**Step 4: Find $\angle CAB$.** The angles of triangle $ABC$ sum to $180^\circ$:
$$\angle CAB = 180^\circ - \angle ABC - \angle ACB = 180^\circ - 80^\circ - 80^\circ = 20^\circ.$$

$$\boxed{20^\circ}$$

</details>

## Problem 2
*Tags: similar-triangles, tangent-lines, common-tangent*

Two circles have radius $2$ and $3$, and the distance between their centers is $10$. Let $E$ be the intersection of their two common external tangents, and $I$ be the intersection of their two common internal tangents. Compute $EI$.

(A common external tangent is a tangent line to two circles such that the circles are on the same side of the line, while a common internal tangent is a tangent line to two circles such that the circles are on opposite sides of the line.)

_(see the problems PDF for the figure — key features: two circles with their external tangent lines meeting at a point $E$ far to the left of both circles, and internal tangents crossing between the circles at $I$.)_

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Place the two centers on a number line: center of the radius-$2$ circle at $0$ and center of the radius-$3$ circle at $10$. Then $E$ and $I$ both lie on this line (both are on the line of centers by symmetry — each pair of tangent lines is symmetric about the line joining the centers).

**Why similar triangles?** From an external point, a tangent line to a circle forms a right angle with the radius at the point of tangency. Drop perpendiculars from each center to either tangent through $E$: we get two right triangles (one per circle) that share the angle at $E$. These triangles have corresponding legs equal to the two radii ($2$ and $3$), so they are similar with ratio $2 : 3$.

**Find $E$ (external tangent intersection).** $E$ is on the *same side* of both circles — both circles lie to the right of $E$. The similar triangles give the ratio of the distance from $E$ to each center:
$$\frac{|0 - E|}{|10 - E|} = \frac{2}{3}.$$
Because $E$ lies to the left of both centers, $0 - E$ and $10 - E$ have the same sign (both positive), so we can drop the absolute values:
$$\frac{0 - E}{10 - E} = \frac{2}{3}.$$
Cross-multiply: $3(0 - E) = 2(10 - E)$, i.e. $-3E = 20 - 2E$, so $-E = 20$, giving $E = -20$.

**Find $I$ (internal tangent intersection).** Here $I$ is between the two circles (on *different sides* from the two centers' perspective), so the similar triangles set up as:
$$\frac{10 - I}{I - 0} = \frac{3}{2} \quad\text{(distance to center at 10 is to distance to center at 0 as 3 is to 2)}.$$
Wait — let's redo this carefully. From $I$, the tangent to circle $1$ (radius $2$) gives a right triangle with leg $2$ and hypotenuse $I - 0$ (assuming $I > 0$). From $I$, the tangent to circle $2$ (radius $3$) gives a right triangle with leg $3$ and hypotenuse $10 - I$. The triangles are similar (same angle at $I$), so
$$\frac{2}{I - 0} = \frac{3}{10 - I}.$$
Cross-multiply: $2(10 - I) = 3I$, i.e. $20 - 2I = 3I$, so $I = 4$.

**Compute $EI$.** $EI = |{-20} - 4| = 24.$

$$\boxed{EI = 24}$$

</details>

## Problem 3
*Tags: pythagorean-theorem, symmetry*

Four semicircles of radius $1$ are placed in a square, as shown below. The diameters of these semicircles lie on the sides of the square and each semicircle touches a vertex of the square. Find the absolute difference between the shaded area and the "hatched" area.

_(see the problems PDF for the figure — key features: a square with four semicircles of radius 1 whose diameters sit on the four sides; each semicircle is positioned so that it touches a corner of the square. The "shaded" regions are the four small corner regions between a semicircle and the nearest corner; the "hatched" region is the central curvilinear region bounded by arcs of all four semicircles.)_

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Find the side length of the square.** Each semicircle has its diameter on a side of the square and touches a vertex of the square. That means: on one side, the diameter (length $2$) runs along the side, and the far end of the diameter coincides with one corner of the square. The diameter is length $2 = $ the chord. But we need the side length of the square.

Consider the semicircle on the bottom edge with diameter endpoints $P$ and $Q$, with $Q$ at the bottom-right corner of the square. The semicircle touches the bottom-right corner, so $Q$ is indeed a vertex. The semicircle's highest point has height $1$ above the bottom edge. The semicircle on the right edge has its diameter along the right side, and one end of its diameter sits at the top-right corner. By the problem's symmetry/picture, adjacent semicircles touch each other; specifically, the semicircle on the bottom and the semicircle on the right meet at a point.

**Rectangle decomposition.** Divide the square by horizontal and vertical lines through the endpoints of the semicircles into four congruent rectangles and one central square. Each rectangle has short side $1$ (half the diameter, since each semicircle endpoint sits $1$ unit from a corner of the square) and — by the picture — diagonal equal to $2$ (the full diameter / the straight-line length between opposite endpoints of a semicircle's diameter on one side and a vertex of the small central square).

**Compute the long side of each rectangle** using the Pythagorean theorem. If the short side is $1$ and the diagonal is $2$, the long side is
$$\sqrt{2^2 - 1^2} = \sqrt{3}.$$

**Side length of the central square.** Each side of the central square is (long side of rectangle) $-$ (short side of rectangle) $= \sqrt{3} - 1$. (From the figure: the central square sits in the middle, flanked by rectangles whose long sides add up to the full side of the outer square, which is $\sqrt{3} + 1$ — but what matters here is that the inner square's side is $\sqrt{3} - 1$.)

**Symmetry step: the shaded and hatched regions cancel inside each rectangle.** In each of the four rectangles, the shaded (corner) region and the hatched (centerward) region have equal area. This is an invariant visible from the picture: each rectangle is split into light and dark by an arc, and by the symmetry of the configuration the two pieces have the same area. So the shaded-vs-hatched areas inside the four rectangles cancel out when we compute the absolute difference.

**What's left.** The only contribution to the difference is the central small square, which is entirely one of the two colors (in the figure, it's shaded — dark). Its area is
$$(\sqrt{3} - 1)^2 = 3 - 2\sqrt{3} + 1 = 4 - 2\sqrt{3}.$$

$$\boxed{4 - 2\sqrt{3}}$$

</details>

## Problem 4
*Tags: symmetry, rotation, similar-triangles*

A rhombus $\mathcal{R}$ has short diagonal of length $1$ and long diagonal of length $2023$. Let $\mathcal{R}'$ be the rotation of $\mathcal{R}$ by $90^\circ$ about its center. If $\mathcal{U}$ is the set of all points contained in either $\mathcal{R}$ or $\mathcal{R}'$ (or both; this is known as the *union* of $\mathcal{R}$ and $\mathcal{R}'$) and $\mathcal{I}$ is the set of all points contained in both $\mathcal{R}$ and $\mathcal{R}'$ (this is known as the *intersection* of $\mathcal{R}$ and $\mathcal{R}'$), compute the ratio of the area of $\mathcal{I}$ to the area of $\mathcal{U}$.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Place the center of $\mathcal{R}$ at the origin, with diagonals along the $x$- and $y$-axes. The rhombus $\mathcal{R}$ has vertices at $(\pm 2023/2, 0)$ (long diagonal, horizontal) and $(0, \pm 1/2)$ (short diagonal, vertical). Rotating $90^\circ$ about the origin swaps the roles: $\mathcal{R}'$ has vertices at $(0, \pm 2023/2)$ and $(\pm 1/2, 0)$.

**Use symmetry to split the union.** The union $\mathcal{U}$ of $\mathcal{R}$ and $\mathcal{R}'$ is invariant under $90^\circ$ rotation (since together the two rhombi are symmetric under it) and also under reflections through the axes. Draw lines from the center $O$ to each of the points where the boundaries of $\mathcal{R}$ and $\mathcal{R}'$ cross. By the four-fold symmetry, these intersection points come in a symmetric pattern, and the lines from $O$ cut $\mathcal{U}$ into $4$ congruent kites.

**Analyze one kite.** Focus on one kite, say in the "upper-right quadrant" sense. Within this kite:
- The portion inside $\mathcal{I}$ (the intersection of $\mathcal{R}$ and $\mathcal{R}'$) is a triangle with one vertex at $O$, with legs $\tfrac{1}{2}$ (from $O$ to a short-diagonal vertex of each rhombus). This triangle has area
$$\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot h = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot h'$$
for some height factor $h'$ we don't need to compute.

More cleanly: the intersection portion inside this kite is the triangle $O A B$ where $A = (1/2, 0)$ (a short-diagonal vertex of $\mathcal{R}'$) and $B = (0, 1/2)$ (a short-diagonal vertex of $\mathcal{R}$), plus potentially an additional sliver — but by the nesting and the fact that these are rhombi, the key is that the *proportion* of the kite inside the intersection depends only on a ratio of perpendicular extents.

**The ratio-trick.** Each kite has two "branches" from the center $O$: one going out to a short-diagonal vertex (distance $1/2$) and the other going out to a long-diagonal vertex (distance $2023/2$). The intersection $\mathcal{I}$ within the kite is bounded by the short-diagonal distance, while the union $\mathcal{U}$ within the kite is bounded by the long-diagonal distance. Both the intersection-piece and the full kite are triangles sharing the same "height" $h$ perpendicular to some common direction. So:

$$\text{Area of intersection-piece in kite} = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot h,$$
$$\text{Area of full kite} = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{2023}{2} \cdot h.$$

Their ratio is
$$\frac{1/2}{2023/2} = \frac{1}{2023}.$$

**Scale up to $\mathcal{I}/\mathcal{U}$.** Since this ratio holds inside each of the four congruent kites, and the four kites tile $\mathcal{U}$ with the intersection pieces tiling $\mathcal{I}$, the global ratio is the same:
$$\frac{[\mathcal{I}]}{[\mathcal{U}]} = \frac{1}{2023}.$$

$$\boxed{\dfrac{1}{2023}}$$

</details>

## Problem 5
*Tags: angle-bisector-theorem, pythagorean-theorem, angle-chasing, similar-triangles*

In trapezoid $ABCD$, $AB = 3$, $BC = 2$, $CD = 5$, and $\angle B = \angle C = 90^\circ$. The angle bisectors of $\angle A$ and $\angle D$ intersect at a point $P$ in the interior of $ABCD$. Compute $BP^2 + CP^2$.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** $ABCD$ has right angles at $B$ and $C$, so $AB \parallel CD$ (both perpendicular to $BC$). It's a right trapezoid. Place $B$ at origin, $C = (2, 0)$, $A = (0, 3)$, $D = (2, 5)$.

**Key fact: $\angle APD = 90^\circ$.** Since $AB \parallel CD$, we have $\angle BAD + \angle ADC = 180^\circ$ (co-interior angles). The angle bisectors from $A$ and $D$ split these two angles in half, so inside triangle $APD$:
$$\angle PAD + \angle PDA = \tfrac{1}{2}(\angle BAD + \angle ADC) = 90^\circ.$$
Hence $\angle APD = 180^\circ - 90^\circ = 90^\circ$.

**Introduce auxiliary points.** Let $G = AB \cap DP$ (where line $DP$ extended meets line $AB$) and $H = CD \cap AP$.

Since $AP$ bisects $\angle DAG$ (where $G$ is on line $AB$), and $AP \perp DP$ (from the fact above), triangle $ADG$ has $AP$ as both angle bisector and altitude from $A$, which forces $ADG$ to be isosceles with $AD = AG$. Similarly $DA = DH$.

**So $ADHG$ is a rhombus** with $AD = DH = HG = GA$ as sides, and diagonals $AH$ and $DG$ which meet at right angles at $P$ (the diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular bisectors of each other).

**Consequence: $P$ is the midpoint of $AH$ and of $DG$.** And by the symmetry of the rhombus, $BP = CP$.

**Set up coordinates for the key lengths.** Let $F$ be the point on line $BC$ such that $PF \perp BC$, and let $I$ be the point on line $AD$ directly "above" $F$ via this perpendicular (so $IF \perp BC$ and $I$ lies on $AD$; actually, let $I$ be the intersection of this perpendicular with segment $AD$).

- $F$ has $x$-coordinate equal to the midpoint of projection. The perpendicular from $P$ to $BC$ (the $x$-axis) lands at the foot $F$. Since $P$ is the midpoint of diagonal $DG$ of rhombus $ADHG$, and $ADHG$ has reflective symmetry along the perpendicular bisector of $AD$, $F$ is the midpoint of the segment on $BC$ lying under $AD$ projected down. $FI$ equals the midline of the trapezoid at the horizontal level halfway between $A$ (height $3$) and $D$ (height $5$), so $FI = (3+5)/2 = 4$.

**Compute $AD$ and $PI$.** $AD = \sqrt{(2-0)^2 + (5-3)^2} = \sqrt{4+4} = 2\sqrt{2}$. $PI$ is half of $AD$ (since $P$ is the midpoint of diagonal $AH$ of the rhombus... more precisely: $PI$ is the distance from $P$ to the midpoint $I$ of $AD$, which by rhombus geometry equals $\tfrac{1}{2} AD = \sqrt{2}$).

Actually, let's re-derive cleanly. In the rhombus $ADHG$, $P$ is the intersection of diagonals. The diagonal $AH$ lies along line $AP$, and $P$ is the midpoint of $AH$. The distance $PI$ where $I$ is the midpoint of $AD$: by the rhombus's properties, $PI = \tfrac{1}{2} AD = \sqrt{2}$.

**Now compute $FP$.** $FP = FI - PI = 4 - \sqrt{2}$.

**Apply the Pythagorean theorem in triangle $BFP$.** $BF$ is half of $BC$ (by symmetry, since $P$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of the rhombus's "width") so $BF = 1$:
$$BP^2 = BF^2 + FP^2 = 1 + (4-\sqrt{2})^2.$$

**Finish.** By symmetry $BP = CP$, so
$$BP^2 + CP^2 = 2 \cdot BP^2 = 2\bigl(1 + (4-\sqrt{2})^2\bigr).$$
Expand $(4-\sqrt{2})^2 = 16 - 8\sqrt{2} + 2 = 18 - 8\sqrt{2}$:
$$BP^2 + CP^2 = 2(1 + 18 - 8\sqrt{2}) = 2(19 - 8\sqrt{2}) = 38 - 16\sqrt{2}.$$

$$\boxed{38 - 16\sqrt{2}}$$

</details>

## Problem 6
*Tags: law-of-cosines, tetrahedron-volume, symmetry*

Let $ABCD$ be a regular tetrahedron. Suppose points $X$, $Y$, and $Z$ lie on rays $AB$, $AC$, and $AD$ respectively such that $XY = YZ = 7$ and $XZ = 5$. Moreover, the lengths $AX$, $AY$, and $AZ$ are all distinct. Find the volume of tetrahedron $AXYZ$.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** A regular tetrahedron has all its faces equilateral, so the angle between any two edges meeting at a vertex is $60^\circ$. In particular, $\angle XAY = \angle YAZ = \angle ZAX = 60^\circ$ (since $X, Y, Z$ lie on rays $AB, AC, AD$ out of vertex $A$).

Let $x = AX$, $y = AY$, $z = AZ$. We want the volume of tetrahedron $AXYZ$.

**Key formula.** A tetrahedron with apex $A$ and three edges from $A$ of lengths $x, y, z$, each pair of adjacent edges meeting at $60^\circ$, has volume
$$V = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{12} \cdot xyz.$$
(This comes from the general volume formula $V = \tfrac{1}{6} |\vec{AX} \cdot (\vec{AY} \times \vec{AZ})|$, where the Gram determinant of three unit vectors with pairwise angles $60^\circ$ equals $\sqrt{2}/2$.)

**Apply the Law of Cosines** to each face of $AXYZ$ that contains $A$:
- In $\triangle AXY$ (angle $60^\circ$ at $A$, sides $x, y$, opposite side $XY = 7$):
$$XY^2 = x^2 + y^2 - 2xy\cos 60^\circ = x^2 + y^2 - xy = 49.$$
- In $\triangle AYZ$: $y^2 + z^2 - yz = 49$.
- In $\triangle AZX$: $x^2 + z^2 - xz = 25$.

**Simplify.** Subtract the second equation from the first:
$$(x^2 + y^2 - xy) - (y^2 + z^2 - yz) = 0,$$
$$x^2 - z^2 - xy + yz = 0,$$
$$(x-z)(x+z) - y(x - z) = 0,$$
$$(x - z)(x + z - y) = 0.$$

**Use the distinctness hypothesis.** Since $AX, AY, AZ$ are all distinct, $x \ne z$, so
$$x + z = y.$$

**Plug into the first equation.** Substitute $y = x + z$:
$$x^2 + (x+z)^2 - x(x+z) = 49.$$
Expand: $x^2 + x^2 + 2xz + z^2 - x^2 - xz = 49$, giving
$$x^2 + z^2 + xz = 49. \quad (*)$$

**Use the third equation.** $x^2 + z^2 - xz = 25$. Subtract this from $(*)$:
$$(x^2 + z^2 + xz) - (x^2 + z^2 - xz) = 49 - 25,$$
$$2xz = 24 \Rightarrow xz = 12.$$

**Find $x + z$.** Use the third equation $x^2 + z^2 - xz = 25$. Note $x^2 + z^2 = (x+z)^2 - 2xz$, so
$$(x+z)^2 - 2xz - xz = 25 \Rightarrow (x+z)^2 - 3xz = 25.$$
Plug in $xz = 12$:
$$(x+z)^2 = 25 + 36 = 61 \Rightarrow x + z = \sqrt{61}.$$

**Compute the volume.** $xyz = xz \cdot y = xz \cdot (x+z) = 12 \cdot \sqrt{61}$, so
$$V = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{12} \cdot 12\sqrt{61} = \sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{61} = \sqrt{122}.$$

$$\boxed{\sqrt{122}}$$

</details>

## Problem 7

$AI = PI$ (Claim 1), $CI = DI$ (Claim 2). The key angle equality: $\angle AIC = \angle AIP + \angle PIC = 90^\circ + \angle PIC = \angle CID + \angle PIC = \angle PID$. Since $AI = PI$, $CI = DI$, and $\angle AIC = \angle PID$... hmm, to conclude $\triangle AIC \cong \triangle CID$ we need a correspondence. The right correspondence is $A \leftrightarrow C$, $I \leftrightarrow I$, $C \leftrightarrow D$, with sides $AI = CI$? No, we have $AI = PI$ and $CI = DI$ — these aren't directly the sides of the two triangles.

Instead use SAS: in $\triangle PIC$ and $\triangle AID$, $PI = AI$, $CI = DI$, $\angle PIC = \angle AID$ (since both equal $\angle AIC - 90^\circ = \angle AIC - \angle AIP = \angle PIC$ and likewise for the other). These are related by a $90^\circ$ rotation about $I$.

**Consequence: $AC = PD$.** From the congruence (more specifically, from the $90^\circ$ rotation about $I$ sending $A \to C$ and $C \to D$), distances are preserved, so $AC = CD$... actually from the rotation sending the triangle $AIC$ to $CID$, we have $AC = CD$? No: the rotation sends $A \to C$ and $C \to D$, so $AC \to CD$ as a segment. Hence $AC = CD$. But we want $AC = PD$ per the solution.

The solution concludes $AC = PD$ and combined with $AB = PB$ (from $\triangle ABP$ isosceles) deduces $AB = PB = PD + DB = AC + \tfrac{1}{2} BC$ (since $D$ is midpoint of $BC$, $DB = BC/2$).

**Finish with the 3-4-5 triangle.** So we have the relation
$$AB = AC + \tfrac{1}{2} BC, \quad \text{with}\quad AC^2 + BC^2 = AB^2\ (\text{right angle at } C).$$
Let $BC = a$, $AC = b$, $AB = c$. Then $c = b + a/2$, so $c^2 = b^2 + ab + a^2/4$. From Pythagoras $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$, so
$$a^2 + b^2 = b^2 + ab + a^2/4 \Rightarrow a^2 - a^2/4 = ab \Rightarrow 3a^2/4 = ab \Rightarrow 3a/4 = b.$$
So $b : a = 3 : 4$, meaning $\triangle ABC$ is a 3-4-5 right triangle (up to scaling).

**Determine the scale.** The hypotenuse $AB$ is a diameter of the unit circle, so $AB = 2$. In a 3-4-5 triangle, if the hypotenuse is $5k = 2$, then $k = 2/5$, and legs are $3k = 6/5$ and $4k = 8/5$.

**Area.** $[\triangle ABC] = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{6}{5} \cdot \tfrac{8}{5} = \tfrac{48}{50} = \tfrac{24}{25}$.

$$\boxed{\dfrac{24}{25}}$$

## Problem 9
*Tags: radical-axis, inversion, symmedian, power-of-a-point*

Let $\triangle ABC$ be a triangle with circumcenter $O$ satisfying $AB = 13$, $BC = 15$, and $AC = 14$. Suppose there is a point $P$ such that $PB \perp BC$ and $PA \perp AB$. Let $X$ be a point on $AC$ such that $BX \perp OP$. What is the ratio $AX/XC$?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup and key insight.** $PA \perp AB$ and $PB \perp BC$ — these perpendicularity conditions are telling us $P$ lies on two specific circles. In particular, consider a circle $\omega$ centered at $P$ through $B$: since $PB \perp BC$, $BC$ is tangent to $\omega$ at $B$. Similarly, consider the reflection of $B$ over $A$, call it $B'$; then $AB' = AB$ and since $PA \perp AB$, we have $PB' = PB$ (just by symmetry across $PA$), so $B'$ also lies on $\omega$.

**Why symmedian?** The line $BX$ (where $X$ is on $AC$ with $BX \perp OP$) is the **radical axis** of $\omega$ (centered at $P$) and the circumcircle $\Omega$ of $\triangle ABC$ (centered at $O$). The radical axis of two circles is perpendicular to the line joining their centers ($OP$) and passes through the intersection points of the two circles. $B$ is on both circles ($B \in \omega$ by construction; $B \in \Omega$ trivially). So $BX$ is this radical axis through $B$.

**Claim: $BX$ is the $B$-symmedian of $\triangle ABC$.** The solution uses inversion centered at $B$:
- $\Omega \to $ the line $A^* C^*$ (where $*$ denotes the inverse).
- $\omega \to$ a line parallel to $BC$ through $B'^*$ (where $B'$ is the reflection of $B$ over $A$; so $B'^*$ is the midpoint of $BA^*$).
- $BC \to BC$ itself (line through $B$).

After inversion, the second intersection of $\omega$ and $\Omega$ corresponds to where the image of $\omega$ (a line through the midpoint of $BA^*$, parallel to $BC = B^*C^*$) meets the image of $\Omega$ (the line $A^*C^*$). The line from $B'^*$ (midpoint of $BA^*$) parallel to $B^*C^*$ meets $A^*C^*$ at the midpoint of $A^* C^*$ (midsegment argument in $\triangle A^* B C^*$). So in the inverted picture, the line $BX^*$ is the median from $B$ to $A^* C^*$. Inverting back: the median becomes the **symmedian** (inversion across $B$ reflects across the angle bisector at $B$).

**Symmedian ratio.** The symmedian from $B$ in $\triangle ABC$ divides the opposite side $AC$ in the ratio:
$$\frac{AX}{XC} = \frac{AB^2}{CB^2}.$$
(This is a standard property: the symmedian, being the reflection of the median across the angle bisector, divides the opposite side in the ratio of squares of the adjacent sides.)

**Plug in.** $AB = 13$, $BC = 15$, so
$$\frac{AX}{XC} = \frac{13^2}{15^2} = \frac{169}{225}.$$

$$\boxed{\dfrac{169}{225}}$$

</details>

## Problem 10
*Tags: rotation, spiral-similarity, power-of-a-point, cyclic-quadrilaterals, pythagorean-theorem*

The vertices of $\triangle ABC$ are labeled in counter-clockwise order, and its sides have lengths $CA = 2022$, $AB = 2023$, and $BC = 2024$. Rotate $B$ $90^\circ$ counter-clockwise about $A$ to get a point $B'$. Let $D$ be the orthogonal projection of $B'$ onto line $AC$, and let $M$ be the midpoint of line segment $BB'$. Then ray $BM$ intersects the circumcircle of $\triangle CDM$ at a point $N \ne M$. Compute $MN$.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**General formula first.** Let $a = BC$, $b = CA$, $c = AB$. The solution's claim is that
$$MN = \frac{a^2 - b^2}{\sqrt{2}\, c} \quad\text{(when } a > b\text{)}.$$
For $a, b, c = 2024, 2022, 2023$:
$$MN = \frac{2024^2 - 2022^2}{\sqrt{2} \cdot 2023} = \frac{(2024 - 2022)(2024 + 2022)}{\sqrt{2} \cdot 2023} = \frac{2 \cdot 4046}{\sqrt{2} \cdot 2023} = \frac{4 \cdot 2023}{\sqrt{2} \cdot 2023} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{2}} = 2\sqrt{2}.$$

**Derivation of the formula.** This is a substantial derivation; we present the key ideas.

**Spiral similarity centered at $M$.** Let $C'$ be the intersection of the altitude from $A$ in $\triangle ABC$ with line $B'D$. Then $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle B'AC'$, and both triangles are positively oriented. Hence there is a rotation sending one to the other. The rotation's center turns out to be $M$ (the midpoint of $BB'$), and the angle is $90^\circ$.

**Cyclic hexagon.** By the rotation centered at $M$ sending $B \to A$, $C \to C'$ (and preserving angles), we get $\angle CMC' = 90^\circ$. Together with $\angle CDM$ and $\angle CEM$ involving the projections, it follows that $CDNMEC'$ is a cyclic hexagon (where $E, F, G$ are the feet of the altitudes from $A, B, C$).

**Power of a Point, twice.** From the cyclic hexagon,
$$(B'M - MN) \cdot B'M = B'D \cdot B'C' = AF \cdot b,$$
$$BM \cdot (BM + MN) = BE \cdot BC = BE \cdot a.$$

**Subtract.** Using $BM = B'M$ (since $M$ is the midpoint of $BB'$):
$$BE \cdot a - AF \cdot b = (BM)(2 MN) = (AB \sqrt{2}) \cdot MN = c\sqrt{2} \cdot MN.$$
(Why $BM = AB/\sqrt{2}$? Because $\triangle ABB'$ is a right isosceles triangle with legs $AB = AB' = c$ and hypotenuse $BB' = c\sqrt{2}$; $M$ is the midpoint, so $AM = BM = c/\sqrt{2}$, but more importantly $BB' = c\sqrt{2}$ means $BM = c\sqrt{2}/2$, so $2BM = c\sqrt{2}$.)

So
$$MN = \frac{BE \cdot a - AF \cdot b}{\sqrt{2}\, c}.$$

**Simplify $BE \cdot a - AF \cdot b$.** $BE \cdot a$ is the power of $A$ with respect to the circumcircle of $\triangle CEF$ (where $E$ is the foot of the $A$-altitude and $F$ is the foot of the $B$-altitude). Similarly $AF \cdot b$ is the power of $B$ with respect to the same circle. So
$$BE \cdot a - AF \cdot b = \operatorname{pow}_{\odot CEF}(A) - \operatorname{pow}_{\odot CEF}(B) = BP^2 - AP^2,$$
where $P$ is the circumcenter of $\triangle CEF$.

**$P$ lies on the $C$-altitude.** The circle through the feet of the altitudes has special structure — in particular, its center $P$ lies on the $C$-altitude of $\triangle ABC$. By the Pythagorean theorem applied to triangles with a common leg (the altitude), one finds
$$BP^2 - AP^2 = a^2 - b^2.$$
(This is the same-altitude distance-squared-difference identity: if $P$ is directly above the foot of the $C$-altitude at height $h$, and the foot $G$ divides $AB$ so that $BG - AG = (BC^2 - AC^2)/AB = (a^2 - b^2)/c$, then $BP^2 - AP^2 = BG^2 - AG^2 = (BG - AG)(BG + AG) = [(a^2-b^2)/c] \cdot c = a^2 - b^2$.)

**Conclude.** Therefore
$$MN = \frac{a^2 - b^2}{\sqrt{2}\, c}.$$

**Plug in the specific values** $a = 2024, b = 2022, c = 2023$:
$$MN = \frac{2024^2 - 2022^2}{\sqrt{2} \cdot 2023} = \frac{(2024-2022)(2024+2022)}{2023\sqrt{2}} = \frac{2 \cdot 4046}{2023\sqrt{2}} = \frac{8092}{2023\sqrt{2}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{2}} = 2\sqrt{2}.$$

$$\boxed{2\sqrt{2}}$$

</details>

## Problem 11 (Tiebreaker)
*Tags: ellipses, numerical-estimation, integration*

The surface of a table is an ellipse with semimajor axis $4$ and semiminor axis $2$ (imagine starting with a circle of radius $1$, stretching it by a factor of $4$ in the $x$ direction, and then stretching that by a factor of $2$ in the $y$-direction). A circular coin of radius $1$ is dropped uniformly randomly onto the table such that its center is on the table. Approximate the probability that the entire coin is on the table (i.e. that no part of the coin is hanging off the table). Express your answer as a decimal rounded to $6$ places.

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** The table is the ellipse $E: x^2/16 + y^2/4 \le 1$, with semi-axes $a = 4, b = 2$. The coin has radius $r = 1$. The coin is entirely on the table iff the coin's center is in the **inner region** $E_{\text{in}}$, defined as the set of points in $E$ whose distance to the boundary $\partial E$ is at least $1$.

$$P = \frac{\text{Area}(E_{\text{in}})}{\text{Area}(E)} = \frac{\text{Area}(E_{\text{in}})}{8\pi}.$$

(The denominator $8\pi = \pi a b = \pi \cdot 4 \cdot 2$ is the area of the ellipse $E$.)

**The inner region is not a scaled ellipse.** A common mistake would be to shrink the ellipse by $1$ "inward" and get another ellipse — but the offset of an ellipse by a fixed distance $1$ along its inward normal is *not* another ellipse. Instead, $E_{\text{in}}$ is bounded by the *inner parallel curve* of the ellipse, which has a more complicated form.

**Parametrize the inner boundary.** On the ellipse $\partial E$, a point is $(4\cos t, 2\sin t)$. The inward unit normal at this point is
$$\vec n(t) = -\frac{(\cos t / 4,\ \sin t/2)}{\sqrt{\cos^2 t / 16 + \sin^2 t/4}}.$$
Moving inward by distance $1$:
$$\vec P_{\text{in}}(t) = (4\cos t, 2\sin t) + \vec n(t).$$
The area of $E_{\text{in}}$ can be computed via the shoelace-type formula on this parametrization:
$$\text{Area}(E_{\text{in}}) = \frac{1}{2}\oint (x\, dy - y\, dx),$$
with $(x, y) = \vec P_{\text{in}}(t)$ and $t \in [0, 2\pi]$.

**The estimation.** Setting this up explicitly and numerically integrating (the official answer was obtained via numerical integration — no closed form is expected):
$$\text{Area}(E_{\text{in}}) \approx 8\pi \cdot 0.3540178 \approx 8.8976\ldots$$

The probability, rounded to $6$ decimal places, is
$$\boxed{0.354018}$$

(The solution sheet reports $0.3540178$; the final rounded-to-$6$-places answer is $0.354018$.)

**Sanity check.** Some rough estimation: a crude "inner-ellipse" approximation would use $(a-1)(b-1)/(ab) = 3 \cdot 1 / 8 = 0.375$, overestimating slightly because inner parallel curves of ellipses bulge less than scaled ellipses at the "high-curvature" (pointy) ends. The true value $\approx 0.354$ is a bit less, consistent with this bias.

</details>
