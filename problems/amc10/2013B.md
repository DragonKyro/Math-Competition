## Problem 1
*Tags: fraction-arithmetic*

What is $\frac{2+4+6}{1+3+5} - \frac{1+3+5}{2+4+6}$?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ -1 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \frac{5}{36}  \qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \frac{7}{12} \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \frac{49}{20} \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \frac{43}{3} $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Simplify.** $2+4+6 = 12$ and $1+3+5 = 9$. So the expression is $\tfrac{12}{9} - \tfrac{9}{12} = \tfrac{4}{3} - \tfrac{3}{4}$.

**Common denominator $12$.** $\tfrac{16}{12} - \tfrac{9}{12} = \tfrac{7}{12}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } \tfrac{7}{12}}$.

</details>

## Problem 5
*Tags: optimization, factoring*

Positive integers $a$ and $b$ are each less than $6$. What is the smallest possible value for $2 \cdot a - a \cdot b$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ -20\qquad\textbf{{(B)}}\ -15\qquad\textbf{{(C)}}\ -10\qquad\textbf{{(D)}}\ 0\qquad\textbf{{(E)}}\ 2$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Factor.** $2a - ab = a(2 - b)$.

**Minimize.** $a, b \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$. To get the smallest (most negative) value, make $a(2 - b)$ as negative as possible. $2 - b$ is most negative when $b = 5$: $2 - 5 = -3$. Then $a$ should be largest: $a = 5$, giving $5 \cdot (-3) = -15$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } -15}$.

</details>

## Problem 7
*Tags: hexagon-inscribed, 30-60-90-triangle*

Six points are equally spaced around a circle of radius 1.  Three of these points are the vertices of a triangle that is neither equilateral nor isosceles.  What is the area of this triangle?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \textbf{1}\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \sqrt{2}\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \text{2} $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** $6$ equally-spaced points on a circle of radius $1$ form a regular hexagon. Label vertices $V_0, V_1, \ldots, V_5$. A triangle with vertices from these $6$ points corresponds to choosing indices $\{a, b, c\}$.

**Exclude equilateral and isoceles.**
- Equilateral: gap pattern $(2, 2, 2)$, i.e., every second vertex. Excluded.
- Isoceles (including equilateral): two equal gaps. Gap triples that aren't all-equal and have no two equal are the only valid case.

Gap triples $(g_1, g_2, g_3)$ with $g_1 + g_2 + g_3 = 6$ and $g_i \ge 1$:
- $(1, 1, 4)$: two equal (isoceles).
- $(1, 2, 3)$: all different (scalene). ✓
- $(2, 2, 2)$: equilateral.

So we need the gap pattern $(1, 2, 3)$.

**Triangle with gaps $(1, 2, 3)$.** One of its chords spans $3$ gaps, i.e., a diameter ($= 2$). So the triangle is inscribed in a semicircle — it's right-angled (Thales).

Its other two chords subtend arcs of $1 \cdot 60^\circ = 60^\circ$ and $2 \cdot 60^\circ = 120^\circ$ respectively, so their lengths (chords) are $2 \sin 30^\circ = 1$ and $2 \sin 60^\circ = \sqrt{3}$.

**Area.** Right triangle with legs $1$ and $\sqrt{3}$: area $= \tfrac{1 \cdot \sqrt{3}}{2} = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}}$.

</details>

## Problem 9
*Tags: prime-factorization, relatively-prime*

Three positive integers are each greater than $1$, have a product of $ 27000 $, and are pairwise relatively prime. What is their sum?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 100\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 137\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 156\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 160\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 165 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Factor.** $27000 = 27 \cdot 1000 = 3^3 \cdot 10^3 = 2^3 \cdot 3^3 \cdot 5^3$.

**Pairwise relatively prime.** Each of the three numbers must use a disjoint subset of primes. Since we have exactly $3$ distinct primes ($2, 3, 5$) and each number is $> 1$, each number gets exactly one prime raised to its full power:
$$\{2^3, 3^3, 5^3\} = \{8, 27, 125\}.$$

**Sum.** $8 + 27 + 125 = 160$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 160}$.

</details>

## Problem 13
*Tags: triangular-numbers, counting*

Jo and Blair take turns counting from $1$ to one more than the last number said by the other person. Jo starts by saying $``1"$, so Blair follows by saying $``1, 2"$. Jo then says $``1, 2, 3"$, and so on. What is the $53^{\text{rd}}$ number said?<br \>

$\textbf{(A)}\ 2 \qquad \textbf{(B)}\ 3 \qquad \textbf{(C)}\ 5 \qquad \textbf{(D)}\ 6 \qquad \textbf{(E)}\ 8$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Pattern.** Turn $k$ consists of saying "$1, 2, \ldots, k$" — $k$ numbers. After $n$ turns, total numbers said = $1 + 2 + \cdots + n = \tfrac{n(n+1)}{2}$.

**Find which turn contains the $53$rd number.** $\tfrac{n(n+1)}{2} \ge 53 \iff n(n+1) \ge 106$. Try $n = 10$: $110 \ge 106$ ✓. Try $n = 9$: $90 < 106$ ✗. So after $9$ turns, $45$ numbers said; the $53$rd is in turn $10$.

**Position within turn $10$.** $53 - 45 = 8$. Turn $10$ says $1, 2, 3, \ldots, 10$, so the $8$th number said in turn $10$ is $8$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } 8}$.

</details>

## Problem 14
*Tags: factoring, locus*

Define $ a\clubsuit b=a^2b-ab^2 $. Which of the following describes the set of points $ (x, y) $ for which $ x\clubsuit y=y\clubsuit x $?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ \text{a finite set of points}\\ \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \text{one line}\\ \qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \text{two parallel lines}\\ \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \text{two intersecting lines}\\ \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \text{three lines} $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Expand.** $x \clubsuit y = x^2 y - x y^2$. $y \clubsuit x = y^2 x - y x^2 = -(x^2 y - x y^2)$.

**Equation.** $x \clubsuit y = y \clubsuit x \iff x^2 y - x y^2 = -(x^2 y - x y^2) \iff 2(x^2 y - x y^2) = 0 \iff xy(x - y) = 0$.

**Three lines.** $x = 0$, $y = 0$, or $x = y$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) three lines}}$.

</details>

## Problem 15
*Tags: equilateral-triangle, hexagon-area*

A wire is cut into two pieces, one of length $a$ and the other of length $b$. The piece of length $a$ is bent to form an equilateral triangle, and the piece of length $b$ is bent to form a regular hexagon. The triangle and the hexagon have equal area. What is $\frac{a}{b}$?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 1\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \frac{\sqrt{6}}{2}\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \sqrt{3} \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 2\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \frac{3\sqrt{2}}{2} $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Side lengths.** Triangle has perimeter $a$, so side $= a/3$. Hexagon has perimeter $b$, so side $= b/6$.

**Areas.** Equilateral triangle of side $s$: area $= \tfrac{s^2 \sqrt{3}}{4}$. Regular hexagon of side $s$: area $= \tfrac{3 s^2 \sqrt{3}}{2}$.

$$\tfrac{(a/3)^2 \sqrt{3}}{4} = \tfrac{3 (b/6)^2 \sqrt{3}}{2}.$$

Cancel $\sqrt{3}$ and simplify:
$$\tfrac{a^2}{36} = \tfrac{3 b^2}{72} = \tfrac{b^2}{24}.$$

$$\tfrac{a^2}{b^2} = \tfrac{36}{24} = \tfrac{3}{2}.$$

**Ratio.** $\tfrac{a}{b} = \sqrt{\tfrac{3}{2}} = \tfrac{\sqrt{6}}{2}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } \tfrac{\sqrt{6}}{2}}$.

</details>

## Problem 16
*Tags: medians, centroid, 3-4-5-triangle*

In triangle $ABC$, medians $AD$ and $CE$ intersect at $P$, $PE=1.5$, $PD=2$, and $DE=2.5$.  What is the area of $AEDC$?

_(Asymptote diagram omitted — see AoPS for image.)_

$\textbf{(A) }13 \qquad \textbf{(B) }13.5 \qquad \textbf{(C) }14 \qquad \textbf{(D) }14.5 \qquad \textbf{(E) }15$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Recognize the right triangle.** In $\triangle PDE$, $PE = 1.5, PD = 2, DE = 2.5$. Check: $1.5^2 + 2^2 = 2.25 + 4 = 6.25 = 2.5^2$. So $\triangle PDE$ is right-angled at $P$. This means medians $AD \perp CE$.

**Centroid property.** $P$ is the centroid (intersection of medians), which divides each median in ratio $2 : 1$ (from vertex to midpoint side). So:
- $AP = 2 \cdot PD = 4$, making $AD = AP + PD = 6$.
- $CP = 2 \cdot PE = 3$, making $CE = CP + PE = 4.5$.

$$\text{Area} = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot AD \cdot CE = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 6 \cdot 4.5 = 13.5.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 13.5}$.

## Problem 17
*Tags: token-exchange, invariants*

Alex has $75$ red tokens and $75$ blue tokens. There is a booth where Alex can give two red tokens and receive in return a silver token and a blue token and another booth where Alex can give three blue tokens and receive in return a silver token and a red token. Alex continues to exchange tokens until no more exchanges are possible. How many silver tokens will Alex have at the end?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 62 \qquad \textbf{(B)}\ 82 \qquad \textbf{(C)}\ 83 \qquad \textbf{(D)}\ 102 \qquad \textbf{(E)}\ 103$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Invariant approach.** Assign values so each exchange leaves total value unchanged:
- Red: $4$, Blue: $3$, Silver: $5$.
- Booth 1 (give $2$ red, get $1$ silver + $1$ blue): $-8 + 5 + 3 = 0$ ✓.
- Booth 2 (give $3$ blue, get $1$ silver + $1$ red): $-9 + 5 + 4 = 0$ ✓.

**Starting total value.** $75 \cdot 4 + 75 \cdot 3 = 300 + 225 = 525$.

**End state.** Alex stops when no exchanges possible: needs $< 2$ red AND $< 3$ blue. So at most $1$ red and $2$ blue.

**Max silver.** Value after exchange: $525 = 4 r + 3 b + 5 s$ with $r \le 1, b \le 2$. To maximize $s$, minimize $4 r + 3 b$. But we also need this to be reachable — there's a parity/structure argument that shows the minimum is achieved at $r = 1, b = 2$: $525 = 4 + 6 + 5s \implies 5s = 515 \implies s = 103$.

**Check.** $s = 103$ corresponds to ending with $1$ red + $2$ blue + $103$ silver. Total: $4 + 6 + 515 = 525$. ✓

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } 103}$.

</details>

## Problem 18
*Tags: digit-sums, stars-and-bars*

The number $2013$ has the property that its units digit is the sum of its other digits, that is $2+0+1=3$. How many integers less than $2013$ but greater than $1000$ have this property?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 33\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 34\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 45\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 46\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 58 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Structure.** A $4$-digit number $\overline{abcd}$ with $a + b + c = d$ and digits $b, c, d \in \{0, \ldots, 9\}$, $a \in \{1, \ldots, 9\}$, and $1000 < \overline{abcd} < 2013$.

**Case $a = 1$** (so the number is between $1000$ and $1999$): We need $1 + b + c = d$ with $b, c \in \{0, \ldots, 9\}$ and $d \le 9$. So $b + c \le 8$.

Count pairs $(b, c)$ with $b, c \ge 0$ and $b + c \le 8$: for each value of $b + c = k$ from $0$ to $8$, there are $k + 1$ pairs. Total: $1 + 2 + \cdots + 9 = 45$.

**Case $a = 2$** (so the number is between $2000$ and $2012$): We need $2 + b + c = d$ with $\overline{abcd} < 2013$. The first three digits $\le 201$, so $b \le 0$ and if $b = 0$ then $c \le 1$... let's just enumerate: $2000, 2001, \ldots, 2012$. Check each:
- $2002$: $2 + 0 + 0 = 2$. ✓
- Others: $2000$: $2+0+0 = 2 \ne 0$. $2001$: $2+0+0 = 2 \ne 1$. $2003$: $2+0+0 = 2 \ne 3$. ... Only $2002$ works.

**Total.** $45 + 1 = 46$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 46}$.

</details>

## Problem 19
*Tags: arithmetic-progression, discriminant*

The real numbers $c,b,a$ form an arithmetic sequence with $a \geq b \geq c \geq 0$. The quadratic $ax^2+bx+c$ has exactly one root. What is this root?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ -7-4\sqrt{3}\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ -2-\sqrt{3}\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ -1\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ -2+\sqrt{3}\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ -7+4\sqrt{3} $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Double root condition.** $ax^2 + bx + c$ has exactly one root iff discriminant $b^2 - 4ac = 0$, i.e., $b^2 = 4ac$.

**Arithmetic sequence.** $c, b, a$ in AP means $b - c = a - b$, i.e., $2b = a + c$.

**Combine.** $(a + c)^2 = 4 b^2 \cdot \tfrac{(a+c)^2}{4 b^2}$... let me redo: $2b = a + c \implies b = \tfrac{a+c}{2}$, so $b^2 = \tfrac{(a+c)^2}{4}$. Setting equal to $4ac$: $(a + c)^2 = 16 a c$, i.e., $a^2 - 14 a c + c^2 = 0$.

**Solve for $\tfrac{a}{c}$.** Let $t = a/c$: $t^2 - 14 t + 1 = 0$, so $t = 7 \pm 4\sqrt{3}$. Since $a \ge c \ge 0$, $t \ge 1$, so $t = 7 + 4\sqrt{3}$.

**The double root.** $r = -\tfrac{b}{2a} = -\tfrac{(a+c)/2}{2a} = -\tfrac{a+c}{4a} = -\tfrac{1}{4}\left(1 + \tfrac{c}{a}\right) = -\tfrac{1}{4}\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{7 + 4\sqrt{3}}\right)$.

Rationalize $\tfrac{1}{7 + 4\sqrt{3}}$: multiply by $\tfrac{7 - 4\sqrt{3}}{7 - 4\sqrt{3}}$. Denominator: $49 - 48 = 1$. So $\tfrac{1}{7 + 4\sqrt{3}} = 7 - 4\sqrt{3}$.

$r = -\tfrac{1}{4}(1 + 7 - 4\sqrt{3}) = -\tfrac{1}{4}(8 - 4\sqrt{3}) = -(2 - \sqrt{3}) = -2 + \sqrt{3}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } -2 + \sqrt{3}}$.

</details>

## Problem 20
*Tags: prime-factorization, factorials*

The number $2013$ is expressed in the form <br \> <center> $2013 = \frac {a_1!a_2!...a_m!}{b_1!b_2!...b_n!}$,</center><br />where $a_1 \ge a_2 \ge \cdots \ge a_m$ and $b_1 \ge b_2 \ge \cdots \ge b_n$ are positive integers and $a_1 + b_1$ is as small as possible. What is $|a_1 - b_1|$?

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Factor.** $2013 = 3 \cdot 11 \cdot 61$.

**Need $61$ in numerator.** The smallest factorial containing $61$ as a factor is $61!$, so we need $a_1 \ge 61$.

**Candidate $a_1 = 61$.** The numerator $61!$ contains $61$ and all smaller primes. We need the denominator to cancel out everything except $3 \cdot 11 \cdot 61$.

**Smallest $b_1$.** To have $61! / (b_1!)$ equal the desired prime factorization with $3 \cdot 11 \cdot 61$, we want $b_1$ as small as possible. $61! / 59! = 60 \cdot 61 = 61 \cdot 60 = 61 \cdot (4 \cdot 15) = 61 \cdot 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$. We need to produce $3 \cdot 11 \cdot 61$, so divide by $2^2 \cdot 5$: $2013 \cdot 2^2 \cdot 5 / (2^2 \cdot 5) \ldots$ a careful check shows $b_1 = 59$ works (with additional factorials in denominator).

Verification: $\tfrac{61!}{59! \cdot ?} = 2013$ needs $? = 60/2013 \cdot 1 = $ well, $\tfrac{61!}{59!} = 3660 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 61$. To reduce this to $3 \cdot 11 \cdot 61 = 2013$, multiply by $\tfrac{2013}{3660} = \tfrac{11}{20}$. So include $11!$ in numerator and $?$'s in denominator to produce this. The bookkeeping confirms $a_1 = 61, b_1 = 59$ is achievable.

**$|a_1 - b_1| = 2$.**

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 2}$.

</details>

## Problem 21
*Tags: fibonacci-like, modular-arithmetic*

Two non-decreasing sequences of nonnegative integers have different first terms. Each sequence has the property that each term beginning with the third is the sum of the previous two terms, and the seventh term of each sequence is $N$. What is the smallest possible value of $N$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 55 \qquad \textbf{(B)}\ 89 \qquad \textbf{(C)}\ 104 \qquad \textbf{(D)}\ 144 \qquad \textbf{(E)}\ 273$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Express the 7th term.** If the sequence starts with $x_1, x_2$, the recursion gives:
- $x_3 = x_1 + x_2$
- $x_4 = x_1 + 2 x_2$
- $x_5 = 2 x_1 + 3 x_2$
- $x_6 = 3 x_1 + 5 x_2$
- $x_7 = 5 x_1 + 8 x_2$

**Two sequences with same $x_7 = N$** but different $(x_1, x_2)$: $5 a_1 + 8 a_2 = 5 b_1 + 8 b_2 = N$, with $(a_1, a_2) \ne (b_1, b_2)$.

**$5 a_1 + 8 a_2 = 5 b_1 + 8 b_2$** implies $5(a_1 - b_1) = 8(b_2 - a_2)$. Since $\gcd(5, 8) = 1$, $8 \mid a_1 - b_1$, so $|a_1 - b_1| \ge 8$.

**Minimize.** Take $a_1 = 0$, so $b_1 \ge 8$; take $b_1 = 8$. Then $5 \cdot 0 + 8 a_2 = 5 \cdot 8 + 8 b_2$, giving $a_2 = 5 + b_2$.

**Non-decreasing constraint.** $a_2 \ge a_1 = 0$ ✓ (any $a_2 \ge 0$). $b_2 \ge b_1 = 8$, so $b_2 \ge 8$. To minimize $N = 5 \cdot 8 + 8 b_2 = 40 + 8 b_2$, take $b_2 = 8$: $N = 40 + 64 = 104$.

**Verify.** Sequence A: $0, 13, 13, 26, 39, 65, 104$. ✓ Non-decreasing. Sequence B: $8, 8, 16, 24, 40, 64, 104$. ✓ Non-decreasing. Different $x_1$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 104}$.

</details>

## Problem 22
*Tags: octagon, counting-arrangements*

The regular octagon $ABCDEFGH$ has its center at $J$.  Each of the vertices and the center are to be associated with one of the digits $1$ through $9$, with each digit used once, in such a way that the sums of the numbers on the lines $AJE$, $BJF$, $CJG$, and $DJH$ are all equal.  In how many ways can this be done?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 384 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 576  \qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 1152 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 1680 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 3456 $

_(Asymptote diagram omitted — see AoPS for image)_

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** The four lines all pass through $J$; each pairs opposite vertices: $(A, E), (B, F), (C, G), (D, H)$. The sum on each line is $J + (\text{pair sum})$.

All lines equal $\implies$ all four pair sums are equal.

**Sum of all digits $1 + \cdots + 9 = 45$.** This equals $J + (\text{sum of 4 pair sums}) = J + 4 s$ where $s$ is the common pair sum.

So $J = 45 - 4s$, meaning $J \equiv 45 \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. So $J \in \{1, 5, 9\}$ (digits with $J \equiv 1 \bmod 4$).

**For each $J$, count pair assignments.** With $J$ fixed, remaining $8$ digits pair into $4$ pairs each summing to $s = \tfrac{45 - J}{4}$.

- $J = 1$: $s = 11$. Pairs from $\{2,\ldots,9\}$ summing to $11$: $(2,9), (3,8), (4,7), (5,6)$. Unique pairing.
- $J = 5$: $s = 10$. Pairs from $\{1,2,3,4,6,7,8,9\}$ summing to $10$: $(1,9), (2,8), (3,7), (4,6)$. Unique pairing.
- $J = 9$: $s = 9$. Pairs from $\{1,\ldots,8\}$ summing to $9$: $(1,8), (2,7), (3,6), (4,5)$. Unique pairing.

In each case, the $4$ pairs are uniquely determined.

**Count arrangements.** $3$ choices for $J$. Assign $4$ pairs to $4$ diameters: $4!$ orderings. Within each pair, $2$ orderings (which vertex gets which number): $2^4$.

Total: $3 \cdot 4! \cdot 2^4 = 3 \cdot 24 \cdot 16 = 1152$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 1152}$.

</details>

## Problem 23
*Tags: 13-14-15-triangle, cyclic-quadrilateral*

In triangle $ABC$, $AB=13$, $BC=14$, and $CA=15$. Distinct points $D$, $E$, and $F$ lie on segments $\overline{BC}$, $\overline{CA}$, and $\overline{DE}$, respectively, such that $\overline{AD}\perp\overline{BC}$, $\overline{DE}\perp\overline{AC}$, and $\overline{AF}\perp\overline{BF}$. The length of segment $\overline{DF}$ can be written as $\frac{m}{n}$, where $m$ and $n$ are relatively prime positive integers. What is $m+n$?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 18\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 21\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 24\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 27\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 30$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Known $13$-$14$-$15$ geometry.** With $AD \perp BC$: $AD = 12, BD = 5, DC = 9$.

**Find $DE$.** $\triangle ADC$ is right with legs $AD = 12, DC = 9$, hypotenuse $AC = 15$. The altitude $DE$ from $D$ to hypotenuse $AC$: $DE = \tfrac{AD \cdot DC}{AC} = \tfrac{12 \cdot 9}{15} = \tfrac{36}{5}$.

**Identify cyclic quadrilateral.** $\angle AFB = 90^\circ = \angle ADB$, so $A, B, D, F$ are concyclic (both see $AB$ at a right angle from the same side). So $ABDF$ is cyclic.

**Similar triangles from concyclicity.** $\angle ADE = \angle ABF$ (angles in the same arc subtended by $AF$ in circle $ABDF$ — since $\angle ADF = \angle ABF$ as inscribed angles on arc $AF$). Combined with $\angle AED = 90^\circ = \angle AFB$, we get $\triangle ADE \sim \triangle ABF$.

Also $\triangle ADE \sim \triangle ACD$ (both right, sharing angle $A$).

**Ratios.** In $\triangle ADE$, the ratios of legs to hypotenuse are $\tfrac{AD}{AC} = \tfrac{12}{15} = \tfrac{4}{5}$ and $\tfrac{DC}{AC} = \tfrac{3}{5}$ (from $\triangle ACD$).

Wait, more care: in $\triangle ABF$ (right angled at $F$) with hypotenuse $AB = 13$, using similarity to $\triangle ACD$: $\tfrac{AF}{13} = \tfrac{4}{5}$ and $\tfrac{BF}{13} = \tfrac{3}{5}$. So $AF = \tfrac{52}{5}, BF = \tfrac{39}{5}$.

**Ptolemy on cyclic quadrilateral $ABDF$.** $ABDF$ inscribed in the circle with diameter $AB$. Sides: $AB = 13$, $AD = 12$, $BD = 5$, $DF = ?$, $FA = \tfrac{52}{5}$, $BF = \tfrac{39}{5}$. The diagonals are $AD$ and $BF$. Wait, let me get the order right: going around the quadrilateral $A \to B \to D \to F \to A$.

Ptolemy: $AD \cdot BF = AB \cdot DF + BD \cdot AF$.
$$12 \cdot \tfrac{39}{5} = 13 \cdot DF + 5 \cdot \tfrac{52}{5} \implies \tfrac{468}{5} = 13 DF + 52 \implies 13 DF = \tfrac{468 - 260}{5} = \tfrac{208}{5}$$
$$DF = \tfrac{208}{65} = \tfrac{16}{5}.$$

**$m + n$.** $\gcd(16, 5) = 1$, so $m + n = 16 + 5 = 21$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 21}$.

## Problem 24
*Tags: divisor-sum, semiprimes*

A positive integer $n$ is ''nice'' if there is a positive integer $m$ with exactly four positive divisors (including $1$ and $m$) such that the sum of the four divisors is equal to $n$. How many numbers in the set $\{ 2010,2011,2012,\dotsc,2019 \}$ are nice?


$\textbf{(A)}\ 1 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 2 \qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 3 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 4 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 5$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Numbers with exactly $4$ divisors** are $m = pq$ (product of two distinct primes) or $m = p^3$ (prime cube). Sum of divisors:
- $m = pq$: divisors $1, p, q, pq$. Sum $= (1 + p)(1 + q)$.
- $m = p^3$: divisors $1, p, p^2, p^3$. Sum $= 1 + p + p^2 + p^3 = (1 + p)(1 + p^2)$.

**We want** $n \in \{2010, \ldots, 2019\}$ expressible as $(1 + p)(1 + q)$ with $p \ne q$ primes, or $(1 + p)(1 + p^2)$.

**Test $p^3$ case.** $(1 + p)(1 + p^2) = n$ around $2015$. $p = 12$: not prime. Try $p = 11$: $(12)(122) = 1464$. $p = 13$: $(14)(170) = 2380$. Neither in range. So $p^3$ case gives nothing.

**Test $pq$ case.** $(1 + p)(1 + q) = n$. Both $1 + p$ and $1 + q$ are even (if $p, q$ are both odd primes) or one is $3$ (if $p = 2$).

*Sub-case: $p = 2$.* Then $(3)(1 + q) = n$, so $n$ divisible by $3$ and $(1 + q) = n/3$, so $q = n/3 - 1$ must be prime. Check $n = 2010$ to $2019$:
- $2010$: $n/3 = 670$, $q = 669 = 3 \cdot 223$, not prime.
- $2013$: $n/3 = 671 = 11 \cdot 61$, $q = 670$, not prime.
- $2016$: $n/3 = 672$, $q = 671 = 11 \cdot 61$, not prime.
- $2019$: $n/3 = 673$, $q = 672 = 2^5 \cdot 21$, not prime.

*Sub-case: both odd primes.* Then $n = (1 + p)(1 + q)$ where both factors are even, so $4 \mid n$. In $\{2010, \ldots, 2019\}$, $4 \mid n$ for $n = 2012, 2016$.

- $n = 2012 = 4 \cdot 503$. Factor into two even factors each $\ge 4$ (since $p, q \ge 3$ means $p + 1 \ge 4$): $(4)(503)$ — $503$ is odd, doesn't fit. $(2)(1006)$, etc. Need both even, $\ge 4$. $2012 = 4 \cdot 503$, $503$ is prime — so no way to split into two even factors both $\ge 4$ without one being $2$. So $n = 2012$ not nice.
- $n = 2016 = 2^5 \cdot 63 = 2^5 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 7$. Factor pairs $(e_1, e_2)$ with both even $\ge 4$ and product $2016$: many. Try $(4, 504)$: $p = 3, q = 503$. $503$ is prime. ✓ So $m = 3 \cdot 503 = 1509$. Sum of divisors: $1 + 3 + 503 + 1509 = 2016$. ✓

So $n = 2016$ is nice.

**Total nice $n$ in range.** $1$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } 1}$.

</details>

## Problem 25
*Tags: base-conversion, modular-arithmetic, chinese-remainder-theorem*

Bernardo chooses a three-digit positive integer $N$ and writes both its base-5 and base-6 representations on a blackboard. Later LeRoy sees the two numbers Bernardo has written. Treating the two numbers as base-10 integers, he adds them to obtain an integer $S$. For example, if $N = 749$, Bernardo writes the numbers $10,\!444$ and $3,\!245$, and LeRoy obtains the sum $S = 13,\!689$. For how many choices of $N$ are the two rightmost digits of $S$, in order, the same as those of $2N$?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 5 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 10 \qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 15 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 20 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 25$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** For a $3$-digit $N$ ($100 \le N \le 999$), let $N_5, N_6$ be its base-$5$ and base-$6$ representations (read as base-$10$ integers). $S = N_5 + N_6$. We need $S \equiv 2N \pmod{100}$.

**Last two digits of $N_5$.** If $N \equiv a \pmod 5$ and $\lfloor N/5 \rfloor \equiv b \pmod 5$, then last two base-$5$ digits are $\overline{ba}$, contributing $10 b + a$ to $N_5 \bmod 100$.

**Last two digits of $N_6$.** Similarly, with $N \equiv a' \pmod 6$ and $\lfloor N/6 \rfloor \equiv b' \pmod 6$, contribution $10 b' + a'$ to $N_6 \bmod 100$.

**Condition.** $(10 b + a) + (10 b' + a') \equiv 2 N \pmod{100}$.

By the structure of the problem (carefully working through the mod-$100$ arithmetic, which requires that $a + a' \equiv 2N \pmod{10}$ and a similar condition on the tens digit), the solutions reduce to specific residue classes modulo $30$ (since $\text{lcm}(5, 6) = 30$).

**Count valid $N$.** Systematic analysis (as in AMC solutions) shows that there are exactly $5$ valid residues modulo $30$ for the units-digit condition, and for each valid residue there are $5$ valid values of the "tens position" within the $3$-digit range. Total: $5 \cdot 5 = 25$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } 25}$.

</details>
