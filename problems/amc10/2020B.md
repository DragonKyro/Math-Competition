## Problem 1
*Tags: arithmetic-evaluation*

What is the value of
$$1-(-2)-3-(-4)-5-(-6)?$$

$\textbf{(A)}\ -20 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ -3 \qquad\textbf{(C)}\  3 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 5 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 21$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Simplify double negatives.** $a - (-b) = a + b$, so
$$1 - (-2) - 3 - (-4) - 5 - (-6) = 1 + 2 - 3 + 4 - 5 + 6.$$

**Combine.** $1 + 2 - 3 + 4 - 5 + 6 = (1 + 2) + (-3 + 4) + (-5 + 6) = 3 + 1 + 1 = 5$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 5}$.

</details>

## Problem 2

Carl has $5$ cubes each having side length $1$, and Kate has $5$ cubes each having side length $2$. What is the total volume of these $10$ cubes?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 24 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 25 \qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 28 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 40 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 45$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Volume of a cube** = side$^3$.

- Carl's $5$ cubes (side $1$): $5 \cdot 1^3 = 5$.
- Kate's $5$ cubes (side $2$): $5 \cdot 2^3 = 5 \cdot 8 = 40$.

**Total.** $5 + 40 = 45$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } 45}$.

</details>

## Problem 3
*Tags: ratio-chain*

The ratio of $w$ to $x$ is $4:3$, the ratio of $y$ to $z$ is $3:2$, and the ratio of $z$ to $x$ is $1:6$. What is the ratio of $w$ to $y?$

$\textbf{(A)}\ 4:3 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 3:2 \qquad\textbf{(C)}\  8:3 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 4:1 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 16:3$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Chain the ratios.** Write each as a fraction:
$$\frac{w}{x} = \frac{4}{3}, \qquad \frac{y}{z} = \frac{3}{2}, \qquad \frac{z}{x} = \frac{1}{6}.$$

**Express $\dfrac{w}{y}$ via the chain.**
$$\frac{w}{y} = \frac{w}{x} \cdot \frac{x}{z} \cdot \frac{z}{y} = \frac{4}{3} \cdot \frac{6}{1} \cdot \frac{2}{3} = \frac{4 \cdot 6 \cdot 2}{3 \cdot 1 \cdot 3} = \frac{48}{9} = \frac{16}{3}.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } 16:3}$.

</details>

## Problem 4
*Tags: complementary-angles, prime-numbers*

The acute angles of a right triangle are $a^{\circ}$ and $b^{\circ}$, where $a>b$ and both $a$ and $b$ are prime numbers. What is the least possible value of $b$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 2 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 3 \qquad\textbf{(C)}\  5 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 7 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 11$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Key equation.** In a right triangle, the two acute angles sum to $90°$, so $a + b = 90$ with $a > b$ and both prime.

**Minimize $b$.** Try each prime in increasing order and check if $90 - b$ is also prime:
- $b = 2$: $a = 88 = 2^3 \cdot 11$, not prime. ✗
- $b = 3$: $a = 87 = 3 \cdot 29$, not prime. ✗
- $b = 5$: $a = 85 = 5 \cdot 17$, not prime. ✗
- $b = 7$: $a = 83$, which is prime ✓ (check: $83$ not divisible by any prime $\leq \sqrt{83} \approx 9.1$; not by $2, 3, 5, 7$). Also $a > b$ ✓.

So the least valid $b$ is $7$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 7}$.

</details>

## Problem 5
*Tags: multinomial-coefficient, permutations*

How many distinguishable arrangements are there of $1$ brown tile, $1$ purple tile, $2$ green tiles, and $3$ yellow tiles in a row from left to right? (Tiles of the same color are indistinguishable.)

$\textbf{(A)}\ 210 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 420 \qquad\textbf{(C)}\  630 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 840 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 1050$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Use the multinomial coefficient.** With $7$ tiles total and repeated colors of counts $3$ (yellow), $2$ (green), $1$ (brown), $1$ (purple):
$$\frac{7!}{3! \cdot 2! \cdot 1! \cdot 1!} = \frac{5040}{6 \cdot 2} = \frac{5040}{12} = 420.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 420}$.

</details>

## Problem 6
*Tags: palindrome, next-palindrome*

Driving along a highway, Megan noticed that her odometer showed $15951$ (miles). This number is a palindrome-it reads the same forward and backward. Then $2$ hours later, the odometer displayed the next higher palindrome. What was her average speed, in miles per hour, during this $2$-hour period?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 50 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 55 \qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 60 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 65 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 70$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Find the next palindrome after $15951$.**

A $5$-digit palindrome has the form $\overline{abcba}$ (first and last digits equal, second and fourth equal). For $15951$: $a = 1, b = 5, c = 9$.

To make the smallest palindrome greater than $15951$, try incrementing the middle digit $c$: but $c = 9$ already, so we'd have to roll over, which forces the outer digits to change.

Next possibility: increment $b$ (the second digit). New $b = 6$, then $c$ can be $0$, giving palindrome $16061$.

Is $16061$ really the next? Check: any palindrome $\overline{a'b'c'b'a'}$ with $a' = 1$ and $b' = 5$ must have $c' \geq 10$ to exceed $15951$ — impossible. So we need $b' \geq 6$ or $a' \geq 2$. Smallest is $b' = 6, c' = 0$: $16061$ ✓.

**Distance and speed.** Distance $= 16061 - 15951 = 110$ miles, over $2$ hours.
$$\text{speed} = \frac{110}{2} = 55 \text{ mph}.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 55}$.

</details>

## Problem 7
*Tags: perfect-squares, divisibility*

How many positive even multiples of $3$ less than $2020$ are perfect squares?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 7 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 8 \qquad\textbf{(C)}\  9 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 10 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 12$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**An even multiple of $3$ is a multiple of $6$.** So we need multiples of $6$ that are perfect squares and $< 2020$.

**Form of a perfect square multiple of $6$.** If $n^2$ is divisible by $6 = 2 \cdot 3$, then $n$ must be divisible by both $2$ and $3$ (since primes dividing $n^2$ divide $n$). So $n = 6k$ for some positive integer $k$, and $n^2 = 36 k^2$.

**Bound.** $36 k^2 < 2020 \Rightarrow k^2 < \tfrac{2020}{36} \approx 56.1 \Rightarrow k \leq 7$ (since $7^2 = 49 < 56.1 < 64 = 8^2$).

So $k = 1, 2, \ldots, 7$, giving $7$ values.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } 7}$.

</details>

## Problem 8
*Tags: right-triangle-locus, thales-theorem*

Points $P$ and $Q$ lie in a plane with $PQ=8$. How many locations for point $R$ in this plane are there such that the triangle with vertices $P$, $Q$, and $R$ is a right triangle with area $12$ square units?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 2 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 4 \qquad\textbf{(C)}\  6 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 8 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 12$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Set up coordinates.** Place $P = (-4, 0)$, $Q = (4, 0)$ (so $PQ = 8$). Let $R = (x, y)$.

**Area condition.** Base $PQ = 8$, so area $= \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 8 \cdot |y| = 4|y| = 12$, giving $|y| = 3$. So $y = \pm 3$.

**Casework on which vertex is the right angle.**

**Case A: right angle at $P$.** Then $PR \perp PQ$. Since $PQ$ is horizontal, $PR$ must be vertical, so $R$ has the same $x$-coordinate as $P$: $x = -4$. Combined with $y = \pm 3$: $2$ points.

**Case B: right angle at $Q$.** Similarly, $x = 4$, $y = \pm 3$: $2$ points.

**Case C: right angle at $R$.** By Thales' theorem, $R$ lies on the circle with diameter $\overline{PQ}$ (radius $4$, centered at origin): $x^2 + y^2 = 16$. Combined with $|y| = 3$: $x^2 = 16 - 9 = 7$, so $x = \pm\sqrt{7}$. Combined with $y = \pm 3$: $2 \cdot 2 = 4$ points.

**Total.** $2 + 2 + 4 = 8$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 8}$.

</details>

## Problem 9
*Tags: completing-the-square, integer-solutions*

How many ordered pairs of integers $(x, y)$ satisfy the equation $$x^{2020}+y^2=2y?$$

$\textbf{(A) } 1 \qquad\textbf{(B) } 2 \qquad\textbf{(C) } 3 \qquad\textbf{(D) } 4 \qquad\textbf{(E) } \text{infinitely many}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Complete the square in $y$.** Rearrange:
$$x^{2020} + y^2 - 2y = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^{2020} + (y - 1)^2 = 1.$$

**Bound $x$.** $x^{2020} \geq 0$ (even exponent) and $(y - 1)^2 \geq 0$. Their sum is $1$, so each term is in $[0, 1]$.

For $x$ an integer, $x^{2020}$ is either $0$ (when $x = 0$) or at least $1$ (when $|x| \geq 1$, where $x^{2020} = |x|^{2020} \geq 1$, with equality when $|x| = 1$).

**Case A: $x = 0$.** Then $(y - 1)^2 = 1$, so $y - 1 = \pm 1$, giving $y = 0$ or $y = 2$. Two solutions: $(0, 0), (0, 2)$.

**Case B: $x = \pm 1$.** Then $x^{2020} = 1$, so $(y - 1)^2 = 0$, giving $y = 1$. Two solutions: $(1, 1), (-1, 1)$.

**Case C: $|x| \geq 2$.** $x^{2020} \geq 2^{2020} \gg 1$, so $(y-1)^2 < 0$, impossible.

**Total.** $2 + 2 = 4$ ordered pairs.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 4}$.

</details>

## Problem 10
*Tags: cone-from-sector, pythagorean-theorem*

A three-quarter sector of a circle of radius $4$ inches together with its interior can be rolled up to form the lateral surface area of a right circular cone by taping together along the two radii shown. What is the volume of the cone in cubic inches?

_(Asymptote diagram omitted — see AoPS for image)_

$\textbf{(A)}\ 3\pi \sqrt5 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 4\pi \sqrt3 \qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 3 \pi \sqrt7 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 6\pi \sqrt3 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 6\pi \sqrt7$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Match the sector arc to the cone's base circumference.** When rolled, the two straight radii of the sector glue together to form the cone's slant edge. So:

- The sector's radius ($4$) becomes the cone's slant height: $\ell = 4$.
- The sector's arc length becomes the cone's base circumference.

**Arc length of a $\tfrac{3}{4}$-sector of radius $4$:** $\tfrac{3}{4} \cdot 2\pi \cdot 4 = 6\pi$.

**Cone base circumference $= 2 \pi r = 6\pi$**, so $r = 3$.

**Find the cone's height via Pythagoras.** With slant $\ell = 4$ and base radius $r = 3$:
$$h = \sqrt{\ell^2 - r^2} = \sqrt{16 - 9} = \sqrt{7}.$$

**Volume.**
$$V = \tfrac{1}{3} \pi r^2 h = \tfrac{1}{3} \pi (3)^2 \sqrt{7} = 3\pi \sqrt{7}.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 3\pi\sqrt{7}}$.

</details>

## Problem 11
*Tags: hypergeometric, counting-overlaps*

Ms. Carr asks her students to read any $5$ of the $10$ books on a reading list. Harold randomly selects $5$ books from this list, and Betty does the same. What is the probability that there are exactly $2$ books that they both select?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \frac{1}{8} \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \frac{5}{36} \qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \frac{14}{45} \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \frac{25}{63} \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \frac{1}{2}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Fix Harold's choice.** By symmetry, we can condition on any specific set of $5$ books for Harold; the answer doesn't depend on which $5$. Call Harold's set $H$ (the $5$ books he picked); the remaining $5$ books are $H^c$.

**Betty picks $5$ books uniformly at random from all $10$.** We want $P(|B \cap H| = 2)$.

**Count favorable vs. total.**
- Total: $\binom{10}{5} = 252$.
- Favorable: Betty picks exactly $2$ from $H$ and exactly $3$ from $H^c$: $\binom{5}{2} \binom{5}{3} = 10 \cdot 10 = 100$.

**Probability.**
$$\frac{100}{252} = \frac{25}{63}.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } \tfrac{25}{63}}$.

</details>

## Problem 12
*Tags: decimal-places, logarithms*

The decimal representation of $$\dfrac{1}{20^{20}}$$ consists of a string of zeros after the decimal point, followed by a $9$ and then several more digits. How many zeros are in that initial string of zeros after the decimal point?

$\textbf{(A)} \text{ 23} \qquad \textbf{(B)} \text{ 24} \qquad \textbf{(C)} \text{ 25} \qquad \textbf{(D)} \text{ 26} \qquad \textbf{(E)} \text{ 27}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Rewrite with a power-of-ten denominator.**
$$\frac{1}{20^{20}} = \frac{1}{2^{20} \cdot 10^{20}} = \frac{5^{20}}{10^{40}}.$$

When $\tfrac{N}{10^{40}}$ is written as a decimal (for integer $N$), it has $40 - (\text{# digits of } N)$ zeros before the first nonzero digit (and then the digits of $N$).

**Number of digits of $5^{20}$.**
$$\log_{10}(5^{20}) = 20 \log_{10} 5 = 20 (1 - \log_{10} 2) \approx 20(1 - 0.30103) = 20 \cdot 0.69897 \approx 13.9794.$$

So $5^{20}$ has $\lfloor 13.9794 \rfloor + 1 = 14$ digits.

**Number of zeros.** $40 - 14 = 26$ zeros before the first nonzero digit.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 26}$.

</details>

## Problem 13
*Tags: spiral-walk, arithmetic-series*

Andy the Ant lives on a coordinate plane and is currently at $(-20, 20)$ facing east (that is, in the positive $x$-direction). Andy moves $1$ unit and then turns $90^{\circ}$ left. From there, Andy moves $2$ units (north) and then turns $90^{\circ}$ left. He then moves $3$ units (west) and again turns $90^{\circ}$ left. Andy continues his progress, increasing his distance each time by $1$ unit and always turning left. What is the location of the point at which Andy makes the $2020$th left turn?

$\textbf{(A)}\ (-1030, -994)\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ (-1030, -990)\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ (-1026, -994)\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ (-1026, -990)\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ (-1022, -994)$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Direction pattern.** Andy turns left each step, so directions cycle: East → North → West → South → East → ⋯. Move $n$ has distance $n$ in this cyclic direction.

- Move $1, 5, 9, \ldots, 4k+1$: East ($+x$).
- Move $2, 6, 10, \ldots, 4k+2$: North ($+y$).
- Move $3, 7, 11, \ldots, 4k+3$: West ($-x$).
- Move $4, 8, 12, \ldots, 4k+4$: South ($-y$).

**Compute final position after $2020$ moves** (start $(-20, 20)$).

**$x$-coordinate.** Changes come from East (moves $1, 5, \ldots, 2017$, adding) and West (moves $3, 7, \ldots, 2019$, subtracting):
$$\Delta x = (1 + 5 + 9 + \cdots + 2017) - (3 + 7 + 11 + \cdots + 2019).$$

Pair term by term: $(1 - 3) + (5 - 7) + (9 - 11) + \cdots + (2017 - 2019) = -2 \cdot 505 = -1010$.

(There are $505$ East moves and $505$ West moves among the first $2020$ moves.)

So final $x = -20 + (-1010) = -1030$.

**$y$-coordinate.** North (moves $2, 6, \ldots, 2018$) minus South (moves $4, 8, \ldots, 2020$):
$$\Delta y = (2 + 6 + \cdots + 2018) - (4 + 8 + \cdots + 2020).$$

Pair: $(2 - 4) + (6 - 8) + \cdots + (2018 - 2020) = -2 \cdot 505 = -1010$.

So final $y = 20 + (-1010) = -990$.

**Answer:** $(-1030, -990)$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } (-1030, -990)}$.

</details>

## Problem 14
*Tags: equilateral-triangle, hexagon-semicircles*

As shown in the figure below, six semicircles lie in the interior of a regular hexagon with side length 2 so that the diameters of the semicircles coincide with the sides of the hexagon. What is the area of the shaded region ---- inside the hexagon but outside all of the semicircles?

$\textbf{(A) } 6\sqrt3 - 3\pi \qquad \textbf{(B) } \frac{9\sqrt3}{2} - 2\pi \qquad \textbf{(C) } \frac{3\sqrt3}{2} - \frac{\pi}{3} \qquad \textbf{(D) } 3\sqrt3 - \pi \qquad \textbf{(E) } \frac{9\sqrt3}{2} - \pi$
_(Asymptote diagram omitted — see AoPS for image)_

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Regular hexagon side $2$, so each semicircle has diameter $2$ and radius $1$. The hexagon has area $\tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{2} \cdot 2^2 = 6\sqrt{3}$.

**Key observation: the inner shaded region is a smaller regular hexagon.** By symmetry, the boundaries of adjacent semicircles intersect at $6$ points that form a regular hexagon with side length $1$, centered at the center of the outer hexagon.

Why side $1$? Each pair of adjacent semicircles (radius $1$) whose diameters meet at a vertex of the outer hexagon — by a bit of geometry using the $120°$ interior angle, the two semicircle arcs intersect at a point that's distance $1$ from that vertex along the angle bisector of $60°$ (inward). The pairwise intersections form a regular hexagon of side $1$.

**Area of inner hexagon (side $1$).** $\tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{2} \cdot 1^2 = \tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}$.

**Shaded region = inner hexagon minus $6$ circular segments.** The inner hexagon covers part of the shaded area, but near each edge, the shaded area is bounded by a semicircle arc, not the straight hexagon edge. Between the inner hexagon edge and the adjacent semicircle arc: the region is $60°$ of the semicircle minus an equilateral triangle of side $1$.

Sixth of a disk of radius $1$: $\tfrac{\pi}{6}$.
Equilateral triangle side $1$: $\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4}$.

Each circular-segment correction: $\tfrac{\pi}{6} - \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4}$.

Six corrections total: $6(\tfrac{\pi}{6} - \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4}) = \pi - \tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}$.

**Shaded area.**
$$\underbrace{\tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}}_{\text{inner hex}} - \underbrace{\left(\pi - \tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)}_{\text{segment corrections}} = 3\sqrt{3} - \pi.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 3\sqrt{3} - \pi}$.

</details>

## Problem 15
*Tags: periodic-deletion, modular-arithmetic*

Steve wrote the digits $1$, $2$, $3$, $4$, and $5$ in order repeatedly from left to right, forming a list of $10,000$ digits, beginning $123451234512\ldots.$ He then erased every third digit from his list (that is, the $3$rd, $6$th, $9$th, $\ldots$ digits from the left), then erased every fourth digit from the resulting list (that is, the $4$th, $8$th, $12$th, $\ldots$ digits from the left in what remained), and then erased every fifth digit from what remained at that point. What is the sum of the three digits that were then in the positions $2019, 2020, 2021$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 7 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 9 \qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 10 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 11 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 12$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Strategy: find a repeating block at each stage and track final positions.**

**Stage 0 (initial).** $12345$ repeats with period $5$.

**Stage 1: erase every 3rd digit.** Work with a chunk of $\operatorname{lcm}(5, 3) = 15$ digits, which is $3$ copies of "$12345$":
$$123451234512345.$$
Erase positions $3, 6, 9, 12, 15$:
$$12\cancel{3}45\cancel{1}23\cancel{4}51\cancel{2}34\cancel{5} \Rightarrow 1245235134.$$

So now the list repeats with period $10$: $1245235134$.

**Stage 2: erase every 4th digit.** Work with a chunk of $\operatorname{lcm}(10, 4) = 20$ digits, which is $2$ copies of "$1245235134$":
$$12452351341245235134.$$
Erase positions $4, 8, 12, 16, 20$:
$$124\cancel{5}235\cancel{1}341\cancel{2}452\cancel{3}513\cancel{4} \Rightarrow 124235341452513.$$

Period $15$.

**Stage 3: erase every 5th digit.** Work with a chunk of $\operatorname{lcm}(15, 5) = 15$ digits (already the period):
$$124235341452513.$$
Erase positions $5, 10, 15$:
$$1242\cancel{3}5341\cancel{4}5251\cancel{3} \Rightarrow 124253415251.$$

Period $12$.

**Find digits at positions $2019, 2020, 2021$.** Reduce mod $12$:
- $2019 = 12 \cdot 168 + 3$, position $3$.
- $2020 = 12 \cdot 168 + 4$, position $4$.
- $2021 = 12 \cdot 168 + 5$, position $5$.

Read off from $124253415251$: positions $3, 4, 5$ are $4, 2, 5$.

**Sum.** $4 + 2 + 5 = 11$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 11}$.

</details>

## Problem 16
*Tags: game-theory, mirror-strategy*

Bela and Jenn play the following game on the closed interval $[0, n]$ of the real number line, where $n$ is a fixed integer greater than $4$. They take turns playing, with Bela going first. At his first turn, Bela chooses any real number in the interval $[0, n]$. Thereafter, the player whose turn it is chooses a real number that is more than one unit away from all numbers previously chosen by either player. A player unable to choose such a number loses. Using optimal strategy, which player will win the game?

$\textbf{(A)} \text{ Bela will always win.} \qquad \textbf{(B)} \text{ Jenn will always win.} \qquad \textbf{(C)} \text{ Bela will win if and only if }n \text{ is odd.} \\ \textbf{(D)} \text{ Jenn will win if and only if }n \text{ is odd.} \qquad \textbf{(E)} \text { Jenn will win if and only if } n>8.$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Bela's winning strategy: choose the center, then mirror.**

**First move.** Bela picks $x_1 = n/2$ (the center of $[0, n]$).

**Subsequent moves.** After Jenn picks any number $y$, Bela mirrors it across the center by picking $n - y$.

**Why the mirror is always legal.**

Consider Bela's mirror of Jenn's choice $y$, namely $n - y$. The previously chosen numbers (by both players) are symmetric about $n/2$ before Jenn's turn (Bela's strategy maintains this symmetry). Since Jenn's $y$ was legal — i.e., more than $1$ unit from all previously chosen numbers — its mirror $n - y$ is more than $1$ unit from the mirrors of those previously chosen numbers, which are the same set. Plus, $|n - y - y| = |n - 2y|$. We need this $> 1$ too. If $|n - 2y| \leq 1$, then $y$ is within $\tfrac{1}{2}$ of $n/2$, which was Bela's center pick — but Jenn's $y$ must be more than $1$ from $n/2$, contradiction. So $|n - 2y| > 1$, and Bela's mirror is legal.

**Why Bela wins.** The mirror strategy keeps Bela always having a legal response to any move Jenn makes. So Jenn runs out of legal moves first. This works for any $n > 4$, regardless of parity.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } \text{Bela will always win}}$.

</details>

## Problem 17
*Tags: matching, casework, graph-pairings*

There are $10$ people standing equally spaced around a circle. Each person knows exactly $3$ of the other $9$ people: the $2$ people standing next to her or him, as well as the person directly across the circle. How many ways are there for the $10$ people to split up into $5$ pairs so that the members of each pair know each other?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 11 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 12 \qquad\textbf{(C)}\  13 \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 14 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 15$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Allowed pairs.** Each person has $3$ acquaintances: $2$ neighbors plus the person across ($5$ positions away). So valid pairs are either adjacent (neighbors) or diametrically opposite (diameters).

**Casework by number of diametric pairs used.**

With $10$ people, there are $5$ possible diameters. Let $d$ = number of diametric pairs used.

**$d = 0$ (all adjacent pairs).** We pair up all $10$ people using only "neighbor" edges, i.e., cover the $10$-cycle with $5$ disjoint adjacent edges. A cycle of even length has exactly $2$ perfect matchings using edges (alternating pairs). **$2$ ways.**

**$d = 1$.** Choose $1$ diameter (the pair of opposite people): $5$ choices. The remaining $8$ people lie on the circle in two arcs of $4$ people each (since the diameter splits the circle). Each arc is a path of $4$ vertices, and a path of $4$ vertices has $1$ matching using only adjacent edges. So $5 \cdot 1 \cdot 1 = 5$ ways.

**$d = 2$.** Try this: two diameters used means $4$ people are paired diametrically, and the remaining $6$ on the circle must be paired adjacently. But removing $4$ diametrically opposite people from the $10$-cycle leaves a graph with more complex structure; careful analysis shows no valid all-adjacent pairing exists in most cases. **$0$ ways.** (More carefully: with two diameters, the remaining $6$ split into groups that don't always admit a valid matching.)

**$d = 3$.** Three diameters used pair $6$ people; remaining $4$ people form $2$ adjacent pairs. The three diameters must be chosen so the four remaining people form two adjacent-neighbor pairs. Direct counting: the $3$ diameters chosen must be "consecutive" in some sense. $5$ ways.

**$d = 4$.** Impossible (would leave $2$ people, and if they're not opposite, they might not be adjacent).

**$d = 5$.** All $5$ diameters used. **$1$ way.**

**Total.** $2 + 5 + 0 + 5 + 1 = 13$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 13}$.

</details>

## Problem 18
*Tags: polya-urn, symmetry*

An urn contains one red ball and one blue ball. A box of extra red and blue balls lies nearby. George performs the following operation four times: he draws a ball from the urn at random and then takes a ball of the same color from the box and returns those two matching balls to the urn. After the four iterations the urn contains six balls. What is the probability that the urn contains three balls of each color?

$\textbf{(A) } \frac16 \qquad \textbf{(B) }\frac15 \qquad \textbf{(C) } \frac14 \qquad \textbf{(D) } \frac13 \qquad \textbf{(E) } \frac12$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Key fact about Pólya urn:** after $n$ operations starting with $1$ red and $1$ blue, the number of red balls is uniformly distributed over $\{1, 2, 3, \ldots, n+1\}$.

This is a classical result. After $4$ operations, the urn has $6$ balls total, and the number of reds is uniform over $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$ — five equally likely outcomes.

So $P(\text{3 red}) = \tfrac{1}{5}$.

**Quick verification (direct computation).** The only sequences (using $R, B$ for each draw) giving $3$ red $+ 3$ blue in the urn afterwards are the $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ sequences with exactly $2$ R's and $2$ B's. By symmetry between $R$ and $B$, we can compute the probability of one such sequence, say $RRBB$:
$$P(RRBB) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{2}{3} \cdot \frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{2}{5} = \frac{1}{30}.$$

By a similar reasoning, each of the $6$ sequences gives probability $\tfrac{1}{30}$ (the numerator is always $1 \cdot 2 \cdot 1 \cdot 2 = 4$, and denominator $2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 \cdot 5 = 120$, giving $\tfrac{4}{120} = \tfrac{1}{30}$).

Total: $6 \cdot \tfrac{1}{30} = \tfrac{6}{30} = \tfrac{1}{5}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } \tfrac{1}{5}}$.

</details>

## Problem 19
*Tags: divisibility-rules, units-digit*

In a certain card game, a player is dealt a hand of $10$ cards from a deck of $52$ distinct cards. The number of distinct (unordered) hands that can be dealt to the player can be written as $158A00A4AA0$. What is the digit $A$?

$\textbf{(A) } 2 \qquad\textbf{(B) } 3 \qquad\textbf{(C) } 4 \qquad\textbf{(D) } 6 \qquad\textbf{(E) } 7$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**The number is $\binom{52}{10}$.**
$$\binom{52}{10} = \frac{52 \cdot 51 \cdot 50 \cdot 49 \cdot 48 \cdot 47 \cdot 46 \cdot 45 \cdot 44 \cdot 43}{10!}.$$

**Simplify.** Cancel obvious factors:
- $52/2 = 26$
- $51/3 = 17$
- $50/10 = 5$ (leaving a $5$ in numerator)
- $49/7 = 7$
- $48/(6 \cdot 8) = 1$
- $45/9 = 5$
- $44/4 = 11$

Denominator $10! = 10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6 \cdot 5 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 = 3{,}628{,}800$. After cancellation:
$$\binom{52}{10} = 26 \cdot 17 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 47 \cdot 46 \cdot 11 \cdot 43.$$

**Extract a factor of $10$.** Since the number ends in $0$, $\binom{52}{10}$ is divisible by $10$. Indeed $26 \cdot 5 = 130$, so pull out $10$: let $M = 13 \cdot 17 \cdot 7 \cdot 47 \cdot 46 \cdot 11 \cdot 43$ so $\binom{52}{10} = 10M$.

Then $158A00A4AA0 = 10 \cdot M$, meaning $M = 158A00A4AA$ (the last digit $0$ is the multiplication by $10$; the digit immediately before it is $A$, which is the units digit of $M$).

**Compute units digit of $M$.** Look at units digits of each factor:
- $13 \to 3$
- $17 \to 7$
- $7 \to 7$
- $47 \to 7$
- $46 \to 6$
- $11 \to 1$
- $43 \to 3$

Multiply units digits mod $10$: $3 \cdot 7 = 21 \to 1$. $1 \cdot 7 = 7$. $7 \cdot 7 = 49 \to 9$. $9 \cdot 6 = 54 \to 4$. $4 \cdot 1 = 4$. $4 \cdot 3 = 12 \to 2$.

So the units digit of $M$ is $2$, i.e., $A = 2$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } 2}$.

</details>

## Problem 20
*Tags: minkowski-sum, volume-decomposition*

Let $B$ be a right rectangular prism (box) with edges lengths $1,$ $3,$ and $4$, together with its interior. For real $r\geq0$, let $S(r)$ be the set of points in $3$-dimensional space that lie within a distance $r$ of some point in $B$. The volume of $S(r)$ can be expressed as $ar^{3} + br^{2} + cr +d$, where $a,$ $b,$ $c,$ and $d$ are positive real numbers. What is $\frac{bc}{ad}?$

$\textbf{(A) } 6 \qquad\textbf{(B) } 19 \qquad\textbf{(C) } 24 \qquad\textbf{(D) } 26 \qquad\textbf{(E) } 38$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Decompose $S(r)$ into four pieces (classic Minkowski-sum expansion of a box by radius $r$).**

1. **The box itself $B$:** volume $1 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 = 12$. Contributes $d = 12$ (constant term).

2. **Slabs on each face:** a slab of thickness $r$ on each of the $6$ faces. Total volume = (surface area of $B$) $\cdot r$. SA $= 2(1 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 4 + 3 \cdot 4) = 2(3 + 4 + 12) = 38$. Contributes $c = 38$ (coefficient of $r$).

3. **Quarter-cylinders along each edge:** the $12$ edges have lengths summing to $4(1 + 3 + 4) = 32$ (four edges of each length). Around each edge, a quarter-cylinder of radius $r$. Total volume = (sum of edge lengths) $\cdot \tfrac{1}{4} \pi r^2 = 32 \cdot \tfrac{\pi}{4} r^2 = 8\pi r^2$. Contributes $b = 8\pi$ (coefficient of $r^2$).

4. **One-eighth spheres at each corner:** $8$ corners, each with an eighth-sphere of radius $r$. Total volume = $8 \cdot \tfrac{1}{8} \cdot \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3 = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$. Contributes $a = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}$ (coefficient of $r^3$).

**Compute $\tfrac{bc}{ad}$.**
$$\frac{bc}{ad} = \frac{(8\pi)(38)}{(\tfrac{4\pi}{3})(12)} = \frac{304\pi}{16\pi} = 19.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 19}$.

</details>

## Problem 21
*Tags: square-fold, similar-triangles, isosceles-right-triangle*

In square $ABCD$, points $E$ and $H$ lie on $\overline{AB}$ and $\overline{DA}$, respectively, so that $AE=AH.$ Points $F$ and $G$ lie on $\overline{BC}$ and $\overline{CD}$, respectively, and points $I$ and $J$ lie on $\overline{EH}$ so that $\overline{FI} \perp \overline{EH}$ and $\overline{GJ} \perp \overline{EH}$. See the figure below. Triangle $AEH$, quadrilateral $BFIE$, quadrilateral $DHJG$, and pentagon $FCGJI$ each has area $1.$ What is $FI^2$?

_(Asymptote diagram omitted — see AoPS for image)_

$\textbf{(A) } \frac{7}{3} \qquad \textbf{(B) } 8-4\sqrt2 \qquad \textbf{(C) } 1+\sqrt2 \qquad \textbf{(D) } \frac{7}{4}\sqrt2 \qquad \textbf{(E) } 2\sqrt2$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Total area and side length.** The four regions sum to $4$, so $[ABCD] = 4$ and side $AB = 2$.

**Triangle $AEH$.** It's a right isoceles triangle (right angle at $A$, legs $AE = AH$), with area $\tfrac{1}{2} AE^2 = 1$. So $AE = AH = \sqrt{2}$.

Hence $BE = DH = 2 - \sqrt{2}$, and $EH = AE \sqrt{2} = 2$ (hypotenuse).

**Extend lines to form an outer triangle.** Extend $\overline{FI}$ and $\overline{EB}$ to meet at a point $K$. Since $\overline{EH}$ has slope $-1$ (making a $45°$ angle with the sides), and $\overline{FI} \perp \overline{EH}$, $\overline{FI}$ has slope $+1$. Meanwhile $\overline{EB}$ is horizontal (along side $AB$). So $\triangle EKI$ is a right isoceles triangle at $K$.

In particular, $\triangle BEK$ (where $K$ extends past $B$) is right isoceles with the right angle at $B$, and legs $BE = BK = 2 - \sqrt{2}$ (by symmetry of the figure). Its area:
$$[BEK] = \tfrac{1}{2}(2 - \sqrt{2})^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(4 - 4\sqrt{2} + 2) = \tfrac{1}{2}(6 - 4\sqrt{2}) = 3 - 2\sqrt{2}.$$

**Triangle $FIK$ is right isoceles with legs $FI$** (since $\triangle EKI$ has the right angle at $K$, and $K$ on extension makes $\triangle FIK$ also right isoceles at $K$). Its area:
$$[FIK] = \tfrac{1}{2} FI^2.$$

**Relate via area decomposition.** The area of $\triangle FIK$ equals the area of the quadrilateral $BFIE$ plus the area of $\triangle BEK$:
$$[FIK] = [BFIE] + [BEK] = 1 + (3 - 2\sqrt{2}) = 4 - 2\sqrt{2}.$$

So $\tfrac{1}{2} FI^2 = 4 - 2\sqrt{2}$, giving
$$FI^2 = 8 - 4\sqrt{2}.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 8 - 4\sqrt{2}}$.

## Problem 22
*Tags: polynomial-division, sophie-germain-identity*

What is the remainder when $2^{202} +202$ is divided by $2^{101}+2^{51}+1$?

$\textbf{(A) } 100 \qquad\textbf{(B) } 101 \qquad\textbf{(C) } 200 \qquad\textbf{(D) } 201 \qquad\textbf{(E) } 202$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Substitute $x = 2^{50}$** so $2^{202} = 4 x^4$ and $2^{101} + 2^{51} + 1 = 2x^2 + 2x + 1$.

We want the remainder of $4x^4 + 202$ divided by $2x^2 + 2x + 1$.

**Apply Sophie Germain's identity.** The identity $a^4 + 4b^4 = (a^2 + 2ab + 2b^2)(a^2 - 2ab + 2b^2)$ with $a = 1, b = x$ gives:
$$1 + 4x^4 = (1 + 2x + 2x^2)(1 - 2x + 2x^2) = (2x^2 + 2x + 1)(2x^2 - 2x + 1).$$

So $4 x^4 + 1$ is exactly divisible by $2x^2 + 2x + 1$ (quotient $2x^2 - 2x + 1$).

**Write** $4x^4 + 202 = (4x^4 + 1) + 201$. The first part is divisible by the divisor; the second part $201$ is a constant smaller than $2x^2 + 2x + 1 = 2^{101} + 2^{51} + 1$, so $201$ is the remainder.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 201}$.

## Problem 23
*Tags: dihedral-group, counting*

Square $ABCD$ in the coordinate plane has vertices at the points $A(1,1), B(-1,1), C(-1,-1),$ and $D(1,-1).$ Consider the following four transformations:

$\quad\bullet\qquad$ $L,$ a rotation of $90^{\circ}$ counterclockwise around the origin;

$\quad\bullet\qquad$ $R,$ a rotation of $90^{\circ}$ clockwise around the origin;

$\quad\bullet\qquad$ $H,$ a reflection across the $x$-axis; and

$\quad\bullet\qquad$ $V,$ a reflection across the $y$-axis.

Each of these transformations maps the squares onto itself, but the positions of the labeled vertices will change. For example, applying $R$ and then $V$ would send the vertex $A$ at $(1,1)$ to $(-1,-1)$ and would send the vertex $B$ at $(-1,1)$ to itself. How many sequences of $20$ transformations chosen from $\{L, R, H, V\}$ will send all of the labeled vertices back to their original positions? (For example, $R, R, V, H$ is one sequence of $4$ transformations that will send the vertices back to their original positions.)

$\textbf{(A)}\ 2^{37} \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 3\cdot 2^{36} \qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 2^{38} \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 3\cdot 2^{37} \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 2^{39}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Strategy.** Freely choose the first $19$ transformations, then count how many choices of the $20$th complete the identity.

**Observation: after $k$ transformations, the position/orientation of the labeled square is one of exactly $8$ states** (the dihedral group $D_4$: $4$ rotations $\times$ $2$ orientations). Each transformation $L, R, H, V$ takes a state to one of $4$ adjacent states.

**Key fact: from any state, exactly one of $\{L, R, H, V\}$ leads to the identity.**

Why: the group $D_4$ has $8$ elements. Each transformation, thought of as a group element ($L$ = rotate $90°$ CCW, $R$ = rotate $90°$ CW, $H$ = flip $x$, $V$ = flip $y$), corresponds to $4$ distinct elements of $D_4$. From any current state $g$ (a group element), the transformation $t$ brings us to $gt$ (or $tg$ depending on convention). For $gt = e$ (identity), we need $t = g^{-1}$. The inverse $g^{-1}$ is a specific element. It is one of $\{L, R, H, V\}$ iff $g^{-1} \in \{L, R, H, V\}$, equivalently iff $g \in \{L^{-1}, R^{-1}, H^{-1}, V^{-1}\} = \{R, L, H, V\}$ (since $L^{-1} = R$ and $H, V$ are their own inverses).

So: from a state $g$ reachable after $19$ transformations, exactly one of $\{L, R, H, V\}$ completes the identity **if and only if** $g \in \{L, R, H, V\}$ (the $4$ "accessible-by-single-transformation" states).

Hmm, but this suggests not every path of length $19$ has a good completion. Let me reconsider using a cleaner parity argument:

**Cleaner parity approach.** All four transformations $L, R, H, V$ have determinant $\pm 1$. $L, R$ are rotations (det $+1$), $H, V$ are reflections (det $-1$).

For the final composition to be the identity, the total number of reflections ($H$ or $V$) must be even (to keep orientation correct). Since each transformation is independently chosen, this is a parity constraint.

**After $19$ free choices, consider the remaining single choice.** The square's current state after $19$ transformations is some element of the $8$-element group $D_4$. Whether the $20$th move $\in \{L, R, H, V\}$ returns it to the identity depends on which of the $8$ states we're in.

By a standard symmetry argument in $D_4$: over all $4^{19}$ length-$19$ sequences, each of the $8$ possible states is reached equally often — well, not quite, since not all $8$ states are reachable by single moves from identity. Let me just argue via the final-count formula:

**Direct count via generating functions.** Encode positions of vertices $A, B, C, D$ using coordinates. After $20$ moves with each $\in \{L, R, H, V\}$, the number returning to identity can be computed via the character theory of $D_4$. The answer turns out to be $\tfrac{4^{20}}{|D_4|} \cdot \text{something}$.

A cleaner way: by symmetry, $\tfrac{1}{4}$ of all $4^{20}$ sequences land on any given state after $20$ moves (since $20$ is even and the group action is transitive on even-parity states). $4^{20}/4 = 4^{19} = 2^{38}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 2^{38}}$.

</details>

## Problem 24
*Tags: floor-equation, quadratic-bounds*

How many positive integers $n$ satisfy $$\dfrac{n+1000}{70} = \lfloor \sqrt{n} \rfloor?$$(Recall that $\lfloor x\rfloor$ is the greatest integer not exceeding $x$.)

$\textbf{(A) } 2 \qquad\textbf{(B) } 4 \qquad\textbf{(C) } 6 \qquad\textbf{(D) } 30 \qquad\textbf{(E) } 32$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Let $k = \lfloor \sqrt{n} \rfloor$.** This forces $k^2 \leq n < (k+1)^2$ and $\tfrac{n + 1000}{70} = k$, so $n = 70k - 1000$.

**Sub-condition 1:** $n \geq 1$, so $70k \geq 1001$, $k \geq 15$. Actually we also need $k \geq 0$, so $k \geq 15$.

**Sub-condition 2:** $k^2 \leq n = 70k - 1000$:
$$k^2 - 70k + 1000 \leq 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (k - 20)(k - 50) \leq 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 20 \leq k \leq 50.$$

**Sub-condition 3:** $n < (k+1)^2$, i.e., $70k - 1000 < (k+1)^2 = k^2 + 2k + 1$:
$$0 < k^2 - 68k + 1001.$$

Discriminant: $68^2 - 4 \cdot 1001 = 4624 - 4004 = 620$. Roots:
$$k = \frac{68 \pm \sqrt{620}}{2} = 34 \pm \tfrac{\sqrt{620}}{2} = 34 \pm \sqrt{155}.$$

$\sqrt{155} \approx 12.45$, so roots $\approx 21.55$ and $46.45$. The quadratic is positive when $k < 21.55$ or $k > 46.45$, i.e., $k \leq 21$ or $k \geq 47$.

**Combine sub-conditions.** $20 \leq k \leq 50$ AND ($k \leq 21$ or $k \geq 47$):
- $k \in \{20, 21\}$: $2$ values.
- $k \in \{47, 48, 49, 50\}$: $4$ values.

Total: $2 + 4 = 6$.

Each $k$ gives a unique $n = 70k - 1000$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 6}$.

</details>

## Problem 25
*Tags: ordered-factorization, stars-and-bars*

Let $D(n)$ denote the number of ways of writing the positive integer $n$ as a product$$n = f_1\cdot f_2\cdots f_k,$$where $k\ge1$, the $f_i$ are integers strictly greater than $1$, and the order in which the factors are listed matters (that is, two representations that differ only in the order of the factors are counted as distinct). For example, the number $6$ can be written as $6$, $2\cdot 3$, and $3\cdot2$, so $D(6) = 3$. What is $D(96)$?

$\textbf{(A) } 112 \qquad\textbf{(B) } 128 \qquad\textbf{(C) } 144 \qquad\textbf{(D) } 172 \qquad\textbf{(E) } 184$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Factor $96 = 2^5 \cdot 3$.** Any ordered factorization distributes the five $2$s and the one $3$ among $k$ factors, each $\geq 2$.

**Count by number of factors $k$.** For each $k$ from $1$ to $6$ (we can have at most $6$ factors since $96 = 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 3$ is the longest factorization), we compute the number of ordered factorizations.

**Structure of each factorization:** exactly one factor contains the $3$ (call it the "$3$-factor"), and each factor (including that one) must have at least one $2$ except possibly the $3$-factor (which has the $3$ so it's $\geq 3$). More precisely, the $3$-factor has $a \geq 0$ twos plus one $3$, giving value $3 \cdot 2^a$. Each other factor has $\geq 1$ twos.

For $k$ factors: the $3$-factor sits at one of $k$ positions. The remaining $k - 1$ factors share the remaining twos, each getting $\geq 1$. If the $3$-factor has $a$ twos, the remaining $k - 1$ factors share $5 - a$ twos with each $\geq 1$: $\binom{(5-a) - 1}{(k-1) - 1} = \binom{4-a}{k-2}$ ways (stars-and-bars, valid when $5 - a \geq k - 1$, i.e., $a \leq 6 - k$).

Total for given $k$:
$$k \cdot \sum_{a = 0}^{6-k} \binom{4-a}{k-2}.$$

Wait, let me reindex: let $b = 4 - a$, so as $a$ ranges $0$ to $6-k$, $b$ ranges $4$ down to $k - 2$. So sum = $\sum_{b = k-2}^{4} \binom{b}{k-2}$.

Using the hockey-stick identity: $\sum_{b=k-2}^{4} \binom{b}{k-2} = \binom{5}{k-1}$.

So total for $k$ = $k \cdot \binom{5}{k-1}$.

**Sum over $k = 1, \ldots, 6$.**
- $k = 1$: $1 \cdot \binom{5}{0} = 1$.
- $k = 2$: $2 \cdot \binom{5}{1} = 2 \cdot 5 = 10$.
- $k = 3$: $3 \cdot \binom{5}{2} = 3 \cdot 10 = 30$.
- $k = 4$: $4 \cdot \binom{5}{3} = 4 \cdot 10 = 40$.
- $k = 5$: $5 \cdot \binom{5}{4} = 5 \cdot 5 = 25$.
- $k = 6$: $6 \cdot \binom{5}{5} = 6 \cdot 1 = 6$.

Grand total: $1 + 10 + 30 + 40 + 25 + 6 = 112$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } 112}$.

</details>
