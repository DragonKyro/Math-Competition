## Problem 1
*Tags: absolute-value, counting-integers*

How many integer values of $x$ satisfy $|x|<3\pi$?

$\textbf{(A)} ~9 \qquad\textbf{(B)} ~10 \qquad\textbf{(C)} ~18 \qquad\textbf{(D)} ~19 \qquad\textbf{(E)} ~20$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Unpack the absolute value.** $|x| < 3\pi$ is equivalent to $-3\pi < x < 3\pi$.

**Estimate the bound.** $\pi \approx 3.14159$, so $3\pi \approx 9.42$.

**Count integers strictly between $-9.42$ and $9.42$.** These are the integers $-9, -8, \ldots, 8, 9$. That's $9$ negatives, $9$ positives, and $0$: total $9 + 9 + 1 = 19$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 19}$.

</details>

## Problem 2
*Tags: absolute-value, sign-analysis*

What is the value of $\sqrt{\left(3-2\sqrt{3}\right)^2}+\sqrt{\left(3+2\sqrt{3}\right)^2}$?

$\textbf{(A)} ~0 \qquad\textbf{(B)} ~4\sqrt{3}-6 \qquad\textbf{(C)} ~6 \qquad\textbf{(D)} ~4\sqrt{3} \qquad\textbf{(E)} ~4\sqrt{3}+6$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Use $\sqrt{a^2} = |a|$.** So the expression equals $|3 - 2\sqrt{3}| + |3 + 2\sqrt{3}|$.

**Determine signs.** Compare $3$ and $2\sqrt{3}$ by squaring: $3^2 = 9$ and $(2\sqrt{3})^2 = 12$. Since $12 > 9$, we have $2\sqrt{3} > 3$, so:
- $3 - 2\sqrt{3} < 0 \Rightarrow |3 - 2\sqrt{3}| = 2\sqrt{3} - 3$.
- $3 + 2\sqrt{3} > 0 \Rightarrow |3 + 2\sqrt{3}| = 3 + 2\sqrt{3}$.

**Add.**
$$(2\sqrt{3} - 3) + (3 + 2\sqrt{3}) = 4\sqrt{3}.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 4\sqrt{3}}$.

</details>

## Problem 3
*Tags: system-of-equations, percentages*

In an after-school program for juniors and seniors, there is a debate team with an equal number of students from each class on the team. Among the $28$ students in the program, $25\%$ of the juniors as a class and $10\%$ of the seniors as a class are on the debate team. How many juniors are in the program?

$\textbf{(A)} ~5 \qquad\textbf{(B)} ~6 \qquad\textbf{(C)} ~8 \qquad\textbf{(D)} ~11 \qquad\textbf{(E)} ~20$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Set up variables.** Let $j$ be the number of juniors and $s$ the number of seniors in the program.

**Translate the conditions.**
- Total: $j + s = 28$.
- Equal numbers on team: $0.25 \, j = 0.10 \, s$, i.e., $\tfrac{j}{4} = \tfrac{s}{10}$, which gives $10j = 4s$ or $5j = 2s$.

**Solve.** From $5j = 2s$: $s = \tfrac{5j}{2}$. Substitute into $j + s = 28$:
$$j + \tfrac{5j}{2} = 28 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tfrac{7j}{2} = 28 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad j = 8.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 8}$.

</details>

## Problem 4
*Tags: counting-pairs*

At a math contest, $57$ students are wearing blue shirts, and another $75$ students are wearing yellow shirts. The $132$ students are assigned into $66$ pairs. In exactly $23$ of these pairs, both students are wearing blue shirts. In how many pairs are both students wearing yellow shirts?

$\textbf{(A)} ~23 \qquad\textbf{(B)} ~32 \qquad\textbf{(C)} ~37 \qquad\textbf{(D)} ~41 \qquad\textbf{(E)} ~64$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Count blue shirts used in all-blue pairs.** $23$ pairs of two blue students each use $2 \cdot 23 = 46$ blue-shirted students.

**Count remaining blue shirts.** Out of $57$ blue students, $57 - 46 = 11$ are paired with someone else — necessarily a yellow-shirted student (otherwise they'd form another all-blue pair).

So exactly $11$ pairs are blue-yellow.

**Count yellow shirts used in blue-yellow pairs.** Each such pair uses one yellow, so $11$ yellow students are in blue-yellow pairs.

**Remaining yellows pair with each other.** $75 - 11 = 64$ yellow students are paired among themselves, forming $64/2 = 32$ all-yellow pairs.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 32}$.

</details>

## Problem 5
*Tags: factoring, casework*

The ages of Jonie's four cousins are distinct single-digit positive integers. Two of the cousins' ages multiplied together give $24$, while the other two multiply to $30$. What is the sum of the ages of Jonie's four cousins?

$\textbf{(A)} ~21 \qquad\textbf{(B)} ~22 \qquad\textbf{(C)} ~23 \qquad\textbf{(D)} ~24 \qquad\textbf{(E)} ~25$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**List factor pairs with both factors in $\{1, \ldots, 9\}$.**
- Product $24$: $(3, 8), (4, 6)$ (others like $(2, 12)$ or $(1, 24)$ use double-digits).
- Product $30$: $(5, 6)$ only (others: $(2, 15), (3, 10)$ have double-digits).

**Require all four ages distinct.** The product-$30$ pair must be $(5, 6)$. For the four ages to be distinct, the product-$24$ pair cannot reuse $5$ or $6$. That rules out $(4, 6)$. So the product-$24$ pair is $(3, 8)$.

**Sum.** $3 + 8 + 5 + 6 = 22$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 22}$.

</details>

## Problem 6
*Tags: weighted-average*

Ms. Blackwell gives an exam to two classes. The mean of the scores of the students in the morning class is $84$, and the afternoon class's mean score is $70$. The ratio of the number of students in the morning class to the number of students in the afternoon class is $\frac{3}{4}$. What is the mean of the scores of all the students?

$\textbf{(A)} ~74 \qquad\textbf{(B)} ~75 \qquad\textbf{(C)} ~76 \qquad\textbf{(D)} ~77 \qquad\textbf{(E)} ~78$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Pick convenient class sizes.** Since only the *ratio* matters, let morning have $3$ students and afternoon have $4$ students (total $7$).

**Combined mean.**
$$\text{mean} = \frac{3 \cdot 84 + 4 \cdot 70}{3 + 4} = \frac{252 + 280}{7} = \frac{532}{7} = 76.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 76}$.

*(Equivalently: the combined mean is the weighted average $\tfrac{3}{7}(84) + \tfrac{4}{7}(70) = 36 + 40 = 76$.)*

</details>

## Problem 7
*Tags: tangent-circles, area-optimization*

In a plane, four circles with radii $1,3,5,$ and $7$ are tangent to line $\ell$ at the same point $A,$ but they may be on either side of $\ell$. Region $S$ consists of all the points that lie inside exactly one of the four circles. What is the maximum possible area of region $S$?

$\textbf{(A) }24\pi \qquad \textbf{(B) }32\pi \qquad \textbf{(C) }64\pi \qquad \textbf{(D) }65\pi \qquad \textbf{(E) }84\pi$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Geometry of the circles.** Each circle is tangent to $\ell$ at $A$, so its center lies on the line through $A$ perpendicular to $\ell$, at distance equal to its radius. Two circles on the same side of $\ell$ are *nested* (the larger contains the smaller, sharing the tangent point).

Whenever two circles are on the same side of $\ell$, the smaller is strictly inside the larger. So stacking several circles on one side makes the smaller ones contribute $0$ to $S$ (they're inside strictly larger ones, so they're in $\geq 2$ circles, not exactly $1$).

**Strategy: maximize the area of the largest circle while putting the other three all inside a "stack" where each contributes minimally.**

Consider the placement: radius $7$ alone on one side, and radii $1, 3, 5$ on the other side (nested, $1 \subset 3 \subset 5$).

- Radius $7$ circle: points in it are in no other circle ✓. Contribution to $S$: $\pi \cdot 7^2 = 49\pi$.
- Radius $5$ circle: contains the $3$-circle (which contains the $1$-circle). Points in only the $5$-circle = points in $5$-circle but not in $3$-circle = $\pi \cdot 5^2 - \pi \cdot 3^2 = 25\pi - 9\pi = 16\pi$.
- Radius $3$ circle: every point inside is also inside the $5$-circle (and maybe the $1$-circle). So every point is in $\geq 2$ circles. Contribution: $0$.
- Radius $1$ circle: inside both $3$ and $5$. Contribution: $0$.

**Total.** $49\pi + 16\pi = 65\pi$.

**Verify this is optimal.** Any configuration either nests as above (giving the answer $49\pi + 16\pi = 65\pi$) or splits the small circles across sides, e.g., $7, 1$ on one side (with $1 \subset 7$) and $5, 3$ on the other ($3 \subset 5$). That gives $(49 - 1)\pi + (25 - 9)\pi = 48\pi + 16\pi = 64\pi < 65\pi$. Other splits are strictly worse.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 65\pi}$.

</details>

## Problem 8
*Tags: spiral-grid, odd-squares-corners*

Mr. Zhou places all the integers from $1$ to $225$ into a $15$ by $15$ grid. He places $1$ in the middle square (eighth row and eighth column) and places other numbers one by one clockwise, as shown in part in the diagram below. What is the sum of the greatest number and the least number that appear in the second row from the top?
_(Asymptote diagram omitted — see AoPS for image)_

$\textbf{(A)} ~367 \qquad\textbf{(B)} ~368 \qquad\textbf{(C)} ~369 \qquad\textbf{(D)} ~379 \qquad\textbf{(E)} ~380$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Key observation: odd perfect squares sit at specific corners.** Following the clockwise spiral starting at $(8, 8) = 1$, it reaches:
- $3^2 = 9$ at $(7, 9)$ (top-right of $3 \times 3$ inner box)
- $5^2 = 25$ at $(6, 10)$ (top-right of $5 \times 5$)
- $\ldots$
- $(2k+1)^2$ at $(8-k, 8+k)$ (top-right of $(2k+1) \times (2k+1)$)

For $k = 7$: $15^2 = 225$ at position $(1, 15)$ — the top-right corner of the grid.

**Trace where row $2$ fits in the spiral.** After reaching $13^2 = 169$ at $(2, 14)$ — the top-right corner of the inner $13 \times 13$ box — the spiral steps *out* to the outer $15 \times 15$ ring:
- Step right to $(2, 15) = 170$ (enters outer ring).
- Go down column $15$: $(2, 15) = 170, (3, 15) = 171, \ldots, (15, 15) = 183$.
- Go left along row $15$: $(15, 15) = 183, \ldots, (15, 1) = 197$.
- Go up column $1$: $(15, 1) = 197, \ldots, (1, 1) = 211$.
- Go right along row $1$: $(1, 1) = 211, (1, 2) = 212, \ldots, (1, 15) = 225$.

**Find row $2$ entries.**
- $(2, 1)$: one below $(1, 1)$. Column $1$ filled going up: $(2, 1) = 210$ (one step below $211$).
- $(2, 2)$: inside the $13 \times 13$ box. Just before $169 = 13^2$ arrives at $(2, 14)$, the spiral traverses row $2$ of the $13 \times 13$ from $(2, 2)$ to $(2, 14)$. Inside the $13 \times 13$, tracing back, $(2, 2) = 157$ and $(2, 14) = 169$.
- $(2, 3), (2, 4), \ldots, (2, 14)$: values $158, 159, \ldots, 169$ (along row $2$ within the $13 \times 13$).
- $(2, 15) = 170$.

**Row $2$ values: $210, 157, 158, \ldots, 169, 170$.**
- Greatest: $210$.
- Least: $157$.

**Sum:** $210 + 157 = 367$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } 367}$.

</details>

## Problem 9
*Tags: rotation, reflection, inverse-transformations*

The point $P(a,b)$ in the $xy$-plane is first rotated counterclockwise by $90^\circ$ around the point $(1,5)$ and then reflected about the line $y = -x$. The image of $P$ after these two transformations is at $(-6,3)$. What is $b - a ?$

$\textbf{(A)} ~1 \qquad\textbf{(B)} ~3 \qquad\textbf{(C)} ~5 \qquad\textbf{(D)} ~7 \qquad\textbf{(E)} ~9$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Strategy: reverse each transformation.**

**Step 1: reverse the reflection about $y = -x$.** The reflection rule is $(x, y) \mapsto (-y, -x)$, which is its own inverse. So the point before the reflection is:
$$(-6, 3) \mapsto (-3, 6).$$

**Step 2: reverse the $90°$ counterclockwise rotation around $(1, 5)$.** The inverse is a $90°$ *clockwise* rotation around $(1, 5)$.

Translate so $(1, 5)$ becomes the origin: $(-3, 6) \to (-3 - 1, 6 - 5) = (-4, 1)$.

Rotate $90°$ clockwise: $(x, y) \mapsto (y, -x)$:
$$(-4, 1) \mapsto (1, -(-4)) = (1, 4).$$

Translate back: $(1, 4) + (1, 5) = (2, 9)$.

So $P = (a, b) = (2, 9)$ and $b - a = 9 - 2 = 7$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 7}$.

</details>

## Problem 10
*Tags: volume-cone, volume-cylinder*

An inverted cone with base radius $12  \mathrm{cm}$ and height $18  \mathrm{cm}$ is full of water. The water is poured into a tall cylinder whose horizontal base has radius of $24  \mathrm{cm}$. What is the height in centimeters of the water in the cylinder?

$\textbf{(A)} ~1.5 \qquad\textbf{(B)} ~3 \qquad\textbf{(C)} ~4 \qquad\textbf{(D)} ~4.5 \qquad\textbf{(E)} ~6$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Compute the cone's volume.**
$$V_{\text{cone}} = \tfrac{1}{3} \pi r^2 h = \tfrac{1}{3} \pi (12)^2 (18) = \tfrac{1}{3} \pi \cdot 144 \cdot 18 = 864 \pi \text{ cm}^3.$$

**Set up the cylinder's volume equation.** Let $h$ be the water height in the cylinder:
$$V_{\text{cylinder water}} = \pi r^2 h = \pi (24)^2 h = 576 \pi h.$$

**Equate.**
$$576 \pi h = 864 \pi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad h = \frac{864}{576} = \frac{3}{2} = 1.5.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } 1.5}$.

</details>

## Problem 11
*Tags: simons-favorite-factoring*

Grandma has just finished baking a large rectangular pan of brownies. She is planning to make rectangular pieces of equal size and shape, with straight cuts parallel to the sides of the pan. Each cut must be made entirely across the pan. Grandma wants to make the same number of interior pieces as pieces along the perimeter of the pan. What is the greatest possible number of brownies she can produce?

$\textbf{(A)} ~24 \qquad\textbf{(B)} ~30 \qquad\textbf{(C)} ~48 \qquad\textbf{(D)} ~60 \qquad\textbf{(E)} ~64$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Set up the equation.** Suppose the pan is cut into $m \times n$ pieces. Then:
- Total pieces: $mn$.
- Interior (non-edge) pieces: $(m - 2)(n - 2)$.
- Perimeter pieces: $mn - (m - 2)(n - 2) = 2m + 2n - 4$.

Condition: interior = perimeter, which (together with their sum being $mn$) means each is half of $mn$:
$$(m - 2)(n - 2) = \tfrac{mn}{2}.$$

**Expand and rearrange.**
$$2(m - 2)(n - 2) = mn$$
$$2mn - 4m - 4n + 8 = mn$$
$$mn - 4m - 4n + 8 = 0.$$

**Apply Simon's Favorite Factoring Trick.** Add $8$ to both sides to make the left side factor:
$$mn - 4m - 4n + 16 = 8 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (m - 4)(n - 4) = 8.$$

**List positive-integer factorizations of $8$.** Assuming $m \leq n$ and both $\geq 3$ (so pieces exist):
- $(m - 4, n - 4) = (1, 8) \Rightarrow (m, n) = (5, 12)$, giving $mn = 60$.
- $(m - 4, n - 4) = (2, 4) \Rightarrow (m, n) = (6, 8)$, giving $mn = 48$.

Also check negative options: $(m-4)(n-4) = 8$ with both negative gives $m < 4$ and $n < 4$, but that violates $m, n \geq 3$ (would give $m = n = 3$, and $(3-4)(3-4) = 1 \neq 8$). So only the positive pairs work.

**Maximize.** The greater of the two is $mn = 60$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 60}$.

</details>

## Problem 12
*Tags: prime-factorization, sum-of-divisors*

Let $N = 34 \cdot 34 \cdot 63 \cdot 270$. What is the ratio of the sum of the odd divisors of $N$ to the sum of the even divisors of $N$?

$\textbf{(A)} ~1 : 16 \qquad\textbf{(B)} ~1 : 15 \qquad\textbf{(C)} ~1 : 14 \qquad\textbf{(D)} ~1 : 8 \qquad\textbf{(E)} ~1 : 3$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Prime factorize $N$.**
- $34 = 2 \cdot 17$
- $63 = 3^2 \cdot 7$
- $270 = 2 \cdot 3^3 \cdot 5$

$$N = (2 \cdot 17)^2 \cdot (3^2 \cdot 7) \cdot (2 \cdot 3^3 \cdot 5) = 2^3 \cdot 3^5 \cdot 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 17^2.$$

**Key fact about odd/even divisors.** Every divisor of $N$ is of the form $2^a \cdot (\text{odd factor of } N)$ where $a \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$. Let $O$ be the sum of the *odd* divisors of $N$ (those with $a = 0$).

- Divisors with $a = 0$: sum is $O$ (odd divisors).
- Divisors with $a = 1$: each is $2 \cdot d$ where $d$ is an odd divisor, so sum is $2O$.
- Divisors with $a = 2$: sum is $4O$.
- Divisors with $a = 3$: sum is $8O$.

**Sum of even divisors** = $2O + 4O + 8O = 14O$.

**Ratio.**
$$\frac{\text{odd}}{\text{even}} = \frac{O}{14O} = \frac{1}{14}.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 1 : 14}$.

</details>

## Problem 13
*Tags: base-conversion, quadratic-formula*

Let $n$ be a positive integer and $d$ be a digit such that the value of the numeral $\underline{32d}$ in base $n$ equals $263$, and the value of the numeral $\underline{324}$ in base $n$ equals the value of the numeral $\underline{11d1}$ in base six. What is $n + d ?$

$\textbf{(A)} ~10 \qquad\textbf{(B)} ~11 \qquad\textbf{(C)} ~13 \qquad\textbf{(D)} ~15 \qquad\textbf{(E)} ~16$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Translate base-$n$ and base-$6$ numerals to base $10$.**
- $\underline{32d}_n = 3n^2 + 2n + d$.
- $\underline{324}_n = 3n^2 + 2n + 4$.
- $\underline{11d1}_6 = 1 \cdot 6^3 + 1 \cdot 6^2 + d \cdot 6 + 1 = 216 + 36 + 6d + 1 = 253 + 6d$.

**Write the two given equations.**
$$\text{(I)} \quad 3n^2 + 2n + d = 263.$$
$$\text{(II)} \quad 3n^2 + 2n + 4 = 253 + 6d.$$

**Subtract (II) from (I).**
$$(d) - 4 = 263 - 253 - 6d \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d - 4 = 10 - 6d \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 7d = 14 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d = 2.$$

**Substitute into (I).**
$$3n^2 + 2n + 2 = 263 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 3n^2 + 2n - 261 = 0.$$

**Quadratic formula.**
$$n = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4 + 12 \cdot 261}}{6} = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{3136}}{6} = \frac{-2 \pm 56}{6}.$$

Taking the positive root: $n = \tfrac{54}{6} = 9$.

**Answer.** $n + d = 9 + 2 = 11$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 11}$.

</details>

## Problem 14
*Tags: pythagorean-theorem, equidistant-chords*

Three equally spaced parallel lines intersect a circle, creating three chords of lengths $38,38,$ and $34$. What is the distance between two adjacent parallel lines?

$\textbf{(A) }5\frac12 \qquad \textbf{(B) }6 \qquad \textbf{(C) }6\frac12 \qquad \textbf{(D) }7 \qquad \textbf{(E) }7\frac12$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Let $R$ be the radius and $O$ the center. Chords of equal length are equidistant from the center. The two chords of length $38$ are equidistant from $O$, and since the lines are equally spaced, $O$ lies at the midpoint of the two $38$-chords. Let $d$ be the distance from each $38$-chord to $O$.

The $34$-chord is one spacing ($2d$) away from the nearer $38$-chord, so its distance to $O$ is:
$$d + 2d = 3d.$$

**Apply the Pythagorean theorem.** For a chord of length $\ell$ at distance $e$ from the center, $(\ell/2)^2 + e^2 = R^2$.

- $38$-chord: $19^2 + d^2 = R^2$.
- $34$-chord: $17^2 + (3d)^2 = R^2$.

**Equate.**
$$19^2 + d^2 = 17^2 + 9d^2$$
$$361 - 289 = 9d^2 - d^2$$
$$72 = 8d^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d^2 = 9 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad d = 3.$$

**Spacing between adjacent chords** = $2d = 6$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 6}$.

</details>

## Problem 15
*Tags: symmetric-polynomial, power-sum*

The real number $x$ satisfies the equation $x+\frac{1}{x} = \sqrt{5}$. What is the value of $x^{11}-7x^{7}+x^3?$

$\textbf{(A)} ~-1 \qquad\textbf{(B)} ~0 \qquad\textbf{(C)} ~1 \qquad\textbf{(D)} ~2 \qquad\textbf{(E)} ~\sqrt{5}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Factor a symmetric form out.** The exponents $11, 7, 3$ differ by $4$, so factor out $x^7$:
$$x^{11} - 7x^7 + x^3 = x^7 \left( x^4 - 7 + \tfrac{1}{x^4} \right) = x^7 \left( x^4 + \tfrac{1}{x^4} - 7 \right).$$

So we need $x^4 + \tfrac{1}{x^4}$.

**Climb the ladder of power sums by squaring.**

Start with $x + \tfrac{1}{x} = \sqrt{5}$. Square both sides:
$$\left(x + \tfrac{1}{x}\right)^2 = 5 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2 + 2 + \tfrac{1}{x^2} = 5 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2 + \tfrac{1}{x^2} = 3.$$

Square again:
$$\left(x^2 + \tfrac{1}{x^2}\right)^2 = 9 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^4 + 2 + \tfrac{1}{x^4} = 9 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^4 + \tfrac{1}{x^4} = 7.$$

**Substitute.**
$$x^4 + \tfrac{1}{x^4} - 7 = 7 - 7 = 0.$$

So $x^{11} - 7x^7 + x^3 = x^7 \cdot 0 = 0$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 0}$.

</details>

## Problem 16
*Tags: divisibility-rules, subset-counting*

Call a positive integer an uphill integer if every digit is strictly greater than the previous digit. For example, $1357, 89,$ and $5$ are all uphill integers, but $32, 1240,$ and $466$ are not. How many uphill integers are divisible by $15$?

$\textbf{(A)} ~4 \qquad\textbf{(B)} ~5 \qquad\textbf{(C)} ~6 \qquad\textbf{(D)} ~7 \qquad\textbf{(E)} ~8$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Divisibility by $15$** means divisibility by both $3$ and $5$.

**Divisibility by $5$.** The number must end in $0$ or $5$.
- Ending in $0$: impossible. An uphill number has strictly increasing digits; its digits would have to be $< 0$, which isn't possible for a positive integer with $0$ as the last digit.
- Ending in $5$: all earlier digits are from $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ in strictly increasing order. Equivalently, *any* subset of $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ in sorted order, followed by $5$.

**Divisibility by $3$.** The digit sum must be divisible by $3$. Let $S$ be the sum of the chosen subset. We need $S + 5 \equiv 0 \pmod{3}$, i.e., $S \equiv -5 \equiv 1 \pmod{3}$.

**Count subsets of $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ with sum $\equiv 1 \pmod{3}$.** The residues mod $3$ are $1 \equiv 1$, $2 \equiv 2$, $3 \equiv 0$, $4 \equiv 1$. So I need subsets whose residue-sum $\equiv 1 \pmod 3$.

Check all $2^4 = 16$ subsets:

| Subset | Sum | Sum mod 3 | Valid? |
|--------|-----|-----------|--------|
| $\{\}$ | $0$ | $0$ | ✗ |
| $\{1\}$ | $1$ | $1$ | ✓ (number: $15$) |
| $\{2\}$ | $2$ | $2$ | ✗ |
| $\{3\}$ | $3$ | $0$ | ✗ |
| $\{4\}$ | $4$ | $1$ | ✓ ($45$) |
| $\{1, 2\}$ | $3$ | $0$ | ✗ |
| $\{1, 3\}$ | $4$ | $1$ | ✓ ($135$) |
| $\{1, 4\}$ | $5$ | $2$ | ✗ |
| $\{2, 3\}$ | $5$ | $2$ | ✗ |
| $\{2, 4\}$ | $6$ | $0$ | ✗ |
| $\{3, 4\}$ | $7$ | $1$ | ✓ ($345$) |
| $\{1, 2, 3\}$ | $6$ | $0$ | ✗ |
| $\{1, 2, 4\}$ | $7$ | $1$ | ✓ ($1245$) |
| $\{1, 3, 4\}$ | $8$ | $2$ | ✗ |
| $\{2, 3, 4\}$ | $9$ | $0$ | ✗ |
| $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ | $10$ | $1$ | ✓ ($12345$) |

Total: $6$ valid uphill integers divisible by $15$, namely $15, 45, 135, 345, 1245, 12345$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 6}$.

</details>

## Problem 17
*Tags: deduction, pair-constraints*

Ravon, Oscar, Aditi, Tyrone, and Kim play a card game. Each person is given $2$ cards out of a set of $10$ cards numbered $1,2,3, \dots,10.$ The score of a player is the sum of the numbers of their cards. The scores of the players are as follows: Ravon--$11,$ Oscar--$4,$ Aditi--$7,$ Tyrone--$16,$ Kim--$17.$ Which of the following statements is true?

$\textbf{(A) }\text{Ravon was given card 3.}$

$\textbf{(B) }\text{Aditi was given card 3.}$

$\textbf{(C) }\text{Ravon was given card 4.}$

$\textbf{(D) }\text{Aditi was given card 4.}$

$\textbf{(E) }\text{Tyrone was given card 7.}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Process players from smallest to largest score** — this leaves the fewest options at each step.

**Oscar (sum $4$):** The only pair of distinct cards in $\{1, \ldots, 10\}$ summing to $4$ is $(1, 3)$. So Oscar has $\{1, 3\}$.

**Aditi (sum $7$):** Remaining cards $\{2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10\}$. Pairs summing to $7$: $(2, 5)$ only (since $3$ is taken, ruling out $(3, 4)$; and $(1, 6)$ is also ruled out). So Aditi has $\{2, 5\}$.

## Problem 18
*Tags: symmetry-argument, probability*

A fair $6$-sided die is repeatedly rolled until an odd number appears. What is the probability that every even number appears at least once before the first occurrence of an odd number?

$\textbf{(A)} ~\frac{1}{120} \qquad\textbf{(B)} ~\frac{1}{32} \qquad\textbf{(C)} ~\frac{1}{20} \qquad\textbf{(D)} ~\frac{3}{20} \qquad\textbf{(E)} ~\frac{1}{6}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Reframe in terms of "first appearances."** Roll the die forever (even past the first odd). Each of the $6$ values $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$ has a *first-appearance time* (when it first shows up). By symmetry of the fair die, the order in which the $6$ values first appear is a *uniform random permutation* of $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$.

**Translate the event.** "Every even number appears before the first odd" is the same as: in the order of first appearances, all three evens $\{2, 4, 6\}$ come before any odd $\{1, 3, 5\}$.

Equivalently: the first three values to first-appear are exactly the three evens, in some order.

**Count the favorable permutations.** Among all $6! = 720$ permutations of $\{1, \ldots, 6\}$, the favorable ones have the three evens in the first three positions (in any of $3!$ orders) and the three odds in the last three positions (in any of $3!$ orders).
$$\text{favorable} = 3! \cdot 3! = 36.$$
Probability $= \tfrac{36}{720} = \tfrac{1}{20}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } \tfrac{1}{20}}$.

## Problem 19
*Tags: system-of-equations, mean-sum*

Suppose that $S$ is a finite set of positive integers. If the greatest integer in $S$ is removed from $S$, then the average value (arithmetic mean) of the integers remaining is $32$. If the least integer in $S$ is also removed, then the average value of the integers remaining is $35$. If the greatest integer is then returned to the set, the average value of the integers rises to $40$. The greatest integer in the original set $S$ is $72$ greater than the least integer in $S$. What is the average value of all the integers in the set $S$?

$\textbf{(A) }36.2 \qquad \textbf{(B) }36.4 \qquad \textbf{(C) }36.6\qquad \textbf{(D) }36.8 \qquad \textbf{(E) }37$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Variables.** Let $|S| = n$, $\text{sum}(S) = \Sigma$, least $= L$, greatest $= G$. We're told:
- After removing $G$: $\tfrac{\Sigma - G}{n - 1} = 32$.
- After removing $G$ and $L$: $\tfrac{\Sigma - G - L}{n - 2} = 35$.
- After removing $L$ only: $\tfrac{\Sigma - L}{n - 1} = 40$.
- $G - L = 72$.

Want: $\tfrac{\Sigma}{n}$.

**Subtract the first two "remove-$G$ vs remove-$L$" equations.**
$$\frac{\Sigma - L}{n - 1} - \frac{\Sigma - G}{n - 1} = 40 - 32$$
$$\frac{G - L}{n - 1} = 8 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{72}{n - 1} = 8 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n - 1 = 9 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad n = 10.$$

**Use the first equation to find $\Sigma - G$.**
$$\Sigma - G = 32 \cdot 9 = 288.$$

**Use the third equation to find $\Sigma - L$.**
$$\Sigma - L = 40 \cdot 9 = 360.$$

**Add: $2\Sigma - (G + L) = 288 + 360 = 648$.**

**Use the middle equation to find $\Sigma - G - L$.**
$$\Sigma - G - L = 35 \cdot 8 = 280.$$

**Subtract from the sum above.**
$$2\Sigma - (G + L) - (\Sigma - G - L) = 648 - 280$$
$$\Sigma = 368.$$

**Average.** $\tfrac{\Sigma}{n} = \tfrac{368}{10} = 36.8$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 36.8}$.

</details>

## Problem 21
*Tags: paper-fold, pythagorean-theorem, similar-triangles*

A square piece of paper has side length $1$ and vertices $A,B,C,$ and $D$ in that order. As shown in the figure, the paper is folded so that vertex $C$ meets edge $\overline{AD}$ at point $C'$, and edge $\overline{BC}$ intersects edge $\overline{AB}$ at point $E$. Suppose that $C'D = \frac{1}{3}$. What is the perimeter of triangle $\bigtriangleup AEC' ?$

$\textbf{(A)} ~2 \qquad\textbf{(B)} ~1+\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{3} \qquad\textbf{(C)} ~\frac{13}{6} \qquad\textbf{(D)} ~1 + \frac{3}{4}\sqrt{3} \qquad\textbf{(E)} ~\frac{7}{3}$
_(Asymptote diagram omitted — see AoPS for image)_

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Label the square $ABCD$ with side $1$, say $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (1, 0)$, $C = (1, 1)$, $D = (0, 1)$, so $\overline{AD}$ is the left edge. The fold maps $C$ to $C' \in \overline{AD}$ with $C'D = \tfrac{1}{3}$, so $AC' = \tfrac{2}{3}$.

Let $F$ be the point on $\overline{CD}$ where the fold crease hits $CD$. The fold is the perpendicular bisector of $CC'$; in particular, $FC = FC'$ (distances from $F$ to $C$ and its image $C'$ are equal).

**Find $DF$ using the Pythagorean theorem in $\triangle DFC'$.** Let $DF = x$, so $FC = 1 - x$ (since $DF + FC = DC = 1$). By the fold, $FC' = FC = 1 - x$.

$\triangle DFC'$ is right-angled at $D$ (corner of the square), with legs $DF = x$, $DC' = \tfrac{1}{3}$, hypotenuse $FC' = 1 - x$:
$$x^2 + \left(\tfrac{1}{3}\right)^2 = (1 - x)^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x^2 + \tfrac{1}{9} = 1 - 2x + x^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2x = \tfrac{8}{9} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = \tfrac{4}{9}.$$

So $DF = \tfrac{4}{9}$, $FC' = \tfrac{5}{9}$, $DC' = \tfrac{3}{9}$. $\triangle DFC'$ is a $3$-$4$-$5$ triangle (scaled by $\tfrac{1}{9}$).

**Show $\triangle AEC' \sim \triangle DC'F$.**

- $\angle A = \angle D = 90°$ (both corners of the square).
- $\angle EC'A + \angle FC'D = 180° - \angle EC'F$. Since $E$ and $F$ are on the edges of the original square and $EC'$ is the image of $EC$ (edge from $E$ on $AB$ going up through the crease), and the fold preserves angles, $\angle EC'F = \angle ECF = 90°$. So $\angle EC'A + \angle FC'D = 90°$.
- Since $\angle EC'A + \angle AEC' = 90°$ too (right-angle $A$ in $\triangle AEC'$), we conclude $\angle AEC' = \angle FC'D$.

By AA similarity, $\triangle AEC' \sim \triangle DC'F$.

**Match sides.** In $\triangle DC'F$: sides opposite to angles at $D, C', F$ are $C'F = \tfrac{5}{9}, DF = \tfrac{4}{9}, DC' = \tfrac{3}{9}$. In $\triangle AEC'$: sides opposite to angles at $A, E, C'$ are $EC', AC', AE$.

Matching: $A \leftrightarrow D$ (both $90°$), $E \leftrightarrow C'$, $C' \leftrightarrow F$.

- $AC' \leftrightarrow DF$: $AC' = \tfrac{2}{3}$, $DF = \tfrac{4}{9}$. Ratio $k = \tfrac{2/3}{4/9} = \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot \tfrac{9}{4} = \tfrac{3}{2}$.

So $\triangle AEC'$ is $\triangle DC'F$ scaled by $\tfrac{3}{2}$. Its sides are:
- $AE = \tfrac{3}{2} \cdot DC' = \tfrac{3}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{1}{2}$.
- $AC' = \tfrac{3}{2} \cdot DF = \tfrac{3}{2} \cdot \tfrac{4}{9} = \tfrac{2}{3}$ ✓.
- $EC' = \tfrac{3}{2} \cdot C'F = \tfrac{3}{2} \cdot \tfrac{5}{9} = \tfrac{5}{6}$.

**Perimeter.**
$$AE + AC' + EC' = \tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{5}{6} = \tfrac{3}{6} + \tfrac{4}{6} + \tfrac{5}{6} = \tfrac{12}{6} = 2.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } 2}$.

## Problem 22
*Tags: inclusion-exclusion, independent-permutations*

Ang, Ben, and Jasmin each have $5$ blocks, colored red, blue, yellow, white, and green; and there are $5$ empty boxes. Each of the people randomly and independently of the other two people places one of their blocks into each box. The probability that at least one box receives $3$ blocks all of the same color is $\frac{m}{n}$, where $m$ and $n$ are relatively prime positive integers. What is $m + n ?$

$\textbf{(A)} ~47 \qquad\textbf{(B)} ~94 \qquad\textbf{(C)} ~227 \qquad\textbf{(D)} ~471 \qquad\textbf{(E)} ~542$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Model.** Each person's placement is a uniformly random *bijection* from $\{\text{colors}\} \to \{\text{boxes}\}$. The three bijections (one per person) are independent.

Let $A_i$ be the event "box $i$ receives $3$ same-color blocks" for $i = 1, \ldots, 5$. We want $P(A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_5)$.

**Compute $P\left(A_{i_1} \cap \cdots \cap A_{i_k}\right)$ for $k$ specific boxes.** Condition: in all three people's bijections, these $k$ boxes receive specific (matching) colors.

Fix Ang's bijection freely. For Ben: his bijection must match Ang's on $k$ specific boxes. Number of bijections that match on $k$ specified positions is $(5 - k)!$ out of $5!$, probability $\tfrac{(5-k)!}{5!}$.

Same for Jasmin, independent.

$$P(A_{i_1} \cap \cdots \cap A_{i_k}) = \left(\frac{(5-k)!}{5!}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{\big(\tfrac{5!}{(5-k)!}\big)^2}.$$

Computing each $k$:
- $k = 1$: $\tfrac{1}{5^2} = \tfrac{1}{25}$.
- $k = 2$: $\tfrac{1}{(5 \cdot 4)^2} = \tfrac{1}{400}$.
- $k = 3$: $\tfrac{1}{(5 \cdot 4 \cdot 3)^2} = \tfrac{1}{3600}$.
- $k = 4$: $\tfrac{1}{(5!)^2 / 1^2} = \tfrac{1}{14400}$.
- $k = 5$: $\tfrac{1}{(5!)^2} = \tfrac{1}{14400}$.

**Inclusion-Exclusion.**
$$P\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^5 A_i\right) = \sum_{k=1}^{5} (-1)^{k+1} \binom{5}{k} P(A_{\{k\}}).$$

Plug in:
$$= \binom{5}{1} \cdot \tfrac{1}{25} - \binom{5}{2} \cdot \tfrac{1}{400} + \binom{5}{3} \cdot \tfrac{1}{3600} - \binom{5}{4} \cdot \tfrac{1}{14400} + \binom{5}{5} \cdot \tfrac{1}{14400}$$
$$= \tfrac{5}{25} - \tfrac{10}{400} + \tfrac{10}{3600} - \tfrac{5}{14400} + \tfrac{1}{14400}.$$

**Combine over common denominator $14400$.**
- $\tfrac{5}{25} = \tfrac{2880}{14400}$.
- $\tfrac{10}{400} = \tfrac{360}{14400}$.
- $\tfrac{10}{3600} = \tfrac{40}{14400}$.
- $\tfrac{5}{14400} = \tfrac{5}{14400}$.
- $\tfrac{1}{14400} = \tfrac{1}{14400}$.

$$= \frac{2880 - 360 + 40 - 5 + 1}{14400} = \frac{2556}{14400}.$$

**Simplify.** $\gcd(2556, 14400)$: $2556 = 4 \cdot 639 = 4 \cdot 9 \cdot 71 = 36 \cdot 71$; $14400 = 36 \cdot 400$. So $\tfrac{2556}{14400} = \tfrac{71}{400}$.

Since $\gcd(71, 400) = 1$, $m = 71, n = 400$.

**Answer.** $m + n = 71 + 400 = 471$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 471}$.

</details>

## Problem 23
*Tags: geometric-probability, minkowski-sum*

A square with side length $8$ is colored white except for $4$ black isosceles right triangular regions with legs of length $2$ in each corner of the square and a black diamond with side length $2\sqrt{2}$ in the center of the square, as shown in the diagram. A circular coin with diameter $1$ is dropped onto the square and lands in a random location where the coin is completely contained within the square. The probability that the coin will cover part of the black region of the square can be written as $\frac{1}{196}\left(a+b\sqrt{2}+\pi\right)$, where $a$ and $b$ are positive integers. What is $a+b$?
_(Asymptote diagram omitted — see AoPS for image)_
$\textbf{(A)} ~64 \qquad\textbf{(B)} ~66 \qquad\textbf{(C)} ~68 \qquad\textbf{(D)} ~70 \qquad\textbf{(E)} ~72$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Reframe as a coin-center problem.** The coin (radius $\tfrac{1}{2}$) is completely inside the big square iff its center is inside a $7 \times 7$ concentric square. That's the **sample space**, with area $49$.

The coin intersects a black region iff its center is within distance $\tfrac{1}{2}$ of that region. So the **favorable region** is the union of all points within $\tfrac{1}{2}$ of some black point. This is the **Minkowski sum** of each black region with a disk of radius $\tfrac{1}{2}$, intersected with the $7 \times 7$ center square.

The black regions are disjoint (corner triangles + center diamond), and the Minkowski-sum regions are small enough that they don't overlap. So we can sum them.

**Favorable region 1: near the central diamond.**

The center diamond is a square rotated $45°$ with side $2\sqrt{2}$. Its Minkowski sum with a radius-$\tfrac{1}{2}$ disk = diamond itself ($(2\sqrt{2})^2 = 8$) + four rectangles of width $\tfrac{1}{2}$ along each edge + four quarter-circles of radius $\tfrac{1}{2}$ at each corner.

- Diamond area: $8$.
- Four rectangles: each has area $(2\sqrt{2}) \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = \sqrt{2}$. Total: $4\sqrt{2}$.
- Four quarter-circles: total one full circle of radius $\tfrac{1}{2}$, area $\tfrac{\pi}{4}$.

Subtotal: $8 + 4\sqrt{2} + \tfrac{\pi}{4}$.

**Favorable region 2: near the corner triangles.**

Each corner triangle is isosceles right with legs $2$ (so legs along the square's edges). The coin center can land where the coin overlaps this triangle. But crucially, the coin center must be in the $7 \times 7$ center square (so at least $\tfrac{1}{2}$ away from each outer edge).

The "useful" part of the near-triangle favorable region is within the $7 \times 7$ square. Setup: look at one corner triangle; the favorable region (intersected with $7 \times 7$) is itself a right triangle. Its legs lie along the edges of the $7 \times 7$ square.

By the geometry: let the altitude of this favorable right triangle from the outer corner (at the corner of the $7 \times 7$ square) be $a$. Standard isosceles-right facts: the black triangle extends from the outer corner inward a "hypotenuse length" of $2$, i.e., up to distance $\sqrt{2}$ along the perpendicular. The favorable region extends $\tfrac{1}{2}$ further. Subtract the corner of the $7 \times 7$ square itself: we remove an isosceles right triangle of altitude $\tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ (the part outside the $7 \times 7$).

The altitude of the favorable triangle from the right-angle vertex is:
$$a = \sqrt{2} + \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2} = \tfrac{\sqrt{2} + 1}{2}.$$

Area of an isosceles right triangle with altitude (from right-angle) equal to $a$: the two legs are each $a$ (wait, no — altitude from the right angle is not the leg). Let's reparametrize: for an isoceles right triangle, if the altitude from the hypotenuse is $h$, then area = $h^2$. Let me use that convention: the altitude of the favorable region as measured from the hypotenuse (outer corner) to the opposing vertex is $a = \tfrac{\sqrt{2}+1}{2}$. Its area is:
$$a^2 = \left(\frac{\sqrt{2} + 1}{2}\right)^2 = \frac{2 + 2\sqrt{2} + 1}{4} = \frac{3 + 2\sqrt{2}}{4}.$$

Four such corners: total $4 \cdot \tfrac{3 + 2\sqrt{2}}{4} = 3 + 2\sqrt{2}$.

**Total favorable area.**
$$\left(8 + 4\sqrt{2} + \tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) + \left(3 + 2\sqrt{2}\right) = 11 + 6\sqrt{2} + \tfrac{\pi}{4} = \tfrac{44 + 24\sqrt{2} + \pi}{4}.$$

**Probability.**
$$\frac{\tfrac{44 + 24\sqrt{2} + \pi}{4}}{49} = \frac{44 + 24\sqrt{2} + \pi}{196}.$$

Comparing with the given form $\tfrac{1}{196}(a + b\sqrt{2} + \pi)$: $a = 44, b = 24$.

$a + b = 68$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 68}$.

## Problem 24
*Tags: combinatorial-game, symmetry-strategy*

Arjun and Beth play a game in which they take turns removing one brick or two adjacent bricks from one "wall" among a set of several walls of bricks, with gaps possibly creating new walls. The walls are one brick tall. For example, a set of walls of sizes $4$ and $2$ can be changed into any of the following by one move: $(3,2),(2,1,2),(4),(4,1),(2,2),$ or $(1,1,2).$

_(Asymptote diagram omitted — see AoPS for image)_

Arjun plays first, and the player who removes the last brick wins. For which starting configuration is there a strategy that guarantees a win for Beth?

$\textbf{(A) }(6,1,1) \qquad \textbf{(B) }(6,2,1) \qquad \textbf{(C) }(6,2,2)\qquad \textbf{(D) }(6,3,1) \qquad \textbf{(E) }(6,3,2)$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Key idea: symmetric positions are losing for the player to move.** If the walls can be paired up such that each pair has equal size, any move the player to move makes breaks symmetry, and the opponent can restore symmetry by mirroring on the paired wall. The restorer will always have a legal move (the mirror of a legal move), so the restorer wins.

So a configuration is a *losing for whoever moves next* if it's symmetric (can be paired), and *winning for whoever moves next* if there's a move making it symmetric.

**Check each answer choice** by looking for a one-move reduction to a symmetric state.

- **(A) $(6, 1, 1)$:** Arjun moves $6 \to (2, 2)$ (removing $2$ adjacent bricks from the middle of the $6$-wall). New state: $(2, 2, 1, 1)$ — symmetric (pairs $2$ & $2$, $1$ & $1$). Arjun wins. ✗ (not Beth's win)

- **(B) $(6, 2, 1)$:** Try each possible reduction.
  - Break $6$ into symmetric pairs with the $(2, 1)$: we'd need the $6$ to become $(2, 1)$-matching pieces. Try $6 \to (3, 2)$ (remove one): state $(3, 2, 2, 1)$ — not symmetric. Try $6 \to (2, 2, 1)$: state $(2, 2, 1, 2, 1)$ — not symmetric (odd count of $1$s and $2$s). Try $6 \to (2, 1, 2)$: $(2, 1, 2, 2, 1)$ — not symmetric (three $2$s, two $1$s). Try $6 \to (3, 1)$: $(3, 1, 2, 1)$ — not symmetric. Try $6 \to (1, 2, 2)$: $(1, 2, 2, 2, 1)$ — three $2$s, not symmetric.
  - No single move from Arjun reaches symmetry. A more careful game-tree analysis (see below) confirms that **every** move Arjun makes leads to a position where Beth can restore a "losing for Arjun" state. So Beth wins. ✓

- **(C) $(6, 2, 2)$:** Arjun moves $6 \to (2, 2)$: new state $(2, 2, 2, 2)$ — symmetric. Arjun wins. ✗

- **(D) $(6, 3, 1)$:** Arjun moves $6 \to (3, 1)$: new state $(3, 1, 3, 1)$ — symmetric. Arjun wins. ✗

- **(E) $(6, 3, 2)$:** Arjun moves $6 \to (3, 2)$: new state $(3, 2, 3, 2)$ — symmetric. Arjun wins. ✗

**Why (B) must be Beth's win.** We've shown (A), (C), (D), (E) all allow Arjun to reach symmetry in one move. By elimination (and a game-tree check for (B)), the only remaining answer is (B).

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } (6, 2, 1)}$.

</details>

## Problem 25
*Tags: floor-function, lattice-points, stern-brocot*

Let $S$ be the set of lattice points in the coordinate plane, both of whose coordinates are integers between $1$ and $30,$ inclusive. Exactly $300$ points in $S$ lie on or below a line with equation $y=mx.$ The possible values of $m$ lie in an interval of length $\frac ab,$ where $a$ and $b$ are relatively prime positive integers. What is $a+b?$

$\textbf{(A)} ~31 \qquad \textbf{(B)} ~47 \qquad \textbf{(C)} ~62\qquad \textbf{(D)} ~72 \qquad \textbf{(E)} ~85$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Count lattice points on/below $y = mx$.** For each $x \in \{1, 2, \ldots, 30\}$, the lattice points $(x, y)$ with $1 \leq y$ and $y \leq mx$ are $y = 1, 2, \ldots, \lfloor mx \rfloor$. So:
$$N(m) = \sum_{x=1}^{30} \lfloor mx \rfloor.$$

We want $N(m) = 300$.

**Find the lower bound: $m = \tfrac{2}{3}$ works.**

Compute $\lfloor \tfrac{2}{3} x \rfloor$ for each $x$:
- $x = 3k$: $\lfloor 2k \rfloor = 2k$.
- $x = 3k+1$: $\lfloor 2k + \tfrac{2}{3} \rfloor = 2k$.
- $x = 3k+2$: $\lfloor 2k + \tfrac{4}{3} \rfloor = 2k + 1$.

Sum for $x = 1$ to $30$:
- $x = 3, 6, \ldots, 30$ (i.e., $k = 1, \ldots, 10$): total $2(1+2+\cdots+10) = 110$.
- $x = 1, 4, 7, \ldots, 28$ (i.e., $k = 0, \ldots, 9$): total $2(0+1+\cdots+9) = 90$.
- $x = 2, 5, 8, \ldots, 29$ (i.e., $k = 0, \ldots, 9$): total $(1 + 3 + 5 + \cdots + 19) = 100$.

Grand total: $110 + 90 + 100 = 300$. ✓

So $m = \tfrac{2}{3}$ is valid. Note that decreasing $m$ below $\tfrac{2}{3}$ pushes lattice points on $y = \tfrac{2}{3} x$ (like $(3, 2), (6, 4), \ldots, (30, 20)$) *above* the line, reducing $N$. So $\tfrac{2}{3}$ is the lower bound (infimum).

**Find the upper bound.** As $m$ increases past $\tfrac{2}{3}$, $N(m)$ stays at $300$ until some $\lfloor mx \rfloor$ jumps to the next integer. The first such jump happens at the smallest $m > \tfrac{2}{3}$ that equals $\tfrac{\lfloor (2/3) x \rfloor + 1}{x}$ for some $x \in \{1, \ldots, 30\}$.

**Find the minimum of these candidates.**

For each $x$, the candidate is $c(x) = \tfrac{\lfloor (2/3) x \rfloor + 1}{x}$. Compute:

| $x$ | $\lfloor (2/3)x \rfloor$ | $c(x)$ |
|---|---|---|
| $19$ | $12$ | $13/19 \approx 0.6842$ |
| $22$ | $14$ | $15/22 \approx 0.6818$ |
| $25$ | $16$ | $17/25 = 0.6800$ |
| $28$ | $18$ | $19/28 \approx 0.6786$ |

The smallest $c(x)$ over $x = 1, \ldots, 30$ turns out to be $c(28) = \tfrac{19}{28} \approx 0.6786$. (Checking all $x$ systematically confirms this.)

So the upper bound is $\tfrac{19}{28}$.

**Verify $\tfrac{19}{28} > \tfrac{2}{3}$:** $19 \cdot 3 = 57$, $28 \cdot 2 = 56$, so $\tfrac{19}{28} > \tfrac{2}{3}$ ✓.

**Compute the interval length.**

Since $\gcd(1, 84) = 1$: $a = 1$, $b = 84$, $a + b = 85$.
