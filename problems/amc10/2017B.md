## Problem 1
*Tags: digit-manipulation, working-backwards*

Mary thought of a positive two-digit number. She multiplied it by $3$ and added $11$. Then she switched the digits of the result, obtaining a number between $71$ and $75$, inclusive. What was Mary's number?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 11\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 12\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 13\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 14\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 15$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Work backwards from the digit-swapped result.**

The result after digit-swap is in $\{71, 72, 73, 74, 75\}$. Unswap each:
- $71 \to 17$
- $72 \to 27$
- $73 \to 37$
- $74 \to 47$
- $75 \to 57$

**Subtract $11$ and divide by $3$.** The pre-swap value is Mary's number $\times 3 + 11$. Subtract $11$:
- $17 - 11 = 6, \div 3 = 2$. Not two-digit.
- $27 - 11 = 16, \div 3$: not integer.
- $37 - 11 = 26, \div 3$: not integer.
- $47 - 11 = 36, \div 3 = 12$. Two-digit ✓.
- $57 - 11 = 46, \div 3$: not integer.

So Mary's number is $12$.

**Verify.** $12 \cdot 3 + 11 = 47$. Swap digits: $74$. In $[71, 75]$ ✓.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 12}$.

</details>

## Problem 2
*Tags: rate-time, unit-conversion*

Sofia ran $5$ laps around the $400$-meter track at her school. For each lap, she ran the first $100$ meters at an average speed of $4$ meters per second and the remaining $300$ meters at an average speed of $5$ meters per second. How much time did Sofia take running the $5$ laps?

$\qquad\textbf{(A)}\ \text{5 minutes and 35 seconds}$ <br>
$\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \text{6 minutes and 40 seconds}$ <br>
$\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \text{7 minutes and 5 seconds}$ <br>
$\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \text{7 minutes and 25 seconds}$ <br>
$\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \text{8 minutes and 10 seconds}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Time per lap.**
- First $100$ m at $4$ m/s: $100/4 = 25$ s.
- Remaining $300$ m at $5$ m/s: $300/5 = 60$ s.
- Total per lap: $25 + 60 = 85$ s.

**Total for $5$ laps.** $5 \cdot 85 = 425$ s $= 7$ min $5$ s.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C)}}$.

</details>

## Problem 3
*Tags: inequalities, positive-expression*

Real numbers $x$, $y$, and $z$ satisfy the inequalities
$0<x<1$, $-1<y<0$, and $1<z<2$.
Which of the following numbers is necessarily positive?

$\textbf{(A)}\ y+x^2\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ y+xz\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ y+y^2\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ y+2y^2\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ y+z$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Check (E): $y + z$.** $y > -1$ and $z > 1$, so $y + z > -1 + 1 = 0$. **Always positive.** ✓

**Rule out others with counterexamples.**
- (A) $y + x^2$: take $x = 0.1, y = -0.5$. $y + x^2 = -0.49 < 0$.
- (B) $y + xz$: take $x = 0.1, y = -0.5, z = 1.1$. $y + xz = -0.5 + 0.11 = -0.39 < 0$.
- (C) $y + y^2$: for $-1 < y < 0$, $y^2 < |y|$, so $y + y^2 < 0$.
- (D) $y + 2y^2$: at $y = -0.3$: $-0.3 + 0.18 = -0.12 < 0$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } y + z}$.

</details>

## Problem 4
*Tags: substitution, rational-equation*

Supposed that $x$ and $y$ are nonzero real numbers such that $\frac{3x+y}{x-3y}=-2$. What is the value of $\frac{x+3y}{3x-y}$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ -3\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ -1\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 1\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 2\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 3$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**From the given equation.** $\tfrac{3x + y}{x - 3y} = -2$, so
$$3x + y = -2(x - 3y) = -2x + 6y,$$
giving $5x = 5y$, so $x = y$.

**Substitute $x = y$ into the target.**
$$\frac{x + 3y}{3x - y} = \frac{y + 3y}{3y - y} = \frac{4y}{2y} = 2.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 2}$.

</details>

## Problem 5
*Tags: system-of-equations, word-problem*

Camilla had twice as many blueberry jelly beans as cherry jelly beans. After eating 10 pieces of each kind, she now has three times as many blueberry jelly beans as cherry jelly beans. How many blueberry jelly beans did she originally have?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 10\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 20\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 30\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 40\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 50$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Let $c$ = original cherry jelly beans, $b$ = original blueberry.**

Original ratio: $b = 2c$.

After eating $10$ of each: $(b - 10) = 3(c - 10)$, so $2c - 10 = 3c - 30$, giving $c = 20$.

Then $b = 2 \cdot 20 = 40$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 40}$.

</details>

## Problem 6
*Tags: volume, box-packing*

What is the largest number of solid $2\text{-in} \times 2\text{-in} \times 1\text{-in}$ blocks that can fit in a $3\text{-in} \times 2\text{-in}\times3\text{-in}$ box?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 3\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 4\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 5\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 6\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 7$

Consider the box's $3 \times 2 \times 3$ dimensions: stack $2$ blocks of $2 \times 2 \times 1$ oriented with "thin" dimension along the height (fills $2 \times 2 \times 2$), then $2$ more similarly stacked, etc. Direct construction shows $4$ fits.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 4}$.

</details>

## Problem 7
*Tags: rate-time, harmonic-mean*

Samia set off on her bicycle to visit her friend, traveling at an average speed of $17$ kilometers per hour. When she had gone half the distance to her friend's house, a tire went flat, and she walked the rest of the way at $5$ kilometers per hour. In all it took her $44$ minutes to reach her friend's house. In kilometers rounded to the nearest tenth, how far did Samia walk?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 2.0\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 2.2\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 2.8\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 3.4\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 4.4$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Let the total distance = $2d$, so she bikes $d$ km at $17$ km/h and walks $d$ km at $5$ km/h.

**Total time.** $\tfrac{d}{17} + \tfrac{d}{5} = d \cdot \tfrac{5 + 17}{85} = \tfrac{22 d}{85}$ hours.

**Set equal to $44$ minutes $= \tfrac{44}{60} = \tfrac{11}{15}$ hours.**
$$\tfrac{22 d}{85} = \tfrac{11}{15}.$$
$$d = \tfrac{11}{15} \cdot \tfrac{85}{22} = \tfrac{11 \cdot 85}{15 \cdot 22} = \tfrac{85}{30} = \tfrac{17}{6}.$$

**Distance walked** $= d = \tfrac{17}{6} \approx 2.833$, rounded to $2.8$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 2.8}$.

</details>

## Problem 8
*Tags: reflection, coordinates, isosceles-triangle*

Points $A(11, 9)$ and $B(2, -3)$ are vertices of $\triangle ABC$ with $AB=AC$. The altitude from $A$ meets the opposite side at $D(-1, 3)$. What are the coordinates of point $C$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ (-8, 9)\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ (-4, 8)\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ (-4, 9)\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ (-2, 3)\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ (-1, 0)$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Since $AB = AC$, $\triangle ABC$ is isosceles with apex $A$.** The altitude from $A$ to $\overline{BC}$ meets $BC$ at its midpoint. So $D$ is the midpoint of $\overline{BC}$.

**Midpoint formula.** $D = \tfrac{B + C}{2}$, so $C = 2D - B$:
$$C = 2(-1, 3) - (2, -3) = (-2 - 2, 6 - (-3)) = (-4, 9).$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } (-4, 9)}$.

</details>

## Problem 9
*Tags: probability, casework*

A radio program has a quiz consisting of $3$ multiple-choice questions, each with $3$ choices. A contestant wins if he or she gets $2$ or more of the questions right. The contestant answers randomly to each question. What is the probability of winning?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \frac{1}{27}\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \frac{1}{9}\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \frac{2}{9}\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \frac{7}{27}\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \frac{1}{2}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Single question.** Probability correct: $\tfrac{1}{3}$; wrong: $\tfrac{2}{3}$.

**Winning condition.** $\geq 2$ correct out of $3$.

**Binomial probability.**
- Exactly $2$ correct: $\binom{3}{2} (1/3)^2 (2/3) = 3 \cdot \tfrac{2}{27} = \tfrac{6}{27}$.
- Exactly $3$ correct: $(1/3)^3 = \tfrac{1}{27}$.

**Sum.** $\tfrac{6}{27} + \tfrac{1}{27} = \tfrac{7}{27}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } \tfrac{7}{27}}$.

</details>

## Problem 10
*Tags: perpendicular-lines, system-of-equations*

The lines with equations $ax-2y=c$ and $2x+by=-c$ are perpendicular and intersect at $(1, -5)$. What is $c$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ -13\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ -8\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 2\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 8\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 13$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Slopes.** From $ax - 2y = c$: $y = \tfrac{a}{2} x - \tfrac{c}{2}$, slope $\tfrac{a}{2}$. From $2x + by = -c$: $y = -\tfrac{2}{b} x - \tfrac{c}{b}$, slope $-\tfrac{2}{b}$.

**Perpendicular** means slopes multiply to $-1$:
$$\frac{a}{2} \cdot \left(-\frac{2}{b}\right) = -1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{a}{b} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a = b.$$

**Both pass through $(1, -5)$.**
- $a(1) - 2(-5) = c$: $a + 10 = c$, so $a = c - 10$.
- $2(1) + b(-5) = -c$: $2 - 5b = -c$, so $5b = c + 2$, $b = \tfrac{c + 2}{5}$.

**Combined with $a = b$.** $c - 10 = \tfrac{c + 2}{5}$. Multiply: $5c - 50 = c + 2$, $4c = 52$, $c = 13$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } 13}$.

</details>

## Problem 11
*Tags: conditional-probability, bayes*

At Typico High School, $60\%$ of the students like dancing, and the rest dislike it. Of those who like dancing, $80\%$ say that they like it, and the rest say that they dislike it. Of those who dislike dancing, $90\%$ say that they dislike it, and the rest say that they like it. What fraction of students who say they dislike dancing actually like it?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 10\%\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 12\%\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 20\%\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 25\%\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 33\frac{1}{3}\%$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Assume $100$ students.**

- Like dancing: $60$. Of these, $80\% = 48$ say "like"; $20\% = 12$ say "dislike."
- Dislike dancing: $40$. Of these, $90\% = 36$ say "dislike"; $10\% = 4$ say "like."

**Total who say "dislike":** $12 + 36 = 48$. Of these, $12$ actually like dancing.

**Fraction.** $12/48 = 1/4 = 25\%$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 25\%}$.

</details>

## Problem 12
*Tags: percentages, rate*

Elmer's new car gives $50\%$ percent better fuel efficiency, measured in kilometers per liter, than his old car. However, his new car uses diesel fuel, which is $20\%$ more expensive per liter than the gasoline his old car used. By what percent will Elmer save money if he uses his new car instead of his old car for a long trip?

$\textbf{(A) } 20\% \qquad \textbf{(B) } 26\tfrac23\% \qquad \textbf{(C) } 27\tfrac79\% \qquad \textbf{(D) } 33\tfrac13\% \qquad \textbf{(E) } 66\tfrac23\%$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup with WLOG values.** Say old car: $10$ km/L, $\$1$/L of gas. New car: $15$ km/L ($50\%$ better), $\$1.20$/L of diesel ($20\%$ more).

**Cost per km.**
- Old: $\tfrac{\$1}{10} = \$0.10$/km.
- New: $\tfrac{\$1.20}{15} = \$0.08$/km.

**Savings ratio.** $\tfrac{0.10 - 0.08}{0.10} = \tfrac{0.02}{0.10} = 20\%$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } 20\%}$.

</details>

## Problem 13
*Tags: inclusion-exclusion, set-counting*

There are $20$ students participating in an after-school program offering classes in yoga, bridge, and painting. Each student must take at least one of these three classes, but may take two or all three. There are $10$ students taking yoga, $13$ taking bridge, and $9$ taking painting. There are $9$ students taking at least two classes. How many students are taking all three classes?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 1\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 2\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 3\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 4\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 5$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Classify students by how many classes they take.** Let:
- $a$ = students in exactly $1$ class.
- $b$ = students in exactly $2$ classes.
- $c$ = students in all $3$ classes.

**Equations.**
- $a + b + c = 20$ (total students).
- $b + c = 9$ ("at least $2$").
- Sum of class sizes: each student counted once per class: $a + 2b + 3c = 10 + 13 + 9 = 32$.

**Solve.** From $a + b + c = 20$ and $a + 2b + 3c = 32$: subtract to get $b + 2c = 12$. Combined with $b + c = 9$: $c = 3$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 3}$.

</details>

## Problem 14
*Tags: fermats-little-theorem, modular-arithmetic*

An integer $N$ is selected at random in the range $1\leq N \leq 2020$ . What is the probability that the remainder when $N^{16}$ is divided by $5$ is $1$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \frac{1}{5}\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \frac{2}{5}\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \frac{3}{5}\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \frac{4}{5}\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 1$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Fermat's Little Theorem.** For prime $p = 5$ and $\gcd(N, 5) = 1$: $N^4 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$.

So $N^{16} = (N^4)^4 \equiv 1^4 \equiv 1 \pmod 5$ whenever $5 \nmid N$.

**If $5 \mid N$:** $N \equiv 0 \pmod 5$, so $N^{16} \equiv 0 \pmod 5$, not $1$.

**Count.** Out of $2020$ values, the multiples of $5$ are $5, 10, \ldots, 2020$: that's $404$ values. The rest ($2020 - 404 = 1616$) have $N^{16} \equiv 1 \pmod 5$.

**Probability.** $\tfrac{1616}{2020} = \tfrac{4}{5}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } \tfrac{4}{5}}$.

</details>

## Problem 15
*Tags: similar-triangles, altitude-on-hypotenuse*

Rectangle $ABCD$ has $AB=3$ and $BC=4$. Point $E$ is the foot of the perpendicular from $B$ to diagonal $\overline{AC}$. What is the area of $\triangle AED$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 1\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \frac{42}{25}\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \frac{28}{15}\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 2\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \frac{54}{25}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Coordinates.** $A = (0, 0), B = (3, 0), C = (3, 4), D = (0, 4)$.

**Diagonal $AC$.** From $(0,0)$ to $(3, 4)$, length $5$.

**Foot of perpendicular from $B$ to $AC$.** Line $AC$: direction $(3, 4)/5 = (0.6, 0.8)$. Project $\vec{AB} = (3, 0)$ onto this:
$$\vec{AE} = (\vec{AB} \cdot \hat{AC}) \hat{AC} = (3 \cdot 0.6 + 0 \cdot 0.8)(0.6, 0.8) = 1.8 \cdot (0.6, 0.8) = (1.08, 1.44).$$

So $E = (1.08, 1.44)$ or $(27/25, 36/25)$.

**Area of $\triangle AED$.** Vertices $A = (0,0), E = (27/25, 36/25), D = (0, 4)$. Using the shoelace formula:
$$\text{Area} = \tfrac{1}{2} |x_A (y_E - y_D) + x_E (y_D - y_A) + x_D (y_A - y_E)|.$$
$$= \tfrac{1}{2} |0 + \tfrac{27}{25}(4 - 0) + 0| = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{108}{25} = \tfrac{54}{25}.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } \tfrac{54}{25}}$.

</details>

## Problem 16
*Tags: complementary-counting, digits*

How many of the base-ten numerals for the positive integers less than or equal to $2017$ contain the digit $0$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 469\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 471\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 475\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 478\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 481$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Complementary counting.** Count positive integers $\leq 2017$ with NO digit $0$, then subtract from $2017$.

**By number of digits.**
- $1$-digit ($1$-$9$): no $0$s. $9$ numbers.
- $2$-digit ($10$-$99$): each digit $\in \{1, \ldots, 9\}$. $9 \cdot 9 = 81$.
- $3$-digit ($100$-$999$): $9^3 = 729$.
- $4$-digit ($1000$-$2017$): carefully count with no $0$. Thousands digit $= 1$ (since $\leq 2017$ and $2000$-$2017$ all have $0$ in hundreds place, so thousands digit must be $1$). Remaining three digits in $\{1, \ldots, 9\}$: $9^3 = 729$.

Total no-zero: $9 + 81 + 729 + 729 = 1548$.

**At-least-one-zero count.** $2017 - 1548 = 469$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } 469}$.

</details>

## Problem 17
*Tags: subsets, monotonic-sequences*

Call a positive integer **monotonous** if it is a one-digit number or its digits, when read from left to right, form either a strictly increasing or a strictly decreasing sequence. For example, $3$, $23578$, and $987620$ are monotonous, but $88$, $7434$, and $23557$ are not. How many monotonous positive integers are there?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 1024\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 1524\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 1533\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 1536\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 2048$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Key idea.** A strictly increasing (or decreasing) sequence of digits is determined by the set of digits used — once chosen, the order is forced.

**Increasing monotonous numbers.** Digits chosen from $\{1, 2, \ldots, 9\}$ (can't start with $0$). Any nonempty subset gives a unique increasing number. Count: $2^9 - 1 = 511$.

**Decreasing monotonous numbers.** Digits chosen from $\{0, 1, \ldots, 9\}$ (can end with $0$, but leading digit can't be $0$ — leading digit is the largest, so any nonempty subset with at least one nonzero digit works). Subsets of $\{0, \ldots, 9\}$: $2^{10}$. Exclude empty set and the subset $\{0\}$ (which would give the "number" $0$, not positive). Count: $2^{10} - 2 = 1022$.

**Overlap: single-digit numbers.** Single-digit numbers ($1$-$9$) are both "increasing" and "decreasing" (vacuously). We double-counted these $9$.

**Total.** $511 + 1022 - 9 = 1524$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 1524}$.

</details>

In the figure below, $3$ of the $6$ disks are to be painted blue, $2$ are to be painted red, and $1$ is to be painted green. Two paintings that can be obtained from one another by a rotation or a reflection of the entire figure are considered the same. How many different paintings are possible?

## Problem 19
*Tags: equilateral-triangle, law-of-cosines*

Let $ABC$ be an equilateral triangle. Extend side $\overline{AB}$ beyond $B$ to a point $B'$ so that $BB'=3 \cdot AB$. Similarly, extend side $\overline{BC}$ beyond $C$ to a point $C'$ so that $CC'=3 \cdot BC$, and extend side $\overline{CA}$ beyond $A$ to a point $A'$ so that $AA'=3 \cdot CA$. What is the ratio of the area of $\triangle A'B'C'$ to the area of $\triangle ABC$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 9:1\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 16:1\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 25:1\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 36:1\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 37:1$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Let $AB = BC = CA = 1$ (unit equilateral). Extensions: $BB' = 3, CC' = 3, AA' = 3$.

**By symmetry, $\triangle A'B'C'$ is equilateral.** Compute $A'B'$.

**In the configuration,** $B' = B + 3 \vec{AB}$ direction, and $A' = A + 3 \vec{CA}$ direction, i.e., $A'$ is on the line $CA$ extended beyond $A$.

**Use Law of Cosines on $\triangle B B' A'$ (or equivalently compute $A'B'$ directly).** Consider triangle with vertex $B'$ (on the extension of $AB$ beyond $B$) and $A'$ (on the extension of $CA$ beyond $A$).

**Alternative: use the identity for equilateral $\triangle ABC$ with such extensions.** A standard result: if each side is extended to $4 \times$ its length in the same rotational direction, the outer triangle has area $= 37 \times$ inner.

More concretely: Using the sides of length $1$ (original) and extensions of $3$, apply the Law of Cosines in $\triangle AB'A'$: the vertex is at $A$, with $AB' = AB + BB' = 4$ (along $\vec{AB}$) and $AA' = 3$ (along $\vec{AC}$ reversed... actually, $AA' = 3$ is along the extension of $CA$ beyond $A$, so it's opposite to $\vec{AC}$).

Angle at $A$ between $\vec{AB'}$ (along $\vec{AB}$) and $\vec{AA'}$ (opposite to $\vec{AC}$): this is $180° - 60° = 120°$.

Law of Cosines: $(A'B')^2 = AB'^2 + AA'^2 - 2 \cdot AB' \cdot AA' \cos(120°) = 16 + 9 - 2 \cdot 4 \cdot 3 \cdot (-\tfrac{1}{2}) = 25 + 12 = 37$.

So $A'B' = \sqrt{37}$.

**Area ratio.** $\left(\tfrac{A'B'}{AB}\right)^2 = 37$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } 37 : 1}$.

</details>

## Problem 20
*Tags: legendres-formula, odd-divisors*

The number $21!=51,090,942,171,709,440,000$ has over $60,000$ positive integer divisors. One of them is chosen at random. What is the probability that it is odd?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \frac{1}{21}\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \frac{1}{19}\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \frac{1}{18}\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \frac{1}{2}\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \frac{11}{21}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Compute power of $2$ in $21!$ (Legendre's formula).**
$$v_2(21!) = \lfloor 21/2 \rfloor + \lfloor 21/4 \rfloor + \lfloor 21/8 \rfloor + \lfloor 21/16 \rfloor = 10 + 5 + 2 + 1 = 18.$$

So $21! = 2^{18} \cdot M$ where $M$ is the odd part.

**Total divisors.** If $M = p_1^{e_1} \cdots p_k^{e_k}$, then divisors of $21!$: $(18 + 1) \cdot \prod (e_i + 1) = 19 \cdot \tau(M)$.

**Odd divisors.** Divisors with no factor of $2$: $\tau(M)$.

**Probability.** $\tfrac{\tau(M)}{19 \tau(M)} = \tfrac{1}{19}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } \tfrac{1}{19}}$.

</details>

## Problem 21
*Tags: inradius, median-triangle*

In $\triangle ABC$, $AB=6$, $AC=8$, $BC=10$, and $D$ is the midpoint of $\overline{BC}$. What is the sum of the radii of the circles inscribed in $\triangle ADB$ and $\triangle ADC$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \sqrt{5}\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \frac{11}{4}\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 2\sqrt{2}\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \frac{17}{6}\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 3$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Recognize $\triangle ABC$.** $6^2 + 8^2 = 36 + 64 = 100 = 10^2$, so $\triangle ABC$ is right-angled at $A$.

**Properties of the median to the hypotenuse.** In a right triangle, the median to the hypotenuse equals half the hypotenuse: $AD = BC/2 = 5$.

Also, $BD = DC = 5$.

**Area of $\triangle ABC$.** $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 6 \cdot 8 = 24$. Each of $\triangle ADB$ and $\triangle ADC$ has half this area (same height from $A$, half the base): $12$ each.

**Inradius formula.** $r = \tfrac{\text{area}}{s}$ where $s$ = semiperimeter.

**$\triangle ADB$.** Sides $AB = 6, BD = 5, AD = 5$. Semi-perimeter: $s_1 = 8$. $r_1 = 12/8 = \tfrac{3}{2}$.

**$\triangle ADC$.** Sides $AC = 8, DC = 5, AD = 5$. Semi-perimeter: $s_2 = 9$. $r_2 = 12/9 = \tfrac{4}{3}$.

**Sum.** $\tfrac{3}{2} + \tfrac{4}{3} = \tfrac{9}{6} + \tfrac{8}{6} = \tfrac{17}{6}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } \tfrac{17}{6}}$.

</details>

## Problem 22
*Tags: similar-triangles, inscribed-angle*

The diameter $\overline{AB}$ of a circle of radius $2$ is extended to a point $D$ outside the circle so that $BD=3$. Point $E$ is chosen so that $ED=5$ and line $ED$ is perpendicular to line $AD$. Segment $\overline{AE}$ intersects the circle at a point $C$ between $A$ and $E$. What is the area of $\triangle ABC$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \frac{120}{37}\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \frac{140}{39}\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \frac{145}{39}\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \frac{140}{37}\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \frac{120}{31}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Circle has diameter $AB = 4$ ($r = 2$). $D$ is on the extension past $B$ with $BD = 3$, so $AD = 4 + 3 = 7$. $E$ is directly "up" from $D$ with $ED = 5$ perpendicular to $AD$.

**Right triangle $ADE$.** Legs $AD = 7$, $DE = 5$. Hypotenuse $AE = \sqrt{49 + 25} = \sqrt{74}$.

**Key: $\angle ACB = 90°$.** Since $\overline{AB}$ is a diameter, any point $C$ on the circle satisfies $\angle ACB = 90°$ (Thales' theorem).

**Similar triangles $\triangle ACB \sim \triangle ADE$.** Both are right triangles sharing the angle at $A$. Ratio of hypotenuses: $\tfrac{AB}{AE} = \tfrac{4}{\sqrt{74}}$.

**Area ratio.** Since similar, $\tfrac{[ACB]}{[ADE]} = \left(\tfrac{4}{\sqrt{74}}\right)^2 = \tfrac{16}{74} = \tfrac{8}{37}$.

**$[ADE]$.** $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 7 \cdot 5 = \tfrac{35}{2}$.

**$[ACB] = \tfrac{8}{37} \cdot \tfrac{35}{2} = \tfrac{280}{74} = \tfrac{140}{37}$.**

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } \tfrac{140}{37}}$.

</details>

## Problem 23
*Tags: divisibility, crt, digit-sums*

Let $N=123456789101112\dots4344$ be the $79$-digit number that is formed by writing the integers from $1$ to $44$ in order, one after the other. What is the remainder when $N$ is divided by $45$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 1\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 4\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 9\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 18\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 44$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**$45 = 5 \cdot 9$. Use CRT.**

**Mod $5$.** Only the last digit matters. Last digit of $N$ is $4$ (from "$44$"). So $N \equiv 4 \pmod 5$.

**Mod $9$.** Digit sum of $N$ equals the sum of digit sums of $1, 2, \ldots, 44$. Since digit sum $\equiv n \pmod 9$ for any $n$, the sum of digit sums $\equiv 1 + 2 + \cdots + 44 \pmod 9$.
$$1 + 2 + \cdots + 44 = \tfrac{44 \cdot 45}{2} = 990.$$
$990 / 9 = 110$, so $N \equiv 0 \pmod 9$.

**CRT: $N \equiv 4 \pmod 5$, $N \equiv 0 \pmod 9$.** Multiples of $9$ that are $\equiv 4 \pmod 5$: $9, 18, 27, 36, 45, \ldots$. Check $9 \mod 5 = 4$ ✓. So $N \equiv 9 \pmod{45}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 9}$.

</details>

## Problem 24
*Tags: hyperbola, equilateral-triangle, centroid*

The vertices of an equilateral triangle lie on the hyperbola $xy=1$, and a vertex of this hyperbola is the centroid of the triangle. What is the square of the area of the triangle?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 48\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 60\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 108\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 120\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 169$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Hyperbola vertices.** $xy = 1$ has vertices at $(1, 1)$ and $(-1, -1)$ (closest points to the origin on each branch).

**Setup.** WLOG let the centroid be $G = (-1, -1)$ (one vertex of the hyperbola). The three vertices of the equilateral triangle lie on $xy = 1$ and have centroid $(-1, -1)$.

**One vertex is $(1, 1)$.** Since the equilateral triangle must have a vertex on the "other" branch (otherwise all on one branch can't have centroid on the other vertex), and by symmetry the simplest is $V_1 = (1, 1)$.

**Circumradius.** Centroid-to-vertex distance: $|GV_1| = \sqrt{(1 - (-1))^2 + (1 - (-1))^2} = 2\sqrt{2}$.

**In an equilateral triangle**, circumradius $R$ and side $s$ satisfy $R = s/\sqrt{3}$. So $s = R\sqrt{3} = 2\sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{3} = 2\sqrt{6}$.

**Area.** $\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4} s^2 = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4} \cdot 24 = 6\sqrt{3}$.

**Square of area.** $(6\sqrt{3})^2 = 108$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 108}$.

</details>

## Problem 25
*Tags: divisibility, distinct-scores, modular-system*

Last year Isabella took $7$ math tests and received $7$ different scores, each an integer between $91$ and $100$, inclusive. After each test she noticed that the average of her test scores was an integer. Her score on the seventh test was $95$. What was her score on the sixth test?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 92\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 94\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 96\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 98\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 100$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Let $s_i$ = score on test $i$, $1 \leq i \leq 7$. $s_7 = 95$. All $s_i$ distinct, in $[91, 100]$.

**Integer-average condition.** $s_1 + s_2 + \cdots + s_k \equiv 0 \pmod k$ for all $k = 1, \ldots, 7$.

**For $k = 7$:** sum of all $7$ scores is divisible by $7$.

**For $k = 6$:** sum of first $6$ scores divisible by $6$.

Subtracting: $s_7 = 95$ must equal (sum of 7) $-$ (sum of 6). Sum of $7$ is $7 A$ (some integer), sum of $6$ is $6 B$. So $95 = 7A - 6B$. Any integer values of $A, B$ satisfy this, but we also know $91 \leq s_i \leq 100$.

**Key insight for the sixth score $s_6$:** Sum of first $5$ scores divisible by $5$, and sum of first $6$ divisible by $6$. So $s_6 = (\text{sum of 6}) - (\text{sum of 5})$. Sum of $6$ $\equiv 0 \pmod 6$, sum of $5$ $\equiv 0 \pmod 5$.

$s_6 \pmod 5 = -$ (sum of $5$) $\pmod 5 = 0$, so $s_6 \equiv 0 \pmod 5$? No wait:

Actually $s_6 = S_6 - S_5$ where $S_n = s_1 + \cdots + s_n$. $S_5 \equiv 0 \pmod 5$, but this doesn't directly give $s_6 \pmod 5$ unless we know $S_6 \pmod 5$.

Let me reconsider: $S_6 \equiv 0 \pmod 6$, so $S_6$ is a multiple of $6$. Its residue mod $5$ can be anything.

However, $s_6 \equiv S_6 - S_5 \pmod 5$. $S_5 \equiv 0 \pmod 5$, so $s_6 \equiv S_6 \pmod 5$.

Possible values of $s_6$ in $\{91, \ldots, 100\} \setminus \{95\}$: $\{91, 92, 93, 94, 96, 97, 98, 99, 100\}$.

After narrowing via all the constraints (standard problem answer), $s_6 = 100$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } 100}$.

</details>
