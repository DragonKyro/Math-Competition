## Problem 1
*Tags: cell-phone-billing*

A cell phone plan costs $20$ dollars each month, plus $5$ cents per text message sent, plus $10$ cents for each minute used over $30$ hours. In January Michelle sent $100$ text messages and talked for $30.5$ hours. How much did she have to pay?

$
\textbf{(A)}\ 24.00 \qquad
\textbf{(B)}\ 24.50 \qquad
\textbf{(C)}\ 25.50 \qquad
\textbf{(D)}\ 28.00 \qquad
\textbf{(E)}\ 30.00 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Breakdown.**
- Base: $\$20$.
- Texts: $100 \cdot \$0.05 = \$5$.
- Overage: $30.5 - 30 = 0.5$ hrs $= 30$ min. $30 \cdot \$0.10 = \$3$.

**Total.** $20 + 5 + 3 = \$28$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } \$28.00}$.

</details>

## Problem 2
*Tags: ceiling-function, division*

A small bottle of shampoo can hold $35$ milliliters of shampoo, whereas a large bottle can hold $500$ milliliters of shampoo. Jasmine wants to buy the minimum number of small bottles necessary to completely fill a large bottle. How many bottles must she buy?

$
\textbf{(A)}\ 11 \qquad
\textbf{(B)}\ 12 \qquad
\textbf{(C)}\ 13 \qquad
\textbf{(D)}\ 14 \qquad
\textbf{(E)}\ 15 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** $\tfrac{500}{35} \approx 14.29$. So $14$ bottles give only $14 \cdot 35 = 490$ mL (not enough). We need $15$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } 15}$.

</details>

## Problem 3
*Tags: nested-averages*

Suppose [$a$ $b$] denotes the average of $a$ and $b$, and {$a$ $b$ $c$} denotes the average of $a$, $b$, and $c$. What is $\{\{\text{1 1 0}\} \text{ [0 1] } 0\}?$

$ \textbf{(A)}\ \frac{2}{9} \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \frac{5}{18} \qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \frac{1}{3} \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \frac{7}{18} \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \frac{2}{3} $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Inner averages.**
- $\{1, 1, 0\} = \tfrac{2}{3}$.
- $[0, 1] = \tfrac{1}{2}$.

**Outer average.**
$$\left\{\tfrac{2}{3},\ \tfrac{1}{2},\ 0\right\} = \tfrac{1}{3}\left(\tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{1}{2} + 0\right) = \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot \tfrac{7}{6} = \tfrac{7}{18}.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } \tfrac{7}{18}}$.

</details>

## Problem 4
*Tags: arithmetic-series, differences*

Let X and Y be the following sums of arithmetic sequences:

$$ \begin{eqnarray*}X &=& 10+12+14+\cdots+100,\\ Y &=& 12+14+16+\cdots+102.\end{eqnarray*} $$

What is the value of $Y - X? $

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 92\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 98\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 100\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 102\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 112 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Align the sums.** Both sequences have the same number of terms ($46$). Pair them:
$$Y - X = (12 - 10) + (14 - 12) + (16 - 14) + \cdots + (102 - 100).$$

Actually, a cleaner observation: $Y = X - 10 + 102$ (shift by removing the first term of $X$ and adding $102$).

So $Y - X = 102 - 10 = 92$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } 92}$.

</details>

## Problem 5
*Tags: weighted-average*

At an elementary school, the students in third grade, fourth grade, and fifth grade run an average of $12$, $15$, and $10$ minutes per day, respectively. There are twice as many third graders as fourth graders, and twice as many fourth graders as fifth graders. What is the average number of minutes run per day by these students?

$
\textbf{(A)}\ 12 \qquad
\textbf{(B)}\ \frac{37}{3} \qquad
\textbf{(C)}\ \frac{88}{7} \qquad
\textbf{(D)}\ 13 \qquad
\textbf{(E)}\ 14 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Let the number of fifth graders be $1$. Then fourth graders $= 2$, third graders $= 4$.

**Weighted average.**
$$\tfrac{4 \cdot 12 + 2 \cdot 15 + 1 \cdot 10}{4 + 2 + 1} = \tfrac{48 + 30 + 10}{7} = \tfrac{88}{7}.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } \tfrac{88}{7}}$.

</details>

## Problem 10
*Tags: prime-factorization, word-problem*

A majority of the $30$ students in Ms. Demeanor's class bought pencils at the school bookstore. Each of these students bought the same number of pencils, and this number was greater than $1$. The cost of a pencil in cents was greater than the number of pencils each student bought, and the total cost of all the pencils was $\$17.71$. What was the cost of a pencil in cents?

$
\textbf{(A)}\ 7 \qquad
\textbf{(B)}\ 11 \qquad
\textbf{(C)}\ 17 \qquad
\textbf{(D)}\ 23 \qquad
\textbf{(E)}\ 77 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Let $s$ = students buying, $n$ = pencils each, $c$ = cost per pencil (cents). Then $s \cdot n \cdot c = 1771$ cents.

**Factor $1771$.** $1771 = 7 \cdot 11 \cdot 23$.

**Constraints.**
- Majority: $s > 15$ (more than half of $30$).
- $n > 1$, $c > n$.
- $s, n, c$ are factors of $1771$: each is in $\{1, 7, 11, 23, 77, 161, 253, 1771\}$.

**From $s > 15$** and $s \le 30$: the only factor of $1771$ in $(15, 30]$ is $23$. So $s = 23$.

Then $n \cdot c = 77$. Factor pairs with $c > n > 1$: $(n, c) = (7, 11)$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 11}$.

</details>

## Problem 12
*Tags: basketball-scoring, linear-equation*

The players on a basketball team made some three-point shots, some two-point shots, and some one-point free throws. They scored as many points with two-point shots as with three-point shots. Their number of successful free throws was one more than their number of successful two-point shots. The team's total score was $61$ points. How many free throws did they make?

$
\textbf{(A)}\ 13 \qquad
\textbf{(B)}\ 14 \qquad
\textbf{(C)}\ 15 \qquad
\textbf{(D)}\ 16 \qquad
\textbf{(E)}\ 17 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Let $t$ = two-point shots, $s$ = three-point shots, $f$ = free throws.
- Equal points on $2$'s and $3$'s: $2t = 3s$, so $s = \tfrac{2t}{3}$.
- Free throws: $f = t + 1$.
- Total: $2t + 3s + f = 61$.

**Substitute.** $2t + 2t + (t + 1) = 61$ (using $3s = 2t$), so $5t + 1 = 61$, $t = 12$.

**Free throws.** $f = 12 + 1 = 13$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } 13}$.

</details>

## Problem 14
*Tags: dice-probability, area-vs-circumference*

A pair of standard $6$-sided dice is rolled once. The sum of the numbers rolled determines the diameter of a circle. What is the probability that the numerical value of the area of the circle is less than the numerical value of the circle's circumference?

$
\textbf{(A)}\ \frac{1}{36} \qquad
\textbf{(B)}\ \frac{1}{12} \qquad
\textbf{(C)}\ \frac{1}{6} \qquad
\textbf{(D)}\ \frac{1}{4} \qquad
\textbf{(E)}\ \frac{5}{18} $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Condition.** Area $< $ circumference: $\pi (d/2)^2 < \pi d \implies \tfrac{d}{4} < 1 \implies d < 4$.

**Possible sums $d$.** Sum of two dice $\in \{2, 3, \ldots, 12\}$. We need $d < 4$, so $d \in \{2, 3\}$.
- $d = 2$: $(1, 1)$. $1$ way.
- $d = 3$: $(1, 2), (2, 1)$. $2$ ways.

**Probability.** $\tfrac{3}{36} = \tfrac{1}{12}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } \tfrac{1}{12}}$.

</details>

## Problem 17
*Tags: periodic-sequence, telescoping*

In the eight term sequence $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$, $E$, $F$, $G$, $H$, the value of $C$ is $5$ and the sum of any three consecutive terms is $30$. What is $A+H$?

$
\textbf{(A)}\ 17 \qquad
\textbf{(B)}\ 18 \qquad
\textbf{(C)}\ 25 \qquad
\textbf{(D)}\ 26 \qquad
\textbf{(E)}\ 43 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Period-3.** Every three consecutive terms sum to $30$, so comparing $A+B+C = B+C+D = 30$ gives $A = D$. Similarly $B = E = H$, $C = F$, and $D = G$, so the sequence is period-$3$: $A, B, C, A, B, C, A, B$.

**Values.** $C = 5$ and $A + B + C = 30$, so $A + B = 25$. Since $H = B$, $A + H = A + B = 25$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 25}$.

</details>

## Problem 18
*Tags: circle-overlap, area*

Circles $A, B,$ and $C$ each has radius $1$. Circles $A$ and $B$ share one point of tangency. Circle $C$ has a point of tangency with the midpoint of $\overline{AB}.$ What is the area inside circle $C$ but outside circle $A$ and circle $B?$

_(Asymptote diagram omitted — see AoPS for image)_


$
\textbf{(A)}\ 3 - \frac{\pi}{2} \qquad
\textbf{(B)}\ \frac{\pi}{2} \qquad
\textbf{(C)}\  2 \qquad
\textbf{(D)}\ \frac{3\pi}{4} \qquad
\textbf{(E)}\ 1+\frac{\pi}{2} $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Circles $A$ and $B$ have radius $1$, tangent at their midpoint $M$. So $AB = 2$ with $M = $ midpoint. Circle $C$ has radius $1$ and is tangent to line $AB$ at $M$. So $C$ is centered $1$ unit from $M$, perpendicular to $AB$.

**Overlap.** Circle $C$ and circle $B$ both pass through $M$. By the symmetry, circles $B$ and $C$ intersect at two points symmetric about $MC$ (well, actually both pass through the tangent point $M$ and meet at one more point).

Actually, with these specific tangencies, circle $C$ intersects each of $A, B$ along arcs, cutting off lens-shaped regions.

**Using a clean rearrangement.** The desired region (inside $C$, outside $A$ and outside $B$) can be rearranged, using the symmetries and area-conservation properties of these tangent unit circles, into a region equivalent to a square with diagonal $2$, which has area $2$.

More precisely, the two lunes inside $C$ but outside $A$ (and symmetrically outside $B$) can be swapped with equal-area regions inside $A$ (or $B$) but outside $C$ to form a $\sqrt{2}$-by-$\sqrt{2}$ square with area $2$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 2}$.

</details>

## Problem 21
*Tags: probability, counterfeit-coins*

Two counterfeit coins of equal weight are mixed with $8$ identical genuine coins. The weight of each of the counterfeit coins is different from the weight of each of the genuine coins. A pair of coins is selected at random without replacement from the $10$ coins. A second pair is selected at random without replacement from the remaining $8$ coins. The combined weight of the first pair is equal to the combined weight of the second pair. What is the probability that all $4$ selected coins are genuine?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ \frac{7}{11}\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \frac{9}{13}\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \frac{11}{15}\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \frac{15}{19}\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \frac{15}{16} $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Conditional probability.** We want $P(\text{all 4 genuine} \mid \text{two pairs have equal weight})$.

**Cases where two pairs have equal weight.**
- *Case A: all $4$ coins genuine.* Weights automatically equal (both pairs sum to $2g$ where $g$ is genuine weight).
- *Case B: exactly $2$ counterfeit coins among the $4$, distributed as one per pair.* Each pair has $1$ counterfeit + $1$ genuine, so each pair weighs $c + g$. Equal weights ✓.
- Other distributions (both counterfeits in one pair, or $1$ counterfeit among $4$): weights not equal (unless $c = g$, which contradicts).

**Count outcomes.** Treat all $4$ coin-positions as distinguishable slots. Total ways to fill $4$ slots from $10$: $10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7 = 5040$.

- Case A (all $4$ genuine): $8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6 \cdot 5 = 1680$.
- Case B ($2$ counterfeit split across $2$ pairs): need exactly one counterfeit in each pair. Choose which slot in pair $1$ is counterfeit ($2$ ways), which slot in pair $2$ is counterfeit ($2$ ways), then fill the $2$ counterfeit slots with the $2$ fakes ($2!$ ways), and the $2$ genuine slots with genuine ($8 \cdot 7$ ways). Total: $2 \cdot 2 \cdot 2 \cdot 56 = 448$.

**Conditional probability.**
$$P = \tfrac{1680}{1680 + 448} = \tfrac{1680}{2128} = \tfrac{15}{19}.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } \tfrac{15}{19}}$.

</details>

## Problem 22
*Tags: pentagon-coloring, casework*

Each vertex of convex pentagon $ABCDE$ is to be assigned a color. There are $6$ colors to choose from, and the ends of each diagonal must have different colors. How many different colorings are possible?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 2520\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 2880\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 3120\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 3250\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 3750 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Diagonals in a pentagon.** The $5$ diagonals of pentagon $ABCDE$: $AC, BD, CE, DA, EB$. (Sides $AB, BC, \ldots$ are not diagonals.)

**Rephrase.** Two vertices must have different colors iff they are connected by a diagonal. The diagonal graph is the "pentagram" graph — which is the same as a $5$-cycle $A - C - E - B - D - A$ (this is a Hamiltonian cycle in the diagonal graph).

So we need to count proper $6$-colorings of a $5$-cycle.

**Chromatic polynomial of $C_n$.** $P(C_n, k) = (k - 1)^n + (-1)^n (k - 1)$.

With $n = 5, k = 6$:
$$P(C_5, 6) = 5^5 - 5 = 3125 - 5 = 3120.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 3120}$.

</details>

## Problem 23
*Tags: base-3, skip-counting*

Seven students count from 1 to 1000 as follows:

Alice says all the numbers, except she skips the middle number in each consecutive group of three numbers. That is, Alice says 1, 3, 4, 6, 7, 9, . . ., 997, 999, 1000.

Barbara says all of the numbers that Alice doesn't say, except she also skips the middle number in each consecutive group of three numbers.

Candice says all of the numbers that neither Alice nor Barbara says, except she also skips the middle number in each consecutive group of three numbers.

Debbie, Eliza, and Fatima say all of the numbers that none of the students with the first names beginning before theirs in the alphabet say, except each also skips the middle number in each of her consecutive groups of three numbers.

Finally, George says the only number that no one else says.

What number does George say?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 37\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 242\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 365\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 728\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 998 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Pattern.** Alice skips numbers $\equiv 2 \pmod 3$. Those become Barbara's input. Barbara divides her numbers into groups of $3$ and skips the middles — which are the numbers $\equiv 5 \pmod 9$. Candice skips $\equiv 14 \pmod{27}$. Debbie skips $\equiv 41 \pmod{81}$. Eliza skips $\equiv 122 \pmod{243}$. Fatima skips $\equiv 365 \pmod{729}$.

The pattern: the skipped residue at step $k$ is $r_k = \tfrac{3^k - 1}{2}$:
- $r_1 = 1$? Let me reverify. Actually, Alice's skipped residue mod $3$ is $2$. Barbara's skipped residue mod $9$ is $5 = 2 + 3$. Candice's is $14 = 5 + 9$. Debbie's is $41 = 14 + 27$. Eliza's is $122 = 41 + 81$. Fatima's is $365 = 122 + 243$.

**George's number.** The single number from $1$ to $1000$ that Fatima skips, $\equiv 365 \pmod{729}$. In $[1, 1000]$: $365$ itself (next is $365 + 729 = 1094 > 1000$).

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 365}$.

</details>

## Problem 25
*Tags: ray-partitional, grid-counting*

Let $R$ be a unit square region and $n \geq 4$ an integer. A point $X$ in the interior of $R$ is called ''n-ray'' partitional if there are $n$ rays emanating from $X$ that divide $R$ into $n$ triangles of equal area. How many points are $100$-ray partitional but not $60$-ray partitional?

$
\textbf{(A)}\ 1500 \qquad
\textbf{(B)}\ 1560 \qquad
\textbf{(C)}\ 2320 \qquad
\textbf{(D)}\ 2480 \qquad
\textbf{(E)}\ 2500 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Characterize $n$-ray partitional points.** Setting up coordinates with the unit square $R$ at $[0,1]^2$, a point $X = (x, y)$ is $n$-ray partitional iff $x = \tfrac{d}{n/2}$ and $y = \tfrac{a}{n/2}$ for positive integers $a, d$ with $a < n/2, d < n/2$.

So for $n = 100$: $X = \left(\tfrac{d}{50}, \tfrac{a}{50}\right)$ with $a, d \in \{1, 2, \ldots, 49\}$. Count: $49^2 = 2401$.

For $n = 60$: $X = \left(\tfrac{m}{30}, \tfrac{k}{30}\right)$ with $m, k \in \{1, \ldots, 29\}$. Count: $29^2 = 841$.

**$100$-partitional AND $60$-partitional.** $(d/50, a/50) = (m/30, k/30)$ for some valid $m, k$. This requires $d/50 = m/30 \iff 3d = 5m$, so $5 \mid d$. Valid $d \in \{5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45\}$ — $9$ values. Similarly $9$ values for $a$. Total: $81$ points are both.

**$100$-partitional but NOT $60$-partitional.** $2401 - 81 = 2320$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 2320}$.

</details>
