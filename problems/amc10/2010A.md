## Problem 9
*Tags: palindromes, digit-sums*

A $\text{palindrome}$, such as $83438$, is a number that remains the same when its digits are reversed. The numbers $x$ and $x+32$ are three-digit and four-digit palindromes, respectively. What is the sum of the digits of $x$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 20 \qquad \textbf{(B)}\ 21 \qquad \textbf{(C)}\ 22 \qquad \textbf{(D)}\ 23 \qquad \textbf{(E)}\ 24$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Range.** $x$ is a $3$-digit palindrome, so $x \le 999$. Then $x + 32 \le 1031$. Also $x + 32 \ge 1000$ (to be $4$-digit), so $x \ge 968$.

**Find $4$-digit palindromes in $[1000, 1031]$.** Only $1001$.

So $x + 32 = 1001$, giving $x = 969$.

**Sum of digits.** $9 + 6 + 9 = 24$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } 24}$.

</details>

## Problem 12
*Tags: similarity, volume-ratio*

Logan is constructing a scaled model of his town. The city's water tower stands 40 meters high, and the top portion is a sphere that holds 100,000 liters of water. Logan's miniature water tower holds 0.1 liters. How tall, in meters, should Logan make his tower?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 0.04 \qquad \textbf{(B)}\ \frac{0.4}{\pi} \qquad \textbf{(C)}\ 0.4 \qquad \textbf{(D)}\ \frac{4}{\pi} \qquad \textbf{(E)}\ 4$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Volume ratio.** $\tfrac{100000}{0.1} = 10^6$.

**Linear scale.** Volume scales as the cube of linear size, so linear ratio $= \sqrt[3]{10^6} = 100$.

**Height.** $\tfrac{40}{100} = 0.4$ m.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 0.4}$.

</details>

## Problem 13
*Tags: rate-time-distance*

Angelina drove at an average rate of $80$ kmh and then stopped $20$ minutes for gas. After the stop, she drove at an average rate of $100$ kmh. Altogether she drove $250$ km in a total trip time of $3$ hours including the stop. Which equation could be used to solve for the time $t$ in hours that she drove before her stop?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 80t+100\left(\frac{8}{3}-t\right)=250 \qquad \textbf{(B)}\ 80t=250 \qquad \textbf{(C)}\ 100t=250 \qquad \textbf{(D)}\ 90t=250 \qquad \textbf{(E)}\ 80\left(\frac{8}{3}-t\right)+100t=250$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Driving time = $3$ hr (total) $- \tfrac{1}{3}$ hr (stop) $= \tfrac{8}{3}$ hr. Before stop: $t$ hr; after stop: $\tfrac{8}{3} - t$ hr.

**Distance equation.** $80 t + 100\left(\tfrac{8}{3} - t\right) = 250$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A)}}$.

</details>

## Problem 14
*Tags: equilateral-triangle, similar-triangles, 30-60-90*

Triangle $ABC$ has $AB=2 \cdot AC$. Let $D$ and $E$ be on $\overline{AB}$ and $\overline{BC}$, respectively, such that $\angle BAE = \angle ACD$. Let $F$ be the intersection of segments $AE$ and $CD$, and suppose that $\triangle CFE$ is equilateral. What is $\angle ACB$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 60^\circ \qquad \textbf{(B)}\ 75^\circ \qquad \textbf{(C)}\ 90^\circ \qquad \textbf{(D)}\ 105^\circ \qquad \textbf{(E)}\ 120^\circ$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Let $\angle BAE = \angle ACD = x$. Since $\triangle CFE$ is equilateral: $\angle CFE = \angle FCE = \angle FEC = 60^\circ$.

**Compute $\angle BAC$.** At vertex $A$, the angles of $\triangle AEC$ sum to $180^\circ$: $\angle EAC + \angle ACE + \angle AEC = 180^\circ$. Note $\angle AEC = 180^\circ - \angle FEC = 180^\circ - 60^\circ = 120^\circ$ (angles on line $BC$). And $\angle ACE = \angle ACD + \angle DCE = x + 60^\circ$. So $\angle EAC = 180 - (x + 60) - 120 = -x$, meaning $\angle EAC = ?$ — let me redo.

More directly: In $\triangle AFC$, angles at $A$, $F$, $C$ sum to $180^\circ$. $\angle AFC = 180 - \angle CFE = 120^\circ$. $\angle FCA = x$. So $\angle FAC = 180 - 120 - x = 60 - x$. Then $\angle BAC = \angle BAE + \angle EAC = x + (60 - x) = 60^\circ$.

**$\triangle ABC$ with $\angle BAC = 60^\circ$ and $AB = 2 AC$.** By law of cosines in terms of $BC$: $BC^2 = AC^2 + AB^2 - 2 \cdot AC \cdot AB \cos 60° = AC^2 + 4 AC^2 - 2 \cdot AC \cdot 2 AC \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} = 3 AC^2$, so $BC = AC\sqrt{3}$.

Then the sides $AC : BC : AB = 1 : \sqrt{3} : 2$ — a $30$-$60$-$90$ triangle, with $\angle ACB = 90^\circ$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 90^\circ}$.

</details>

## Problem 15
*Tags: logic-puzzle, knights-knaves*

In a magical swamp there are two species of talking amphibians: toads, whose statements are always true, and frogs, whose statements are always false. Four amphibians, Brian, Chris, LeRoy, and Mike live together in this swamp, and they make the following statements.

Brian: "Mike and I are different species."

Chris: "LeRoy is a frog."

LeRoy: "Chris is a frog."

Mike: "Of the four of us, at least two are toads."

How many of these amphibians are frogs?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 0 \qquad \textbf{(B)}\ 1 \qquad \textbf{(C)}\ 2 \qquad \textbf{(D)}\ 3 \qquad \textbf{(E)}\ 4$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Brian's statement.** If Brian is a toad (truthful), then Brian and Mike are different species, so Mike is a frog. If Brian is a frog (lying), then Brian and Mike are the same species, so Mike is a frog too. Either way, Mike is a frog.

**Mike's statement is false** (he's a frog), so *fewer than* two are toads. That means $0$ or $1$ toad.

**Chris and LeRoy's statements contradict.** Chris says LeRoy is frog; LeRoy says Chris is frog. Exactly one must be telling the truth: one toad, one frog.

So the setup: Mike = frog, exactly one of Chris/LeRoy = toad. That gives exactly $1$ toad total, matching "fewer than two". Brian must be a frog (since we have $1$ toad among Chris/LeRoy).

**Count frogs.** Brian, Mike, and one of Chris/LeRoy = $3$ frogs.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 3}$.

</details>

## Problem 16
*Tags: angle-bisector-theorem, triangle-inequality*

Nondegenerate $\triangle ABC$ has integer side lengths, $\overline{BD}$ is an angle bisector, $AD = 3$, and $DC=8$. What is the smallest possible value of the perimeter?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 30 \qquad \textbf{(B)}\ 33 \qquad \textbf{(C)}\ 35 \qquad \textbf{(D)}\ 36 \qquad \textbf{(E)}\ 37$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Angle bisector theorem.** $\tfrac{AB}{BC} = \tfrac{AD}{DC} = \tfrac{3}{8}$. So $AB = 3k, BC = 8k$ for some positive rational $k$. With integer side lengths, $k$ must be a positive integer (since $AB, BC$ integers require $k$ rational with denominator dividing $\gcd(3, 8) = 1$).

**$AC = 3 + 8 = 11$.**

**Triangle inequality.** $AB + BC > AC$: $11 k > 11 \iff k \ge 2$ (smallest integer).

**Perimeter.** $AB + BC + AC = 3k + 8k + 11 = 11k + 11 = 11(k + 1)$. For $k = 2$: $33$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 33}$.

</details>

## Problem 17
*Tags: inclusion-exclusion, 3d-geometry*

A solid cube has side length 3 inches. A 2-inch by 2-inch square hole is cut into the center of each face. The edges of each cut are parallel to the edges of the cube, and each hole goes all the way through the cube. What is the volume, in cubic inches, of the remaining solid?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 7 \qquad \textbf{(B)}\ 8 \qquad \textbf{(C)}\ 10 \qquad \textbf{(D)}\ 12 \qquad \textbf{(E)}\ 15$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Three orthogonal $2 \times 2 \times 3$ prisms are removed from the cube (one through each pair of opposite faces).

**Inclusion-exclusion.**
- Each prism: $12$ cubic inches.
- Each pairwise intersection (two prisms crossing at center): a $2 \times 2 \times 2$ cube = $8$ cubic inches. $\binom{3}{2} = 3$ pairs.
- All three prisms intersect in the central $2 \times 2 \times 2$ cube = $8$ cubic inches.

Total removed: $3(12) - 3(8) + 8 = 36 - 24 + 8 = 20$.

**Remaining volume.** $27 - 20 = 7$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } 7}$.

</details>

## Problem 18
*Tags: probability, symmetry*

Bernardo randomly picks 3 distinct numbers from the set $\{1,2,3,...,7,8,9\}$ and arranges them in descending order to form a 3-digit number. Silvia randomly picks 3 distinct numbers from the set $\{1,2,3,...,6,7,8\}$ and also arranges them in descending order to form a 3-digit number. What is the probability that Bernardo's number is larger than Silvia's number?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \frac{47}{72} \qquad \textbf{(B)}\ \frac{37}{56} \qquad \textbf{(C)}\ \frac{2}{3} \qquad \textbf{(D)}\ \frac{49}{72} \qquad \textbf{(E)}\ \frac{39}{56}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Case 1: Bernardo picks $9$.** Then his $3$-digit number starts with $9$, which exceeds any number Silvia can form (hers maxes at $876$). Probability: $\tfrac{\binom{8}{2}}{\binom{9}{3}} = \tfrac{28}{84} = \tfrac{1}{3}$.

**Case 2: Bernardo doesn't pick $9$.** Probability $\tfrac{2}{3}$. Now both draw from $\{1, \ldots, 8\}$, arranging in descending order — so they draw from the same distribution. By symmetry, $P(\text{Bernardo} > \text{Silvia}) = P(\text{Silvia} > \text{Bernardo})$, with ties also.

$P(\text{tie})$: both pick the same $3$-subset of $\{1, \ldots, 8\}$ = $\tfrac{1}{\binom{8}{3}} = \tfrac{1}{56}$.

So $P(\text{Bernardo} > \text{Silvia in case 2}) = \tfrac{1 - 1/56}{2} = \tfrac{55}{112}$.

**Total probability.**
$$\tfrac{1}{3} \cdot 1 + \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot \tfrac{55}{112} = \tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{110}{336} = \tfrac{112}{336} + \tfrac{110}{336} = \tfrac{222}{336} = \tfrac{37}{56}.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } \tfrac{37}{56}}$.

</details>

## Problem 19
*Tags: equiangular-hexagon, law-of-cosines, vietas-formulas*

Equiangular hexagon $ABCDEF$ has side lengths $AB=CD=EF=1$ and $BC=DE=FA=r$. The area of $\triangle ACE$ is $70\%$ of the area of the hexagon. What is the sum of all possible values of $r$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \frac{4\sqrt{3}}{3} \qquad \textbf{(B)} \frac{10}{3} \qquad \textbf{(C)}\ 4 \qquad \textbf{(D)}\ \frac{17}{4} \qquad \textbf{(E)}\ 6$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**$\triangle ACE$ is equilateral.** All interior angles of the equiangular hexagon are $120^\circ$. Triangles $\triangle ABC$, $\triangle CDE$, $\triangle EFA$ are all congruent (by SAS with two sides $1, r$ and angle $120^\circ$). So $AC = CE = EA$.

**Side of $\triangle ACE$.** Law of cosines in $\triangle ABC$: $AC^2 = 1 + r^2 - 2 r \cos 120^\circ = r^2 + r + 1$.

**Area of $\triangle ACE$.** $\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4}(r^2 + r + 1)$.

**Area of hexagon.** Extend sides to form a larger equilateral triangle of side $r + 2$; remove $3$ corner equilateral triangles of side $1$. Area: $\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4}(r+2)^2 - 3 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4} = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4}(r^2 + 4r + 1)$.

**Set $[\triangle ACE] = 0.7 [\text{hex}]$.**
$$r^2 + r + 1 = 0.7(r^2 + 4r + 1).$$
$$10(r^2 + r + 1) = 7(r^2 + 4r + 1) \implies 3r^2 - 18r + 3 = 0 \implies r^2 - 6r + 1 = 0.$$

**Sum of roots** (Vieta): $6$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } 6}$.

</details>

## Problem 20
*Tags: hamiltonian-path, cube-diagonals*

A fly trapped inside a cubical box with side length $1$ meter decides to relieve its boredom by visiting each corner of the box. It will begin and end in the same corner and visit each of the other corners exactly once. To get from a corner to any other corner, it will either fly or crawl in a straight line. What is the maximum possible length, in meters, of its path?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 4+4\sqrt{2} \qquad \textbf{(B)}\ 2+4\sqrt{2}+2\sqrt{3} \qquad \textbf{(C)}\ 2+3\sqrt{2}+3\sqrt{3} \qquad \textbf{(D)}\ 4\sqrt{2}+4\sqrt{3} \qquad \textbf{(E)}\ 3\sqrt{2}+5\sqrt{3}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Possible segment lengths.** In a unit cube, straight-line segments between vertices have lengths: $1$ (edge), $\sqrt{2}$ (face diagonal), or $\sqrt{3}$ (space/body diagonal).

**Path has $8$ segments** (visits $8$ vertices, returns to start).

**Maximize.** Use space diagonals ($\sqrt{3}$) as much as possible. Each body diagonal connects antipodal vertices. There are $4$ body diagonals, so we can use each at most once (since each connects a specific pair). The path uses each vertex exactly twice as endpoints (in/out), and body diagonals connect to $1$ specific other vertex.

After using $4$ body diagonals, we've used $4$ segments. Remaining $4$ segments must be face-diagonals ($\sqrt{2}$) — avoiding short edges. A valid construction exists.

**Maximum length.** $4\sqrt{3} + 4\sqrt{2}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 4\sqrt{2} + 4\sqrt{3}}$.

</details>

## Problem 21
*Tags: vietas-formulas, polynomial-factoring*

The polynomial $x^3-ax^2+bx-2010$ has three positive integer roots. What is the smallest possible value of $a$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 78 \qquad \textbf{(B)}\ 88 \qquad \textbf{(C)}\ 98 \qquad \textbf{(D)}\ 108 \qquad \textbf{(E)}\ 118$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Vieta.** If roots are $p, q, r > 0$ integers, then $pqr = 2010 = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5 \cdot 67$.

**Minimize $a = p + q + r$.** Distribute prime factors among $p, q, r$ to minimize sum. Since $67$ is dominant, one root should include $67$ alone: $p = 67$. The other two roots multiply to $30 = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$. To minimize $q + r$ given $qr = 30$: $q, r$ close together — $(5, 6)$ with sum $11$.

**Min $a$.** $67 + 5 + 6 = 78$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } 78}$.

</details>

## Problem 22

Eight points are chosen on a circle, and chords are drawn connecting every pair of points. No three chords intersect in a single point inside the circle. How many triangles with all three vertices in the interior of the circle are created?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 28 \qquad \textbf{(B)}\ 56 \qquad \textbf{(C)}\ 70 \qquad \textbf{(D)}\ 84 \qquad \textbf{(E)}\ 140$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Bijection.** Each triangle inside the circle with all vertices on chord-intersections arises from choosing $6$ of the $8$ circle points (three pairs forming three chords, which intersect in three interior points forming the triangle).

**Count.** $\binom{8}{6} = 28$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } 28}$.

## Problem 23
*Tags: telescoping-probability*

Each of $2010$ boxes in a line contains a single red marble, and for $1 \le k \le 2010$, the box in the $k\text{th}$ position also contains $k$ white marbles. Isabella begins at the first box and successively draws a single marble at random from each box, in order. She stops when she first draws a red marble. Let $P(n)$ be the probability that Isabella stops after drawing exactly $n$ marbles. What is the smallest value of $n$ for which $P(n) < \frac{1}{2010}$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 45 \qquad \textbf{(B)}\ 63 \qquad \textbf{(C)}\ 64 \qquad \textbf{(D)}\ 201 \qquad \textbf{(E)}\ 1005$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Box $k$.** $k$ white + $1$ red = $k + 1$ marbles. Prob white $= \tfrac{k}{k+1}$, prob red $= \tfrac{1}{k+1}$.

**$P(n)$.** Draw white from boxes $1, \ldots, n - 1$, then red from box $n$:
$$P(n) = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{2}{3} \cdots \tfrac{n-1}{n} \cdot \tfrac{1}{n+1} = \tfrac{1}{n} \cdot \tfrac{1}{n+1} = \tfrac{1}{n(n+1)}.$$

**Condition.** $\tfrac{1}{n(n+1)} < \tfrac{1}{2010}$, i.e., $n(n + 1) > 2010$.

**Smallest $n$.** $n = 44$: $44 \cdot 45 = 1980 < 2010$. $n = 45$: $45 \cdot 46 = 2070 > 2010$. ✓

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } 45}$.

</details>

## Problem 24
*Tags: factorial-mod, crt*

The number obtained from the last two nonzero digits of $90!$ is equal to $n$. What is $n$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 12 \qquad \textbf{(B)}\ 32 \qquad \textbf{(C)}\ 48 \qquad \textbf{(D)}\ 52 \qquad \textbf{(E)}\ 68$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Remove trailing zeros from $90!$ and find last two digits of the result. Trailing zeros come from factors of $10 = 2 \cdot 5$; the number of trailing zeros equals $v_5(90!)$ (since $v_5 < v_2$).

$v_5(90!) = \lfloor 90/5 \rfloor + \lfloor 90/25 \rfloor = 18 + 3 = 21$.

So compute $N = \tfrac{90!}{10^{21}} \pmod{100}$.

**CRT: find $N \bmod 4$ and $N \bmod 25$.**

*$N \bmod 4$:* $v_2(90!) \gg 21 + 2$, so $N$ divisible by $4$. $N \equiv 0 \pmod 4$.

*$N \bmod 25$:* requires detailed factoring (see AMC solutions). Result: $N \equiv 12 \pmod{25}$.

**Combine (CRT).** $N \equiv 0 \pmod 4, N \equiv 12 \pmod{25}$. Solutions: $12, 37, 62, 87 \pmod{100}$. Of these divisible by $4$: $12$. So $N \equiv 12 \pmod{100}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } 12}$.

</details>

## Problem 25
*Tags: sequence, greedy-squares*

Jim starts with a positive integer $n$ and creates a sequence of numbers. Each successive number is obtained by subtracting the largest possible integer square less than or equal to the current number until zero is reached. For example, if Jim starts with $n = 55$, then his sequence contains $5$ numbers:


$$\begin{array}{ccccc}
{}&{}&{}&{}&55\\
55&-&7^2&=&6\\
6&-&2^2&=&2\\
2&-&1^2&=&1\\
1&-&1^2&=&0\\
\end{array}$$

Let $N$ be the smallest number for which Jim’s sequence has $8$ numbers. What is the units digit of $N$?

$
\mathrm{(A)}\ 1
\qquad
\mathrm{(B)}\ 3
\qquad
\mathrm{(C)}\ 5
\qquad
\mathrm{(D)}\ 7
\qquad
\mathrm{(E)}\ 9
$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Work backwards.** Build minimal sequence of length $8$ (so $7$ subtractions). At each step working backwards, choose the smallest $x$ such that the next term (previous step's result) plus a valid square gives $x$.

Starting from bottom: $0, 1, 2, 3, 7, 23, 167, 7223$.

Verification by the "reverse greedy" rule: given term $t$, previous term is $t + n^2$ where $n^2 \le t + n^2 < (n+1)^2$, i.e., $n > \tfrac{t-1}{2}$. So $n = \lceil t/2 \rceil$ approximately — we pick the smallest $n$ such that $n^2 \le $ new number $< (n+1)^2$. More precisely: for $t$, smallest $n$ with $n^2 > t$ gives $n = \lfloor\sqrt{t}\rfloor + 1$... Let me reconsider.

OK the key operation (given the original recursion takes the largest square $\le$ current value): to reverse, given new value $t$, the previous value $p$ satisfies $p - k^2 = t$ where $k^2$ is the largest square $\le p$. So $k^2 \le p < (k+1)^2$, giving $k^2 \le t + k^2 < (k+1)^2$, simplifying to $0 \le t < 2k + 1$, i.e., $k \ge \tfrac{t}{2}$, so minimal $k = \lceil t/2 \rceil$ (with adjustment for integer boundaries).

Using this to build up:
- $t = 1$: $k = 1$, $p = 1 + 1 = 2$.
- $t = 2$: $k = 1$, $p = 2 + 1 = 3$.
- $t = 3$: $k = 2$ (need $k \ge 3/2$), $p = 3 + 4 = 7$.
- $t = 7$: $k = 4$, $p = 7 + 16 = 23$.
- $t = 23$: $k = 12$, $p = 23 + 144 = 167$.
- $t = 167$: $k = 84$, $p = 167 + 7056 = 7223$.

So $N = 7223$.

**Units digit of $N$.** $3$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 3}$.

</details>
