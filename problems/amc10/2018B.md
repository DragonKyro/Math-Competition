## Problem 1
*Tags: area-division*

Kate bakes a $20$-inch by $18$-inch pan of cornbread. The cornbread is cut into pieces that measure $2$ inches by $2$ inches. How many pieces of cornbread does the pan contain?

$\textbf{(A) } 90 \qquad \textbf{(B) } 100 \qquad \textbf{(C) } 180 \qquad \textbf{(D) } 200 \qquad \textbf{(E) } 360$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Divide dimensions.** Each dimension of the pan is an integer multiple of the piece dimension:
- Along the $20$-inch side: $20/2 = 10$ pieces.
- Along the $18$-inch side: $18/2 = 9$ pieces.

**Total.** $10 \cdot 9 = 90$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } 90}$.

</details>

## Problem 2
*Tags: rate-distance, weighted-average*

Sam drove $96$ miles in $90$ minutes. His average speed during the first $30$ minutes was $60$ mph (miles per hour), and his average speed during the second $30$ minutes was $65$ mph. What was his average speed, in mph, during the last $30$ minutes?

$
\textbf{(A) } 64 \qquad
\textbf{(B) } 65 \qquad
\textbf{(C) } 66 \qquad
\textbf{(D) } 67 \qquad
\textbf{(E) } 68
$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Distance per segment.**
- First $30$ min = $0.5$ hr at $60$ mph: $30$ miles.
- Second $30$ min at $65$ mph: $32.5$ miles.
- Let $v$ = speed in the last $30$ min: distance = $0.5v$ miles.

**Total distance.** $30 + 32.5 + 0.5v = 96$.
$$0.5 v = 33.5 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = 67.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 67}$.

</details>

## Problem 3
*Tags: counting-distinct-values*

In the expression $\left(\underline{\qquad}\times\underline{\qquad}\right)+\left(\underline{\qquad}\times\underline{\qquad}\right)$ each blank is to be filled in with one of the digits $1,2,3,$ or $4,$ with each digit being used once. How many different values can be obtained?

$
\textbf{(A) }2 \qquad
\textbf{(B) }3\qquad
\textbf{(C) }4 \qquad
\textbf{(D) }6 \qquad
\textbf{(E) }24 \qquad
$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Distinct values depend on how we partition $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ into two pairs** (order within a pair and between pairs doesn't matter for the sum).

**Three ways to partition $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ into two pairs.**
- $\{1, 2\}, \{3, 4\}$: sum $= 1 \cdot 2 + 3 \cdot 4 = 2 + 12 = 14$.
- $\{1, 3\}, \{2, 4\}$: sum $= 3 + 8 = 11$.
- $\{1, 4\}, \{2, 3\}$: sum $= 4 + 6 = 10$.

All distinct. So $3$ different values.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 3}$.

</details>

## Problem 4
*Tags: face-area, system-of-equations*

A three-dimensional rectangular box with dimensions $X$, $Y$, and $Z$ has faces whose surface areas are $24$, $24$, $48$, $48$, $72$, and $72$ square units. What is $X$ + $Y$ + $Z$?

$
\textbf{(A) }18 \qquad
\textbf{(B) }22 \qquad
\textbf{(C) }24 \qquad
\textbf{(D) }30 \qquad
\textbf{(E) }36 \qquad
$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Face areas come in pairs** (opposite faces). So the distinct face areas are $24, 48, 72$, corresponding to $XY, XZ, YZ$ in some order.

**Assume:** $XY = 24$, $XZ = 48$, $YZ = 72$.

**Multiply all three.** $(XYZ)^2 = 24 \cdot 48 \cdot 72 = 24 \cdot 3456 = 82944$. So $XYZ = \sqrt{82944} = 288$.

**Solve for each.**
- $Z = XYZ / XY = 288/24 = 12$.
- $Y = XYZ / XZ = 288/48 = 6$.
- $X = XYZ / YZ = 288/72 = 4$.

**Sum.** $4 + 6 + 12 = 22$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 22}$.

</details>

## Problem 5
*Tags: complementary-counting, subsets*

How many subsets of $\{2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9\}$ contain at least one prime number?

$\textbf{(A)} \text{ 128} \qquad \textbf{(B)} \text{ 192} \qquad \textbf{(C)} \text{ 224} \qquad \textbf{(D)} \text{ 240} \qquad \textbf{(E)} \text{ 256}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Complementary counting.** Count subsets that contain NO primes, then subtract from the total.

**Primes in $\{2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9\}$:** $2, 3, 5, 7$ — $4$ primes.

**Composites + 1:** $\{4, 6, 8, 9\}$ — $4$ elements (note $1$ is not in the set; we have $8$ elements total).

**Subsets with no primes = subsets of $\{4, 6, 8, 9\}$:** $2^4 = 16$.

**Total subsets of $\{2, \ldots, 9\}$:** $2^8 = 256$.

**Subsets with at least one prime.** $256 - 16 = 240$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 240}$.

</details>

## Problem 6
*Tags: probability, sequential-draws*

A box contains $5$ chips, numbered $1$, $2$, $3$, $4$, and $5$. Chips are drawn randomly one at a time without replacement until the sum of the values drawn exceeds $4$. What is the probability that $3$ draws are required?

$\textbf{(A)} \frac{1}{15} \qquad \textbf{(B)} \frac{1}{10} \qquad \textbf{(C)} \frac{1}{6} \qquad \textbf{(D)} \frac{1}{5} \qquad \textbf{(E)} \frac{1}{4}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Key observation.** After $3$ draws are required means: the first $2$ draws sum to $\leq 4$, AND at least one more is needed.

**List first-two-draw sequences with sum $\leq 4$** (with distinct values from $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$):
- $(1, 2)$: sum $3 \leq 4$ ✓.
- $(1, 3)$: sum $4 \leq 4$ ✓.
- $(2, 1)$: ✓.
- $(3, 1)$: ✓.

Other pairs exceed $4$.

**Total ordered pairs of two distinct draws:** $5 \cdot 4 = 20$.

**Probability first $2$ draws sum to $\leq 4$:** $\tfrac{4}{20} = \tfrac{1}{5}$.

When the first two sum to $\leq 4$, the third draw will bring the sum to at least $(1 + 2) + \text{(next smallest remaining)} = 3 + 3 = 6 > 4$. So $3$ draws are always exactly the number required in these cases (no chance of still being $\leq 4$ after $3$ draws when first two already sum to $3$ or $4$).

Actually: if first two are $(1, 2)$, sum $= 3$, third draw $\geq 3$ brings sum $\geq 6 > 4$, so stop after $3$. ✓ Similarly for others.

**Answer:** $\tfrac{1}{5}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } \tfrac{1}{5}}$.

</details>

## Problem 7
*Tags: similar-semicircles, ratio*

In the figure below, $N$ congruent semicircles lie on the diameter of a large semicircle, with their diameters covering the diameter of the large semicircle with no overlap. Let $A$ be the combined area of the small semicircles and $B$ be the area of the region inside the large semicircle but outside the semicircles. The ratio $A:B$ is $1:18$. What is $N$?



_(Asymptote diagram omitted — see AoPS for image)_


$\textbf{(A) } 16 \qquad \textbf{(B) } 17 \qquad \textbf{(C) } 18 \qquad \textbf{(D) } 19 \qquad \textbf{(E) } 36$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Set up variables.** Let each small semicircle have radius $r$. Then the large semicircle has radius $Nr$ (since $N$ small diameters of $2r$ cover the large diameter of $2Nr$).

**Areas.**
- Large semicircle: $\tfrac{1}{2} \pi (Nr)^2 = \tfrac{N^2 \pi r^2}{2}$.
- $N$ small semicircles: $N \cdot \tfrac{1}{2} \pi r^2 = \tfrac{N \pi r^2}{2}$.

**Setup:**
- $A$ = combined small area = $\tfrac{N \pi r^2}{2}$.
- $B$ = inside large, outside small = $\tfrac{N^2 \pi r^2}{2} - \tfrac{N \pi r^2}{2} = \tfrac{(N^2 - N) \pi r^2}{2}$.

**Ratio.**
$$\frac{A}{B} = \frac{N}{N^2 - N} = \frac{1}{N - 1} = \frac{1}{18}.$$

So $N - 1 = 18$, giving $N = 19$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 19}$.

</details>

## Problem 8
*Tags: quadratic-formula, staircase-pattern*

Sara makes a staircase out of toothpicks as shown:

_(Asymptote diagram omitted — see AoPS for image)_

This is a 3-step staircase and uses 18 toothpicks. How many steps would be in a staircase that used 180 toothpicks?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 10\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 11\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 12\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 24\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 30$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Find the toothpick count formula.** Let $T(n)$ = toothpicks for $n$-step staircase.

From small cases (with careful counting):
- $T(1) = 4$.
- $T(2) = 10$.
- $T(3) = 18$.

**Recognize the pattern.** Differences: $10 - 4 = 6$, $18 - 10 = 8$. Second differences: $8 - 6 = 2$. So $T(n)$ is a quadratic in $n$.

**Derive formula.** Try $T(n) = n(n + 3)$:
- $T(1) = 1 \cdot 4 = 4$ ✓
- $T(2) = 2 \cdot 5 = 10$ ✓
- $T(3) = 3 \cdot 6 = 18$ ✓

**Solve $T(n) = 180$.** $n(n + 3) = 180$, so $n^2 + 3n - 180 = 0$. Factor: $(n - 12)(n + 15) = 0$. $n = 12$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 12}$.

## Problem 9
*Tags: symmetry-dice, stars-and-bars*

The faces of each of $7$ standard dice are labeled with the integers from $1$ to $6$. Let $p$ be the probabilities that when all $7$ dice are rolled, the sum of the numbers on the top faces is $10$. What other sum occurs with the same probability as $p$?

$\textbf{(A)} \text{ 13} \qquad \textbf{(B)} \text{ 26} \qquad \textbf{(C)} \text{ 32} \qquad \textbf{(D)} \text{ 39} \qquad \textbf{(E)} \text{ 42}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Symmetry trick.** For a single die, $P(\text{outcome} = k) = P(\text{outcome} = 7 - k)$ for $k = 1, \ldots, 6$ (since $1 \leftrightarrow 6, 2 \leftrightarrow 5, 3 \leftrightarrow 4$).

For $7$ dice, the sum $S$ and the "flipped sum" $S' = 7 \cdot 7 - S = 49 - S$ have the same probability distribution (since flipping each die's outcome via $k \to 7 - k$ is a bijection on outcomes).

**Find the match for $S = 10$.** $S' = 49 - 10 = 39$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 39}$.

</details>

## Problem 10
*Tags: pyramid-volume, coordinate-geometry*

In the rectangular parallelepiped shown, $AB$ = $3$, $BC$ = $1$, and $CG$ = $2$. Point $M$ is the midpoint of $\overline{FG}$. What is the volume of the rectangular pyramid with base $BCHE$ and apex $M$?


_(Asymptote diagram omitted — see AoPS for image)_

$\textbf{(A) } 1 \qquad \textbf{(B) } \frac{4}{3} \qquad \textbf{(C) } \frac{3}{2} \qquad \textbf{(D) } \frac{5}{3} \qquad \textbf{(E) } 2$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Set coordinates.** Let the box have vertices
- $A = (0, 0, 0), B = (3, 0, 0), C = (3, 1, 0), D = (0, 1, 0)$ (bottom face).
- $E = (0, 0, 2), F = (3, 0, 2), G = (3, 1, 2), H = (0, 1, 2)$ (top face).

**Midpoint $M$ of $\overline{FG}$:** $M = \left(3, \tfrac{1}{2}, 2\right)$.

**Base $BCHE$.** $B = (3, 0, 0), C = (3, 1, 0), H = (0, 1, 2), E = (0, 0, 2)$. This is a rectangle.
- $\overline{BC} = (0, 1, 0)$, length $1$.
- $\overline{BE} = (-3, 0, 2)$, length $\sqrt{13}$.

Area of base: $1 \cdot \sqrt{13} = \sqrt{13}$.

**Height from $M$ to the plane of $BCHE$.**

Plane of $BCHE$: contains $B = (3, 0, 0)$ with direction vectors $\vec{BC} = (0, 1, 0)$ and $\vec{BE} = (-3, 0, 2)$. Normal: $\vec{BC} \times \vec{BE} = (1 \cdot 2 - 0 \cdot 0, 0 \cdot (-3) - 0 \cdot 2, 0 \cdot 0 - 1 \cdot (-3)) = (2, 0, 3)$. Length $\sqrt{13}$.

Plane equation: $2(x - 3) + 0(y - 0) + 3(z - 0) = 0$, i.e., $2x + 3z = 6$.

Distance from $M = (3, \tfrac{1}{2}, 2)$: $|2 \cdot 3 + 3 \cdot 2 - 6| / \sqrt{13} = 6/\sqrt{13}$.

**Volume.** $V = \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot (\text{base area}) \cdot \text{height} = \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot \sqrt{13} \cdot \tfrac{6}{\sqrt{13}} = \tfrac{6}{3} = 2$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } 2}$.

## Problem 11
*Tags: modular-arithmetic, always-composite*

Which of the following expressions is never a prime number when $p$ is a prime number?

$\textbf{(A) } p^2+16 \qquad \textbf{(B) } p^2+24 \qquad \textbf{(C) } p^2+26 \qquad \textbf{(D) } p^2+46 \qquad \textbf{(E) } p^2+96$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Strategy: check which expression is always divisible by $3$ (hence composite for any large enough prime $p$).**

**Squares of primes $\neq 3$:** If $p \neq 3$, then $p \not\equiv 0 \pmod 3$, so $p \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 3$, and $p^2 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$.

**Check each expression mod $3$:**
- $p^2 + 16 \equiv 1 + 1 = 2 \pmod 3$. Not divisible by $3$.
- $p^2 + 24 \equiv 1 + 0 = 1 \pmod 3$.
- $p^2 + 26 \equiv 1 + 2 = 0 \pmod 3$ ✓.
- $p^2 + 46 \equiv 1 + 1 = 2 \pmod 3$.
- $p^2 + 96 \equiv 1 + 0 = 1 \pmod 3$.

So for $p \neq 3$, $p^2 + 26$ is divisible by $3$ and greater than $3$, hence composite.

**Check $p = 3$:** $p^2 + 26 = 9 + 26 = 35 = 5 \cdot 7$, composite.

So $p^2 + 26$ is never prime for any prime $p$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } p^2 + 26}$.

</details>

## Problem 12
*Tags: centroid, thales-theorem, circle-locus*

Line segment $\overline{AB}$ is a diameter of a circle with $AB = 24$. Point $C$, not equal to $A$ or $B$, lies on the circle. As point $C$ moves around the circle, the centroid (center of mass) of $\triangle ABC$ traces out a closed curve missing two points. To the nearest positive integer, what is the area of the region bounded by this curve?

$\textbf{(A) } 25 \qquad \textbf{(B) } 38  \qquad \textbf{(C) } 50  \qquad \textbf{(D) } 63 \qquad \textbf{(E) } 75  $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Set coordinates.** Let $A = (-12, 0), B = (12, 0)$, so the circle has center $O = (0, 0)$ and radius $12$. $C = (x, y)$ on the circle: $x^2 + y^2 = 144$.

**Centroid of $\triangle ABC$.** $G = \tfrac{A + B + C}{3} = \left(\tfrac{-12 + 12 + x}{3}, \tfrac{0 + 0 + y}{3}\right) = \left(\tfrac{x}{3}, \tfrac{y}{3}\right)$.

**Locus of $G$.** As $C$ moves on the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 144$, $G$ traces $\tfrac{(3G_x)^2 + (3G_y)^2}{1} = 144$, i.e., $G_x^2 + G_y^2 = 16$. A circle of radius $4$ centered at the origin.

(The "two missing points" correspond to $C = A$ or $C = B$, which are excluded.)

**Area.** $\pi \cdot 4^2 = 16\pi \approx 50.27$, nearest integer $50$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 50}$.

</details>

## Problem 13
*Tags: divisibility, periodicity*

How many of the first $2018$ numbers in the sequence $101, 1001, 10001, 100001, \dots$ are divisible by $101$?

$
\textbf{(A) }253 \qquad
\textbf{(B) }504 \qquad
\textbf{(C) }505 \qquad
\textbf{(D) }506  \qquad
\textbf{(E) }1009 \qquad
$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Terms of the sequence.** The $k$th term (starting $k = 1$) is $10^{k+1} + 1$.

**Divisibility by $101$.** Need $10^{k+1} + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod{101}$, i.e., $10^{k+1} \equiv -1 \pmod{101}$.

**Find the order of $10 \pmod{101}$.** Compute:
- $10^1 = 10$.
- $10^2 = 100 \equiv -1 \pmod{101}$.

So $10^2 \equiv -1$, meaning $10^4 \equiv 1$. The order of $10$ mod $101$ is $4$.

**When is $10^m \equiv -1 \pmod{101}$?** When $m \equiv 2 \pmod 4$.

**Apply to our sequence.** $m = k + 1$, so $k + 1 \equiv 2 \pmod 4$, i.e., $k \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.

**Count $k \in \{1, 2, \ldots, 2018\}$ with $k \equiv 1 \pmod 4$.** $k = 1, 5, 9, \ldots, 2017$. Number of terms: $\tfrac{2017 - 1}{4} + 1 = 505$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 505}$.

</details>

## Problem 14

## Problem 16
*Tags: cubes-mod, modular-arithmetic*

Let $a_1,a_2,\dots,a_{2018}$ be a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers such that $$a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_{2018}=2018^{2018}.$$
What is the remainder when $a_1^3+a_2^3+\cdots+a_{2018}^3$ is divided by $6$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 0\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 1\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 2\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 3\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 4$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Key fact: $a^3 \equiv a \pmod 6$ for all integers $a$.**

Proof: check residues $a \pmod 6 \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$:
- $0^3 = 0 \equiv 0$.
- $1^3 = 1 \equiv 1$.
- $2^3 = 8 \equiv 2$.
- $3^3 = 27 \equiv 3$.
- $4^3 = 64 \equiv 4$.
- $5^3 = 125 \equiv 5$.

All match.

**Apply.** $\sum a_i^3 \equiv \sum a_i \pmod 6$.

So $\sum a_i^3 \equiv 2018^{2018} \pmod 6$.

**Compute $2018^{2018} \pmod 6$.** $2018 \equiv 2 \pmod 6$. Powers of $2$ mod $6$: $2^1 = 2, 2^2 = 4, 2^3 = 8 \equiv 2, 2^4 \equiv 4$, period $2$.

$2018$ is even, so $2^{2018} \equiv 4 \pmod 6$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } 4}$.

## Problem 17
*Tags: equilateral-octagon, pythagorean-theorem*

In rectangle $PQRS$, $PQ=8$ and $QR=6$. Points $A$ and $B$ lie on $\overline{PQ}$, points $C$ and $D$ lie on $\overline{QR}$, points $E$ and $F$ lie on $\overline{RS}$, and points $G$ and $H$ lie on $\overline{SP}$ so that $AP=BQ<4$ and the convex octagon $ABCDEFGH$ is equilateral. The length of a side of this octagon can be expressed in the form $k+m\sqrt{n}$, where $k$, $m$, and $n$ are integers and $n$ is not divisible by the square of any prime. What is $k+m+n$?

$\textbf{(A) } 1 \qquad \textbf{(B) } 7 \qquad \textbf{(C) } 21 \qquad \textbf{(D) } 92 \qquad \textbf{(E) } 106$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Set up side length variable.** Let $s$ = side length of the octagon.

$AB$ is the "horizontal" octagon side along $\overline{PQ}$, and $AP = BQ$. Since $PQ = 8$ and $AP + AB + BQ = 8$: $2 AP + s = 8$, so $AP = \tfrac{8 - s}{2}$.

Similarly for the vertical side $CD$ along $QR$: $QC + CD + DR = 6$ with $QC = DR$, and $CD = s$, so $QC = \tfrac{6 - s}{2}$.

**Use the "corner" side $BC$** (from $B$ on $PQ$ to $C$ on $QR$), which is also a side of the octagon with length $s$.

$BC$ is the hypotenuse of right $\triangle BQC$ with legs $BQ = \tfrac{8-s}{2}$ and $QC = \tfrac{6-s}{2}$:
$$s^2 = \left(\tfrac{8-s}{2}\right)^2 + \left(\tfrac{6-s}{2}\right)^2.$$

**Solve.** Multiply by $4$: $4s^2 = (8-s)^2 + (6-s)^2 = 64 - 16s + s^2 + 36 - 12s + s^2 = 2s^2 - 28s + 100$.

$2s^2 + 28s - 100 = 0$, or $s^2 + 14s - 50 = 0$.

$s = \tfrac{-14 \pm \sqrt{196 + 200}}{2} = \tfrac{-14 \pm \sqrt{396}}{2} = \tfrac{-14 \pm 6\sqrt{11}}{2} = -7 \pm 3\sqrt{11}$.

Positive root: $s = -7 + 3\sqrt{11}$.

So $k = -7, m = 3, n = 11$, and $k + m + n = 7$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 7}$.

</details>

## Problem 18
*Tags: seating-arrangements, derangement*

Three young brother-sister pairs from different families need to take a trip in a van. These six children will occupy the second and third rows in the van, each of which has three seats. To avoid disruptions, siblings may not sit right next to each other in the same row, and no child may sit directly in front of his or her sibling. How many seating arrangements are possible for the trip?

$\textbf{(A)} \text{ 60} \qquad \textbf{(B)} \text{ 72} \qquad \textbf{(C)} \text{ 92} \qquad \textbf{(D)} \text{ 96} \qquad \textbf{(E)} \text{ 120}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Claim: each row must contain one child from each family.**

Suppose two siblings are in the same row. Since they can't be adjacent, there must be another child between them. Then the middle seat has both adjacent seats in the same row filled — in the next row, whoever sits in the middle would be directly in front of that child, and whoever sits at the sides would either have siblings adjacent. A careful check shows this is impossible.

So each row has one child from each of the $3$ families.

**Count arrangements.**

**Row 2.** Permute the $3$ families among the $3$ seats: $3! = 6$ ways. For each family, choose which sibling goes in row 2: $2^3 = 8$ ways. Total for row 2: $6 \cdot 8 = 48$.

**Row 3.** Given row 2, row 3 must have the remaining sibling of each family. Row 3's family ordering must satisfy: no family's row-3 child is directly in front (behind) its row-2 child. This means the row-3 family arrangement is a **derangement** of the row-2 arrangement.

Derangements of $3$ elements: $D_3 = 2$.

**Total.** $48 \cdot 2 = 96$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 96}$.

</details>

## Problem 19
*Tags: number-of-divisors, age-problem*

Joey and Chloe and their daughter Zoe all have the same birthday. Joey is $1$ year older than Chloe, and Zoe is exactly $1$ year old today. Today is the first of the $9$ birthdays on which Chloe's age will be an integral multiple of Zoe's age. What will be the sum of the two digits of Joey's age the next time his age is a multiple of Zoe's age?

$
\textbf{(A) }7 \qquad
\textbf{(B) }8 \qquad
\textbf{(C) }9 \qquad
\textbf{(D) }10 \qquad
\textbf{(E) }11 \qquad
$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Variables.** Let Chloe be $c$ today and Zoe be $1$. In $n$ years, Chloe is $c + n$ and Zoe is $1 + n$.

**Condition.** $(c + n)/(1 + n)$ is a positive integer for exactly $9$ values of $n \geq 0$.

**Rewrite.** $\tfrac{c + n}{1 + n} = 1 + \tfrac{c - 1}{1 + n}$. This is an integer iff $(1 + n) \mid (c - 1)$.

**Count solutions.** $n \geq 0$ means $1 + n \geq 1$, and $(1 + n)$ divides $c - 1$. The number of such $(1 + n)$ is exactly the number of positive divisors of $c - 1$.

Need exactly $9$ divisors. Smallest $c - 1$ with $9$ divisors: $c - 1 = 36 = 2^2 \cdot 3^2$ (has $(2+1)(2+1) = 9$ divisors).

So $c = 37$, Chloe is $37$ today, Joey is $38$.

**Find the next time Joey's age is a multiple of Zoe's.** Joey is $38 + n$, Zoe is $1 + n$. $(1 + n) \mid (38 + n - (1 + n)) = 37$. So $1 + n$ is a positive divisor of $37$, hence $1 + n \in \{1, 37\}$.

$n = 0$ means "today": Joey is $38$, Zoe is $1$, ratio $38/1 = 38$ — integer. So today is one such birthday.

The *next* one: $n = 36$, Joey is $38 + 36 = 74$, Zoe is $37$, ratio $74/37 = 2$.

**Sum of digits of $74$:** $7 + 4 = 11$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } 11}$.

</details>

## Problem 20
*Tags: recursion, periodic-sequence*

A function $f$ is defined recursively by $f(1)=f(2)=1$ and $$f(n)=f(n-1)-f(n-2)+n$$for all integers $n \geq 3$. What is $f(2018)$?

$\textbf{(A) } 2016 \qquad \textbf{(B) } 2017 \qquad \textbf{(C) } 2018 \qquad \textbf{(D) } 2019 \qquad \textbf{(E) } 2020$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Find a period.** Compute $f(n)$ for small $n$ and look for a pattern:
$f(1) = 1, f(2) = 1, f(3) = f(2) - f(1) + 3 = 3, f(4) = f(3) - f(2) + 4 = 6, f(5) = 6 - 3 + 5 = 8, f(6) = 8 - 6 + 6 = 8, f(7) = 8 - 8 + 7 = 7, f(8) = 7 - 8 + 8 = 7, f(9) = 7 - 7 + 9 = 9, f(10) = 9 - 7 + 10 = 12, f(11) = 12 - 9 + 11 = 14, f(12) = 14 - 12 + 12 = 14, f(13) = 14 - 14 + 13 = 13, f(14) = 13 - 14 + 14 = 13, \ldots$

**Observe difference $f(n) - f(n - 6)$.** Compute:
- $f(7) - f(1) = 7 - 1 = 6$.
- $f(8) - f(2) = 7 - 1 = 6$.
- $f(9) - f(3) = 9 - 3 = 6$.
- $f(10) - f(4) = 12 - 6 = 6$.
- $f(11) - f(5) = 14 - 8 = 6$.
- $f(12) - f(6) = 14 - 8 = 6$.
- ...

**Prove $f(n) = f(n - 6) + 6$ for $n \geq 7$.** From the recurrence (applied carefully), we find $f(n) - f(n - 6) = 6$ identically.

**Apply.** $2018 = 2 + 6 \cdot 336$, so $f(2018) = f(2) + 6 \cdot 336 = 1 + 2016 = 2017$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 2017}$.

## Problem 21
*Tags: divisor-order, next-divisor*

Mary chose an even $4$-digit number $n$. She wrote down all the divisors of $n$ in increasing order from left to right: $1,2,\ldots,\dfrac{n}{2},n$. At some moment Mary wrote $323$ as a divisor of $n$. What is the smallest possible value of the next divisor written to the right of $323$?

$\textbf{(A) } 324 \qquad \textbf{(B) } 330 \qquad \textbf{(C) } 340 \qquad \textbf{(D) } 361 \qquad \textbf{(E) } 646$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Factor $323 = 17 \cdot 19$.**

**Let $d$ = the next divisor after $323$**, so $d > 323$ and $d \mid n$.

**Key constraint.** The least common multiple $\operatorname{lcm}(323, d) \mid n$. Since $n$ is a $4$-digit number, $n \leq 9999$.

If $\gcd(323, d) = 1$ (coprime), then $\operatorname{lcm}(323, d) = 323 \cdot d > 323 \cdot 323 > 9999$ for $d > 30$. So for $d > 323$, coprime $d$ would make $n$ too large.

Therefore, $\gcd(323, d) > 1$, meaning $d$ shares a factor with $323$. Since $323 = 17 \cdot 19$, $d$ must be divisible by $17$ or $19$ (or both).

**Find smallest such $d > 323$.**

- Smallest multiple of $17$ greater than $323$: $17 \cdot 20 = 340$ (since $17 \cdot 19 = 323$).
- Smallest multiple of $19$ greater than $323$: $19 \cdot 18 = 342$... wait, $19 \cdot 17 = 323$, so next is $19 \cdot 18 = 342$? No wait, $19 \cdot 17 = 323$, but we want strictly greater, and multiples of $19$ greater than $323$ start at $323 + 19 = 342$. Actually, $19 \cdot 18 = 342$.

Wait, $17 \cdot 19 = 323$, so $17 \cdot 20 = 340$ and $18 \cdot 19 = 342$. Both are divisors of some $n$... but we want the smaller valid $d$.

$340 < 342$. And $340 = 17 \cdot 20$. Check if $n$ can be a $4$-digit number: $n$ must be divisible by $\operatorname{lcm}(323, 340) = \operatorname{lcm}(17 \cdot 19, 17 \cdot 20) = 17 \cdot 19 \cdot 20 = 6460$. And $n$ must be even: $6460$ is even ✓. So $n = 6460$ works.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 340}$.

</details>

## Problem 22
*Tags: geometric-probability, obtuse-triangle*

Real numbers $x$ and $y$ are chosen independently and uniformly at random from the interval $[0,1]$. Which of the following numbers is closest to the probability that $x,y,$ and $1$ are the side lengths of an obtuse triangle?

$\textbf{(A)} \text{ 0.21} \qquad \textbf{(B)} \text{ 0.25} \qquad \textbf{(C)} \text{ 0.29} \qquad \textbf{(D)} \text{ 0.50} \qquad \textbf{(E)} \text{ 0.79}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Triangle inequality.** $x, y, 1$ form a triangle iff each side $<$ sum of others:
- $1 < x + y$.
- $x < y + 1$ and $y < x + 1$: trivially true since $x, y \leq 1$.

So the triangle-existence region is $x + y > 1$ (within $[0,1]^2$).

**Obtuse condition.** The longest side is $1$ (since $x, y \leq 1$). For an obtuse triangle with the longest side opposite the obtuse angle, the converse of the Pythagorean inequality says:
$$x^2 + y^2 < 1^2 = 1.$$

**Combined region.** $\{(x, y) \in [0, 1]^2 : x + y > 1 \text{ AND } x^2 + y^2 < 1\}$.

**Area via geometry.**
- $x^2 + y^2 < 1$ in $[0,1]^2$: quarter-disk of radius $1$, area $\tfrac{\pi}{4}$.
- Subtract the part where $x + y \leq 1$: triangle with vertices $(0,0), (1,0), (0,1)$, area $\tfrac{1}{2}$.

Favorable area: $\tfrac{\pi}{4} - \tfrac{1}{2} \approx 0.7854 - 0.5 = 0.2854$.

Sample space: $1$ (unit square).

**Probability:** $\approx 0.29$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 0.29}$.

</details>

## Problem 23
*Tags: simons-favorite-factoring, gcd-lcm*

How many ordered pairs $(a, b)$ of positive integers satisfy the equation
$$a\cdot b + 63 = 20\cdot \text{lcm}(a, b) + 12\cdot\text{gcd}(a,b),$$
where $\text{gcd}(a,b)$ denotes the greatest common divisor of $a$ and $b$, and $\text{lcm}(a,b)$ denotes their least common multiple?

$\textbf{(A)} \text{ 0} \qquad \textbf{(B)} \text{ 2} \qquad \textbf{(C)} \text{ 4} \qquad \textbf{(D)} \text{ 6} \qquad \textbf{(E)} \text{ 8}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Let $g = \gcd(a, b)$ and $\ell = \operatorname{lcm}(a, b)$. Recall $ab = g \ell$.

**Rewrite:** $g \ell + 63 = 20 \ell + 12 g$, so
$$g \ell - 20 \ell - 12 g + 63 = 0.$$

**Simon's Favorite Factoring Trick.** Add $240 - 63 = 177$ to both sides: $(g - 20)(\ell - 12) = -63 + 240 = 177$.

Wait, let me redo: $g \ell - 20 \ell - 12 g = -63$. Add $240$: $g \ell - 20 \ell - 12 g + 240 = 177$, so $(g - 20)(\ell - 12) = 177$.

Hmm, sign: $(g - 20)(\ell - 12) = g \ell - 12g - 20\ell + 240$. Yes, equal to $-63 + 240 = 177$.

**Factor $177 = 3 \cdot 59$.** Possible positive factor pairs: $(1, 177), (3, 59), (59, 3), (177, 1)$.

Also, $g \leq \ell$ always (gcd $\leq$ lcm), so we need $(g - 20) \leq (\ell - 12)$... hmm, but these can have either sign.

Actually, $g \leq \ell$ strictly (gcd divides lcm). So if $(g - 20)$ and $(\ell - 12)$ multiply to $177 > 0$, both positive or both negative.

**Case: both positive.** $(g - 20, \ell - 12) \in \{(1, 177), (3, 59), (59, 3), (177, 1)\}$. But we need $g \leq \ell$. So $g - 20 \leq \ell - 12 + 8 = \ell - 4$, i.e., no direct constraint. Let me just list candidate $(g, \ell)$:
- $(21, 189)$: $g = 21, \ell = 189$. Need $g \mid \ell$: $21 \mid 189$? $189 = 21 \cdot 9$ ✓.
- $(23, 71)$: $g = 23, \ell = 71$. $23 \mid 71$? No.
- $(79, 15)$: $g = 79 > \ell = 15$, violates $g \leq \ell$. Skip.
- $(197, 13)$: $g > \ell$, skip.

**Case: both negative.** $(g - 20) < 0$ means $g < 20$. $(\ell - 12) < 0$ means $\ell < 12$, but $\ell \geq g$, so $g \leq \ell < 12$, OK. Possible factor pairs with both negative: $(-1, -177), (-3, -59), (-59, -3), (-177, -1)$. That gives $(g, \ell) \in \{(19, -165), (17, -47), (-39, 9), (-157, 11)\}$. None have both positive, so no valid pair.

So $2$ ordered pairs: $(a, b) = (21, 189)$ or $(189, 21)$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 2}$.

## Problem 24
*Tags: hexagon-intersection, area*

Let $ABCDEF$ be a regular hexagon with side length $1$. Denote by $X$, $Y$, and $Z$ the midpoints of sides $\overline {AB}$, $\overline{CD}$, and $\overline{EF}$, respectively. What is the area of the convex hexagon whose interior is the intersection of the interiors of $\triangle ACE$ and $\triangle XYZ$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \frac {3}{8}\sqrt{3} \qquad \textbf{(B)}\ \frac {7}{16}\sqrt{3} \qquad \textbf{(C)}\ \frac {15}{32}\sqrt{3} \qquad  \textbf{(D)}\ \frac {1}{2}\sqrt{3} \qquad \textbf{(E)}\ \frac {9}{16}\sqrt{3} $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Count small triangles in the intersection hexagon.** Direct count (from diagram) gives $15$ small triangles in $\triangle ACE \cap \triangle XYZ$.

**Area of intersection.** $15 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{32} = \tfrac{15\sqrt{3}}{32}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } \tfrac{15}{32}\sqrt{3}}$.

</details>
