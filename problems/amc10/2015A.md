## Problem 1
*Tags: order-of-operations*

What is the value of $(2^0-1+5^2-0)^{-1}\times5?$

$ \textbf{(A)}\ -125\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ -120\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \frac{1}{5}\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \frac{5}{24}\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 25$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Simplify inside the parentheses.** $2^0 = 1$ and $5^2 = 25$, so
$$2^0 - 1 + 5^2 - 0 = 1 - 1 + 25 - 0 = 25.$$

**Apply the inverse, then multiply by $5$.** $25^{-1} \cdot 5 = \tfrac{5}{25} = \tfrac{1}{5}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } \tfrac{1}{5}}$.

</details>

## Problem 2
*Tags: systems-of-equations*

A box contains a collection of triangular and square tiles. There are $25$ tiles in the box, containing $84$ edges total. How many square tiles are there in the box?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 3\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 5\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 7\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 9\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 11$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Let $t$ = number of triangles, $s$ = number of squares. Triangles have $3$ edges, squares have $4$.
$$t + s = 25, \qquad 3t + 4s = 84.$$

**Solve.** Multiply the first equation by $3$: $3t + 3s = 75$. Subtract from the second: $s = 9$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 9}$.

</details>

## Problem 3
*Tags: pattern-recognition, staircase*

Ann made a $3$-step staircase using $18$ toothpicks as shown in the figure. How many toothpicks does she need to add to complete a $5$-step staircase?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 9\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 18\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 20\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 22\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 24$

_(Asymptote diagram omitted — see AoPS for image)_

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Find the pattern.** Count toothpicks for small staircases:
- $1$-step staircase: $4$ toothpicks (a single square).
- $2$-step staircase: $10$ toothpicks.
- $3$-step staircase: $18$ toothpicks.

Differences: $10 - 4 = 6$, $18 - 10 = 8$. So going from $n$-step to $(n+1)$-step adds $2n + 4$ toothpicks (since $6 = 2 \cdot 1 + 4$ and $8 = 2 \cdot 2 + 4$).

**Continue the pattern.**
- From $3$ to $4$ steps: add $2 \cdot 3 + 4 = 10$.
- From $4$ to $5$ steps: add $2 \cdot 4 + 4 = 12$.

**Total added.** $10 + 12 = 22$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 22}$.

</details>

## Problem 4
*Tags: ratios, equal-sharing*

Pablo, Sofia, and Mia got some candy eggs at a party.  Pablo had three times as many eggs as Sofia, and Sofia had twice as many eggs as Mia.  Pablo decides to give some of his eggs to Sofia and Mia so that all three will have the same number of eggs.  What fraction of his eggs should Pablo give to Sofia?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ \frac{1}{12}\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \frac{1}{6}\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \frac{1}{4}\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \frac{1}{3}\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \frac{1}{2}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Assign a variable.** Let Mia have $m$ eggs. Then Sofia has $2m$, Pablo has $6m$. Total: $9m$ eggs.

**Target.** For equal shares, each person needs $\tfrac{9m}{3} = 3m$ eggs.

**What Pablo gives.** Pablo has $6m$ and needs to keep $3m$, so he gives away $3m$ total. Sofia needs $3m - 2m = m$ more, and Mia needs $3m - m = 2m$ more. ✓ ($m + 2m = 3m$.)

**Fraction given to Sofia.** $\tfrac{m}{6m} = \tfrac{1}{6}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } \tfrac{1}{6}}$.

</details>

## Problem 5
*Tags: mean, sum-of-values*

Mr. Patrick teaches math to $ 15 $ students. He was grading tests and found that when he graded everyone's test except Payton's, the average grade for the class was $ 80 $. After he graded Payton's test, the test average became $ 81 $. What was Payton's score on the test?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 81\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 85\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 91\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 94\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 95 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Sum of first $14$ scores.** Mean $= 80$ over $14$ students, so total $= 14 \cdot 80 = 1120$.

**Sum of all $15$ scores.** Mean $= 81$ over $15$, so total $= 15 \cdot 81 = 1215$.

**Payton's score.** $1215 - 1120 = 95$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } 95}$.

</details>

## Problem 6
*Tags: ratios*

The sum of two positive numbers is $ 5 $ times their difference. What is the ratio of the larger number to the smaller number?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ \frac{5}{4}\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \frac{3}{2}\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \frac{9}{5}\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 2 \qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \frac{5}{2} $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Set up.** Let the larger number be $a$, smaller be $b$ (both positive, $a > b$). Given:
$$a + b = 5(a - b).$$

**Solve.** Expand: $a + b = 5a - 5b$, so $6b = 4a$, giving $\tfrac{a}{b} = \tfrac{6}{4} = \tfrac{3}{2}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } \tfrac{3}{2}}$.

</details>

## Problem 7
*Tags: arithmetic-sequence-counting*

How many terms are in the arithmetic sequence $13$, $16$, $19$, $\dotsc$, $70$, $73$?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 20 \qquad\textbf{(B)} \ 21 \qquad\textbf{(C)} \ 24 \qquad\textbf{(D)} \ 60 \qquad\textbf{(E)} \ 61 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**General term.** The $n$-th term is $a_n = 13 + 3(n - 1)$ (common difference $3$, first term $13$).

**Solve $a_n = 73$.**
$$13 + 3(n - 1) = 73 \implies 3(n - 1) = 60 \implies n - 1 = 20 \implies n = 21.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 21}$.

</details>

## Problem 8
*Tags: age-problems, linear-equations*

Two years ago Pete was three times as old as his cousin Claire. Two years before that, Pete was four times as old as Claire. In how many years will the ratio of their ages be $2$ : $1$ ?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 2 \qquad\textbf{(B)} \ 4 \qquad\textbf{(C)} \ 5 \qquad\textbf{(D)} \ 6 \qquad\textbf{(E)} \ 8 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Set up equations.** Let $p, c$ be Pete's and Claire's current ages. The two clues:
- Two years ago: $p - 2 = 3(c - 2)$, so $p = 3c - 4$.
- Four years ago: $p - 4 = 4(c - 4)$, so $p = 4c - 12$.

**Solve.** $3c - 4 = 4c - 12 \implies c = 8$, and $p = 3(8) - 4 = 20$.

**Find when ratio is $2:1$.** We want $x$ years from now with $p + x = 2(c + x)$:
$$20 + x = 2(8 + x) = 16 + 2x \implies x = 4.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 4}$.

</details>

## Problem 9
*Tags: cylinder-volume, percentage-change*

Two right circular cylinders have the same volume. The radius of the second cylinder is $10\%$ more than the radius of the first. What is the relationship between the heights of the two cylinders?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ \text{The second height is } 10\% \text{ less than the first.} \\
\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \text{The first height is } 10\% \text{ more than the second.} \\
\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \text{The second height is } 21\% \text{ less than the first.} \\
\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \text{The first height is } 21\% \text{ more than the second.} \\
\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \text{The second height is } 80\% \text{ of the first.} $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Cylinder $1$: radius $r$, height $h_1$. Cylinder $2$: radius $1.1 r$, height $h_2$. Same volume:
$$\pi r^2 h_1 = \pi (1.1 r)^2 h_2 = 1.21 \pi r^2 h_2.$$

**Solve.** $h_1 = 1.21 h_2$.

**Interpret.** The first height is $1.21$ times the second — $21\%$ more than the second.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D)}}$.

</details>

## Problem 10
*Tags: permutation-constraints, adjacency*

How many rearrangements of $abcd$ are there in which no two adjacent letters are also adjacent letters in the alphabet?  For example, no such rearrangements could include either $ab$ or $ba$.

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 0\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 1\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 2\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 3\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 4$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Forbidden adjacencies.** We cannot place $a$ next to $b$, $b$ next to $c$, or $c$ next to $d$. Build a "compatibility graph" on $\{a, b, c, d\}$ where edges connect letters that *can* be adjacent:
- $a$ and $c$: compatible.
- $a$ and $d$: compatible.
- $b$ and $d$: compatible.
- $a$-$b$, $b$-$c$, $c$-$d$: forbidden.

Graph: $c - a - d - b$ (a path of length $3$, connecting $c, a, d, b$ in that order).

**Find Hamiltonian paths in this compatibility graph.** A valid rearrangement is a Hamiltonian path. The graph is the path $c - a - d - b$, which has exactly $2$ Hamiltonian paths (the path itself and its reverse): $cadb$ and $bdac$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 2}$.

</details>

## Problem 12
*Tags: substitution, perfect-square*

Points $( \sqrt{\pi} , a)$ and $( \sqrt{\pi} , b)$ are distinct points on the graph of $y^2 + x^4 = 2x^2 y + 1$. What is $|a-b|$?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 1 \qquad\textbf{(B)} \ \frac{\pi}{2} \qquad\textbf{(C)} \ 2 \qquad\textbf{(D)} \ \sqrt{1+\pi} \qquad\textbf{(E)} \ 1 + \sqrt{\pi} $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Rearrange.** The equation $y^2 + x^4 = 2 x^2 y + 1$ becomes
$$y^2 - 2 x^2 y + x^4 = 1 \implies (y - x^2)^2 = 1 \implies y - x^2 = \pm 1.$$

**Plug in $x = \sqrt{\pi}$.** $x^2 = \pi$, so $y = \pi + 1$ or $y = \pi - 1$.

**Compute $|a - b|$.** $|(\pi + 1) - (\pi - 1)| = 2$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 2}$.

</details>

## Problem 14
*Tags: coin-rotation-paradox, rolling-circles*

The diagram below shows the circular face of a clock with radius $20$ cm and a circular disk with radius $10$ cm externally tangent to the clock face at $12$ o' clock. The disk has an arrow painted on it, initially pointing in the upward vertical direction. Let the disk roll clockwise around the clock face. At what point on the clock face will the disk be tangent when the arrow is next pointing in the upward vertical direction?

_(Asymptote diagram omitted — see AoPS for image)_

$ \textbf{(A) }\text{2 o' clock} \qquad\textbf{(B) }\text{3 o' clock} \qquad\textbf{(C) }\text{4 o' clock} \qquad\textbf{(D) }\text{6 o' clock} \qquad\textbf{(E) }\text{8 o' clock}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Coin rotation paradox.** When a disk rolls around the outside of a circle without slipping, the total number of full rotations the disk makes (as seen from a fixed frame) is
$$\frac{(\text{length of path of disk center})}{(\text{circumference of disk})}.$$
The path of the disk's center is a circle of radius $20 + 10 = 30$, so its length is $2 \pi \cdot 30 = 60 \pi$.

**Rotations per full revolution around the clock.** $\tfrac{60 \pi}{2 \pi \cdot 10} = 3$. So the disk makes $3$ full rotations while going all the way around the clock.

**Where does it return to "arrow up" for the first time?** After $1$ full rotation of the disk, the arrow points up again. That's $\tfrac{1}{3}$ of the way around the clock — i.e., $\tfrac{12}{3} = 4$ hours past $12$, which is $4$ o'clock.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 4 \text{ o'clock}}$.

</details>

## Problem 15
*Tags: simons-favorite-factoring, coprime-solutions*

Consider the set of all fractions $\frac{x}{y}$, where $x$ and $y$ are relatively prime positive integers. How many of these fractions have the property that if both numerator and denominator are increased by $1$, the value of the fraction is increased by $10\%$?

$\textbf{(A) }0\qquad\textbf{(B) }1\qquad\textbf{(C) }2\qquad\textbf{(D) }3\qquad\textbf{(E) }\text{infinitely many}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Translate the condition.** "$10\%$ increase" means the new fraction is $\tfrac{11}{10}$ times the old:
$$\frac{x + 1}{y + 1} = \frac{11 x}{10 y}.$$

**Clear fractions.** $10y(x + 1) = 11x(y + 1)$, so $10xy + 10y = 11xy + 11x$, giving
$$-xy + 10y - 11x = 0 \implies xy + 11x - 10y = 0.$$

**Simon's Favorite Factoring Trick.** $xy + 11x - 10y = 0 \implies x(y + 11) - 10(y + 11) + 110 = 0 \implies (x - 10)(y + 11) = -110$.

**Enumerate.** We need $x, y > 0$ with $\gcd(x, y) = 1$. Since $y + 11 > 11 > 0$ and the product is $-110$, we need $x - 10 < 0$, i.e., $x < 10$.

Pairs $(x - 10, y + 11)$ with product $-110$ and $y + 11 \ge 12$ (so $y \ge 1$):
- $(-1, 110)$: $x = 9, y = 99$. $\gcd(9, 99) = 9 \neq 1$. Reject.
- $(-2, 55)$: $x = 8, y = 44$. $\gcd(8, 44) = 4$. Reject.
- $(-5, 22)$: $x = 5, y = 11$. $\gcd(5, 11) = 1$. ✓
- $(-10, 11)$: $x = 0$. Not positive. Reject.

(Pairs with $|x - 10| > 10$ would make $x$ non-positive.)

**Only one valid fraction.** $\tfrac{5}{11}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 1}$.

</details>

## Problem 16
*Tags: symmetric-system, algebraic-manipulation*

If $y+4 = (x-2)^2, x+4 = (y-2)^2$, and $x \neq y$, what is the value of $x^2+y^2$?

$ \textbf{(A) }10\qquad\textbf{(B) }15\qquad\textbf{(C) }20\qquad\textbf{(D) }25\qquad\textbf{(E) }\text{30} $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Expand both equations.**
- $(x - 2)^2 = y + 4 \implies x^2 - 4x + 4 = y + 4 \implies x^2 - 4x = y$.
- $(y - 2)^2 = x + 4 \implies y^2 - 4y = x$.

**Add the two.** $x^2 + y^2 - 4x - 4y = y + x$, so
$$x^2 + y^2 = 5x + 5y = 5(x + y). \qquad (*)$$

**Subtract the two.** $x^2 - y^2 - 4x + 4y = y - x$, so
$$(x - y)(x + y) - 4(x - y) = -(x - y).$$
Factor $x - y$ out of all terms:
$$(x - y)\big[(x + y) - 4 + 1\big] = 0 \implies (x - y)(x + y - 3) = 0.$$
Since $x \neq y$, we get $x + y = 3$.

**Plug into $(*)$.** $x^2 + y^2 = 5 \cdot 3 = 15$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 15}$.

</details>

## Problem 17
*Tags: equilateral-triangle, coordinate-geometry*

A line that passes through the origin intersects both the line $ x = 1$ and the line $y=1+ \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3} x$. The three lines create an equilateral triangle. What is the perimeter of the triangle?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 2\sqrt{6} \qquad\textbf{(B)} \ 2+2\sqrt{3} \qquad\textbf{(C)} \ 6 \qquad\textbf{(D)} \ 3 + 2\sqrt{3} \qquad\textbf{(E)} \ 6 + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3} $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Identify the slopes.** The line $y = 1 + \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3} x$ has slope $\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3} = \tan 30^\circ$, so it makes a $30^\circ$ angle with the horizontal ($60^\circ$ with the vertical line $x = 1$). The line $x = 1$ is vertical.

**Symmetry.** For the triangle to be equilateral with one side vertical ($x = 1$), the other two sides must be symmetric about the horizontal (the triangle has a horizontal axis of symmetry). So the third line (through the origin) must have slope $-\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$ (mirror of the given line through the midline):
$$y = -\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3} x.$$

**Find vertices on $x = 1$.**
- Intersection with $y = 1 + \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3} x$: at $x = 1$, $y = 1 + \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$.
- Intersection with $y = -\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3} x$: at $x = 1$, $y = -\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$.

**Length of the vertical side.**
$$\left(1 + \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\right) - \left(-\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\right) = 1 + \tfrac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}.$$

**Perimeter (equilateral).**
$$3 \left(1 + \tfrac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}\right) = 3 + 2\sqrt{3}.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 3 + 2\sqrt{3}}$.

</details>

## Problem 18
*Tags: base-conversion, digit-counting*

Hexadecimal (base-16) numbers are written using numeric digits $0$ through $9$ as well as the letters $A$ through $F$ to represent $10$ through $15$. Among the first $1000$ positive integers, there are $n$ whose hexadecimal representation contains only numeric digits. What is the sum of the digits of $n$?

$ \textbf{(A) }17\qquad\textbf{(B) }18\qquad\textbf{(C) }19\qquad\textbf{(D) }20\qquad\textbf{(E) }21 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Convert $1000$ to hexadecimal.** $1000 = 3 \cdot 256 + 232 = 3 \cdot 256 + 14 \cdot 16 + 8 = 3 \mathrm{E} 8_{16}$.

**Count positive integers $\le 1000$ whose hex representation uses only digits $0$–$9$.** These are integers in hex whose every digit is in $\{0, 1, \ldots, 9\}$ (no $\mathrm{A}$–$\mathrm{F}$).

**Group by number of hex digits.**
- $1$-hex-digit (values $1$ to $9$): $9$ numbers.
- $2$-hex-digits (values $16$ to $255$ in decimal, i.e., $10_{16}$ to $\mathrm{FF}_{16}$): each of the $2$ hex digits is in $\{0, \ldots, 9\}$, but the leading digit must be nonzero. So first digit $\in \{1, \ldots, 9\}$ ($9$ choices), second $\in \{0, \ldots, 9\}$ ($10$ choices): $90$ numbers.
- $3$-hex-digits up to $1000 = 3\mathrm{E}8_{16}$: leading hex digit must be in $\{1, 2, 3\}$ (to stay $\le 3\mathrm{E}8$, and nonzero). If leading digit is $1$ or $2$, other two digits are free in $\{0, \ldots, 9\}$: $2 \cdot 10 \cdot 10 = 200$ numbers. If leading digit is $3$: then we need the $3$-digit hex number $\le 3\mathrm{E}8_{16}$. The second digit is in $\{0, \ldots, 9\}$ (since $\mathrm{E} = 14 > 9$, all $10$ values $0$–$9$ are $< \mathrm{E}$), so second digit choices: $10$. Third digit $\in \{0, \ldots, 9\}$: $10$ choices. That's $10 \cdot 10 = 100$ numbers starting with $3$. Total $3$-digit: $200 + 100 = 300$.

**Sum.** $9 + 90 + 300 = 399$.

**Digit sum of $n = 399$.** $3 + 9 + 9 = 21$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } 21}$.

</details>

## Problem 19
*Tags: 30-60-90-triangle, 45-45-90-triangle, angle-trisector*

The isosceles right triangle $ABC$ has right angle at $C$ and area $12.5$. The rays trisecting $\angle ACB$ intersect $AB$ at $D$ and $E$. What is the area of $\bigtriangleup CDE$?

$ \textbf{(A) }\dfrac{5\sqrt{2}}{3}\qquad\textbf{(B) }\dfrac{50\sqrt{3}-75}{4}\qquad\textbf{(C) }\dfrac{15\sqrt{3}}{8}\qquad\textbf{(D) }\dfrac{50-25\sqrt{3}}{2}\qquad\textbf{(E) }\dfrac{25}{6} $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Find the leg length.** Right isosceles triangle with legs $s$ has area $\tfrac{s^2}{2} = 12.5$, so $s = 5$. Thus $CA = CB = 5$ and $AB = 5\sqrt{2}$.

**Drop the altitude from $C$ to $AB$, foot $F$.** $CF = \tfrac{5}{\sqrt{2}} = \tfrac{5\sqrt{2}}{2}$, and $F$ is the midpoint of $AB$. Note $\angle ACF = \angle BCF = 45^\circ$.

**Trisectors.** The trisectors of $\angle ACB = 90^\circ$ are the two rays at $30^\circ$ and $60^\circ$ from $CA$ (equivalently, $60^\circ$ and $30^\circ$ from $CB$). So $\angle ACD = 30^\circ$ and $\angle BCE = 30^\circ$, meaning $\angle DCE = 30^\circ$. By symmetry, $D$ and $E$ are symmetric about $F$, and $\angle DCF = \angle ECF = 15^\circ$.

**Find $DF$.** In right triangle $CFD$ with right angle at $F$, $CF = \tfrac{5\sqrt{2}}{2}$, and $\angle DCF = 15^\circ$. So
$$DF = CF \cdot \tan 15^\circ = \tfrac{5\sqrt{2}}{2} \tan 15^\circ.$$

**Compute $\tan 15^\circ$.** $\tan 15^\circ = \tan(45^\circ - 30^\circ) = \tfrac{\tan 45^\circ - \tan 30^\circ}{1 + \tan 45^\circ \tan 30^\circ} = \tfrac{1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}}{1 + \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}} = \tfrac{\sqrt{3} - 1}{\sqrt{3} + 1}$. Rationalize:
$$\tan 15^\circ = \tfrac{(\sqrt{3} - 1)^2}{(\sqrt{3} + 1)(\sqrt{3} - 1)} = \tfrac{4 - 2\sqrt{3}}{2} = 2 - \sqrt{3}.$$

**So $DF = \tfrac{5\sqrt{2}}{2}(2 - \sqrt{3})$.** And $DE = 2 \cdot DF = 5\sqrt{2}(2 - \sqrt{3})$.

**Area of $\triangle CDE$** (base $DE$, height $CF$):
$$[CDE] = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot DE \cdot CF = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 5\sqrt{2}(2 - \sqrt{3}) \cdot \tfrac{5\sqrt{2}}{2} = \tfrac{5\sqrt{2} \cdot 5\sqrt{2}}{4} (2 - \sqrt{3}) = \tfrac{50}{4}(2 - \sqrt{3}) = \tfrac{25}{2}(2 - \sqrt{3}) = \tfrac{50 - 25\sqrt{3}}{2}.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } \tfrac{50 - 25\sqrt{3}}{2}}$.

</details>

## Problem 20
*Tags: simons-favorite-factoring, rectangle-area-perimeter*

A rectangle with positive integer side lengths in $\text{cm}$ has area $A$ $\text{cm}^2$ and perimeter $P$ $\text{cm}$. Which of the following numbers cannot equal $A+P$?

$ \textbf{(A) }100\qquad\textbf{(B) }102\qquad\textbf{(C) }104\qquad\textbf{(D) }106\qquad\textbf{(E) }108 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Let the sides be positive integers $a, b$. Then $A = ab$ and $P = 2a + 2b$. So
$$A + P = ab + 2a + 2b = (a + 2)(b + 2) - 4.$$

**Rearranging.** $A + P + 4 = (a + 2)(b + 2)$. Each factor $a + 2, b + 2 \ge 3$ (since $a, b \ge 1$), so $A + P + 4$ must factor as a product of two integers both $\ge 3$.

**Check each choice.** $A + P + 4$ must be factorable as a product of two factors $\ge 3$.
- $100 + 4 = 104 = 8 \cdot 13$: both $\ge 3$. ✓
- $102 + 4 = 106 = 2 \cdot 53$: $53$ is prime, and the only factorizations are $1 \cdot 106$ and $2 \cdot 53$ — neither has both factors $\ge 3$. ✗
- $104 + 4 = 108 = 4 \cdot 27 = 6 \cdot 18 = \ldots$: ✓
- $106 + 4 = 110 = 10 \cdot 11$: ✓
- $108 + 4 = 112 = 7 \cdot 16$: ✓

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 102}$.

</details>

## Problem 21
*Tags: tetrahedron, 3d-geometry*

Tetrahedron $ABCD$ has $AB=5$, $AC=3$, $BC=4$, $BD=4$, $AD=3$, and $CD=\tfrac{12}5\sqrt2$.  What is the volume of the tetrahedron?

$\textbf{(A) }3\sqrt2\qquad\textbf{(B) }2\sqrt5\qquad\textbf{(C) }\dfrac{24}5\qquad\textbf{(D) }3\sqrt3\qquad\textbf{(E) }\dfrac{24}5\sqrt2$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Key observation.** Both $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle ABD$ are $3$-$4$-$5$ right triangles — the right angle is at $C$ in the first (since $3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2$) and at $D$ in the second (since $AD^2 + BD^2 = 3^2 + 4^2 = 5^2 = AB^2$).

**Drop perpendiculars from $C$ and $D$ to $AB$.** In $\triangle ABC$, the altitude from $C$ to hypotenuse $AB$ has length $\tfrac{3 \cdot 4}{5} = \tfrac{12}{5}$, and the foot is at a specific point $T$ on $AB$ (distance $\tfrac{9}{5}$ from $A$). Similarly in $\triangle ABD$, the altitude from $D$ to $AB$ has length $\tfrac{12}{5}$, foot also at $T$ (by symmetry: the foot divides the hypotenuse into segments proportional to the adjacent leg-squares, and both triangles have the same leg arrangement from $A$). So $CT = DT = \tfrac{12}{5}$, with $T \in AB$.

**Use the given $CD$ to find the angle between the planes.** In triangle $CTD$, $CT = DT = \tfrac{12}{5}$ and $CD = \tfrac{12}{5}\sqrt{2}$. Check: $CT^2 + DT^2 = 2 \cdot (12/5)^2 = (12/5)^2 \cdot 2 = ((12/5)\sqrt{2})^2 = CD^2$. So $\triangle CTD$ is a right isosceles triangle with right angle at $T$. This means $CT \perp DT$, i.e., the two altitudes (and hence the two planes $ABC$ and $ABD$) are perpendicular along $AB$.

**Compute the volume.** Treat $\triangle ABC$ as the base, lying in a horizontal plane. Then plane $ABD$ is vertical (perpendicular to base plane), so the height from $D$ to base plane equals $DT = \tfrac{12}{5}$.

Base area: $[\triangle ABC] = \tfrac{1}{2}(3)(4) = 6$.

Volume:
$$V = \tfrac{1}{3} \cdot 6 \cdot \tfrac{12}{5} = \tfrac{24}{5}.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } \tfrac{24}{5}}$.

</details>

## Problem 22
*Tags: circular-arrangements, casework*

Eight people are sitting around a circular table, each holding a fair coin. All eight people flip their coins and those who flip heads stand while those who flip tails remain seated. What is the probability that no two adjacent people will stand?

$\textbf{(A)}\dfrac{47}{256}\qquad\textbf{(B)}\dfrac{3}{16}\qquad\textbf{(C) }\dfrac{49}{256}\qquad\textbf{(D) }\dfrac{25}{128}\qquad\textbf{(E) }\dfrac{51}{256} $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Each of the $8$ people flips independently H or T. Total outcomes: $2^8 = 256$. We want to count outcomes with no two adjacent H's (around a cycle).

**Count by number of H's.** Let $k$ be the number of H's (standing people); $k$ can be $0, 1, 2, 3, 4$. (More would force adjacency on an $8$-cycle.)

*$k = 0$:* $1$ way (all T's).

*$k = 1$:* $\binom{8}{1} = 8$ ways.

*$k = 2$:* Choose $2$ of $8$ seats with no two adjacent on a cycle. Total pairs $\binom{8}{2} = 28$; adjacent pairs on an $8$-cycle: $8$. Non-adjacent: $28 - 8 = 20$.

*$k = 3$:* Arrangements of $3$ H's in $8$ seats cyclically with no two adjacent. A standard formula: $\tfrac{n}{n - k}\binom{n - k}{k} = \tfrac{8}{5}\binom{5}{3} = \tfrac{8}{5} \cdot 10 = 16$.

*$k = 4$:* Same formula: $\tfrac{8}{4}\binom{4}{4} = 2 \cdot 1 = 2$. (These are the two alternating patterns.)

**Total favorable.** $1 + 8 + 20 + 16 + 2 = 47$.

**Probability.** $\tfrac{47}{256}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } \tfrac{47}{256}}$.

</details>

## Problem 23
*Tags: simons-favorite-factoring, vietas-formulas, integer-roots*

The zeroes of the function $f(x)=x^2-ax+2a$ are integers. What is the sum of the possible values of $a?$

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 7\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 8\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 16\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 17\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 18$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Vieta's formulas.** If $r, s$ are the roots, then $r + s = a$ and $rs = 2a$.

**Substitute.** $rs = 2(r + s)$, so
$$rs - 2r - 2s = 0 \implies (r - 2)(s - 2) = 4 \quad (\text{SFFT}).$$

**Enumerate integer factor pairs $(r - 2, s - 2)$ of $4$.**
- $(1, 4)$: $(r, s) = (3, 6)$, $a = 9$.
- $(4, 1)$: same $a = 9$ (duplicate).
- $(2, 2)$: $(r, s) = (4, 4)$, $a = 8$.
- $(-1, -4)$: $(r, s) = (1, -2)$, $a = -1$.
- $(-4, -1)$: duplicate of above.
- $(-2, -2)$: $(r, s) = (0, 0)$, $a = 0$.

**Distinct values of $a$:** $9, 8, -1, 0$. Sum: $9 + 8 - 1 + 0 = 16$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 16}$.

</details>
