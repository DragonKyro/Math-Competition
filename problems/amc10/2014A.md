## Problem 1
*Tags: fraction-arithmetic*

What is $ 10\cdot\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{5}+\tfrac{1}{10}\right)^{-1}? $

$\textbf{(A)}\ 3 \qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 8\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \frac{25}{2} \qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \frac{170}{3}\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 170$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Add the fractions.** Common denominator $10$:
$$\tfrac{1}{2} + \tfrac{1}{5} + \tfrac{1}{10} = \tfrac{5}{10} + \tfrac{2}{10} + \tfrac{1}{10} = \tfrac{8}{10} = \tfrac{4}{5}.$$

**Invert and multiply.** $10 \cdot \left(\tfrac{4}{5}\right)^{-1} = 10 \cdot \tfrac{5}{4} = \tfrac{50}{4} = \tfrac{25}{2}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } \tfrac{25}{2}}$.

</details>

## Problem 2
*Tags: rate, fractions*

Roy's cat eats $\frac{1}{3}$ of a can of cat food every morning and $\frac{1}{4}$ of a can of cat food every evening. Before feeding his cat on Monday morning, Roy opened a box containing $6$ cans of cat food. On what day of the week did the cat finish eating all the cat food in the box?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ \text{Tuesday}\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \text{Wednesday}\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \text{Thursday}\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \text{Friday}\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \text{Saturday}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Daily consumption.** $\tfrac{1}{3} + \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{7}{12}$ cans per day.

**When does the cat exceed $6$ cans consumed?** After $n$ full days the total is $\tfrac{7n}{12}$. We need the smallest $n$ for which $\tfrac{7n}{12} \ge 6$, i.e., $n \ge \tfrac{72}{7} \approx 10.29$. So after $10$ full days the cat has eaten $\tfrac{70}{12} = \tfrac{35}{6}$ cans (less than $6$), and during day $11$'s morning meal it goes over.

**Day $11$'s morning:** after that morning's $\tfrac{1}{3}$, total is $\tfrac{35}{6} + \tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{35 + 2}{6} = \tfrac{37}{6} > 6$. So the cat finishes on day $11$'s morning.

**Weekday.** Day $1$ = Monday, so day $11$ = Monday $+ 10$ = Thursday.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) Thursday}}$.

</details>

## Problem 3
*Tags: revenue-cost-profit*

Bridget bakes 48 loaves of bread for her bakery. She sells half of them in the morning for $\textdollar 2.50$ each. In the afternoon she sells two thirds of what she has left, and because they are not fresh, she charges only half price. In the late afternoon she sells the remaining loaves at a dollar each. Each loaf costs $\textdollar 0.75$ for her to make. In dollars, what is her profit for the day?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 24\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 36\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 44\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 48\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 52$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Morning.** Sells $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 48 = 24$ loaves at $\$2.50$: revenue $= 24 \cdot 2.50 = \$60$. Remaining: $24$ loaves.

**Afternoon.** Sells $\tfrac{2}{3} \cdot 24 = 16$ loaves at $\$1.25$ (half of $\$2.50$): revenue $= 16 \cdot 1.25 = \$20$. Remaining: $24 - 16 = 8$ loaves.

**Late afternoon.** Sells $8$ loaves at $\$1.00$: revenue $= \$8$.

**Total revenue.** $60 + 20 + 8 = \$88$.

**Total cost.** $48 \cdot 0.75 = \$36$.

**Profit.** $88 - 36 = \$52$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } 52}$.

</details>

## Problem 4
*Tags: casework, permutations-with-constraints*

Walking down Jane Street, Ralph passed four houses in a row, each painted a different color. He passed the orange house before the red house, and he passed the blue house before the yellow house. The blue house was not next to the yellow house. How many orderings of the colored houses are possible?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 2\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 3\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 4\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 5\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 6$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Constraints.** Orderings of $\{$O, R, B, Y$\}$ in a row satisfying: O before R; B before Y; B and Y not adjacent.

**Enumerate by B's position.** Since B is before Y and they're not adjacent, $Y$ is at least $2$ positions after $B$.

- B in position $1$: $Y \in \{3, 4\}$.
  - $Y = 3$: positions $2, 4$ get O, R in some order with O before R. Position $2$ = O, position $4$ = R. Ordering: $\text{B, O, Y, R}$. ✓
  - $Y = 4$: positions $2, 3$ get O, R with O before R. $\text{B, O, R, Y}$. ✓
- B in position $2$: $Y = 4$. Positions $1, 3$ get O, R with O before R. $\text{O, B, R, Y}$. ✓
- B in position $3$: $Y$ would need to be position $\ge 5$; no such position. ✗

**Total.** $3$ valid orderings.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 3}$.

</details>

## Problem 5
*Tags: mean-and-median, percentile*

On an algebra quiz, $10\%$ of the students scored $70$ points, $35\%$ scored $80$ points, $30\%$ scored $90$ points, and the rest scored $100$ points. What is the difference between the mean and median score of the students' scores on this quiz?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 1\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 2\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 3\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 4\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 5$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Percentages.** $70, 80, 90, 100$ at $10\%, 35\%, 30\%, 25\%$ respectively (since $100\% - 10 - 35 - 30 = 25\%$).

**Mean.** $0.10(70) + 0.35(80) + 0.30(90) + 0.25(100) = 7 + 28 + 27 + 25 = 87$.

**Median.** Cumulative frequency: up through $70$: $10\%$. Up through $80$: $45\%$. Up through $90$: $75\%$. So the $50$th percentile lies in the $90$-point group: median $= 90$.

**Difference.** $|90 - 87| = 3$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 3}$.

</details>

## Problem 6
*Tags: proportional-reasoning, rate*

Suppose that $a$ cows give $b$ gallons of milk in $c$ days. At this rate, how many gallons of milk will $d$ cows give in $e$ days?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \frac{bde}{ac}\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \frac{ac}{bde}\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \frac{abde}{c}\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \frac{bcde}{a}\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \frac{abc}{de}$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Rate per cow per day.** $a$ cows produce $b$ gallons in $c$ days, so one cow produces $\tfrac{b}{ac}$ gallons per day.

**Scale up.** $d$ cows for $e$ days produce $d \cdot e \cdot \tfrac{b}{ac} = \tfrac{bde}{ac}$ gallons.

**Sanity check.** Plug in $d = a$, $e = c$: answer should be $b$. $\tfrac{b \cdot a \cdot c}{ac} = b$. ✓

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(A) } \tfrac{bde}{ac}}$.

</details>

## Problem 7
*Tags: inequality-reasoning, counterexamples*

Nonzero real numbers $x$, $y$, $a$, and $b$ satisfy $x < a$ and $y < b$. How many of the following inequalities must be true?

$\textbf{(I)}\ x+y < a+b\qquad$

$
\textbf{(II)}\ x-y < a-b\qquad$

$
\textbf{(III)}\ xy < ab\qquad$

$
\textbf{(IV)}\ \frac{x}{y} < \frac{a}{b}$

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 0\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 1\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 2\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 3\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 4$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**$(I)$ must be true.** Adding the inequalities $x < a$ and $y < b$ gives $x + y < a + b$. ✓

**$(II), (III), (IV)$ can fail.** One counterexample refutes all three. Try $x = -3, y = -4, a = 1, b = 4$: $x < a$ ($-3 < 1$) ✓ and $y < b$ ($-4 < 4$) ✓.
- $(II)$: $x - y = 1$, $a - b = -3$. $1 < -3$? ✗
- $(III)$: $xy = 12$, $ab = 4$. $12 < 4$? ✗
- $(IV)$: $\tfrac{x}{y} = \tfrac{3}{4}$, $\tfrac{a}{b} = \tfrac{1}{4}$. $\tfrac{3}{4} < \tfrac{1}{4}$? ✗

So only $(I)$ must hold.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 1}$.

</details>

## Problem 8
*Tags: factorial-manipulation, perfect-squares*

Which of the following numbers is a perfect square?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ \dfrac{14!15!}2\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \dfrac{15!16!}2\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \dfrac{16!17!}2\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \dfrac{17!18!}2\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \dfrac{18!19!}2 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Simplify the general form.** $\dfrac{n! (n+1)!}{2} = \dfrac{(n!)^2 (n+1)}{2} = (n!)^2 \cdot \dfrac{n + 1}{2}$.

**Condition for perfect square.** $(n!)^2$ is already a perfect square, so the whole expression is a perfect square iff $\tfrac{n+1}{2}$ is a perfect square.

**Check $n$ values from the choices:**
- $n = 14$: $\tfrac{15}{2}$, not a perfect square.
- $n = 15$: $\tfrac{16}{2} = 8$, not a perfect square.
- $n = 16$: $\tfrac{17}{2}$, not an integer.
- $n = 17$: $\tfrac{18}{2} = 9 = 3^2$. ✓
- $n = 18$: $\tfrac{19}{2}$, not an integer.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } \tfrac{17! \cdot 18!}{2}}$.

</details>

## Problem 9
*Tags: right-triangle-altitude*

The two legs of a right triangle, which are altitudes, have lengths $2\sqrt3$ and $6$. How long is the third altitude of the triangle?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 1\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 2\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 3\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 4\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 5 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**In a right triangle,** the two legs are altitudes to each other. The third altitude drops from the right-angle vertex to the hypotenuse.

**Find the hypotenuse.** $h = \sqrt{(2\sqrt{3})^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{12 + 36} = \sqrt{48} = 4\sqrt{3}$.

**Area via legs.** Area $= \tfrac{1}{2}(2\sqrt{3})(6) = 6\sqrt{3}$.

**Third altitude** (from hypotenuse): $\tfrac{2 \cdot \text{area}}{h} = \tfrac{12\sqrt{3}}{4\sqrt{3}} = 3$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 3}$.

</details>

## Problem 11

A customer who intends to purchase an appliance has three coupons, only one of which may be used:

Coupon 1: $10\%$ off the listed price if the listed price is at least $\textdollar50$

Coupon 2: $\textdollar 20$ off the listed price if the listed price is at least $\textdollar100$

Coupon 3: $18\%$ off the amount by which the listed price exceeds $\textdollar100$

For which of the following listed prices will coupon $1$ offer a greater price reduction than either coupon $2$ or coupon $3$?

$\textbf{(A) }\textdollar179.95\qquad

A regular hexagon has side length 6. Congruent arcs with radius 3 are drawn with the center at each of the vertices, creating circular sectors as shown. The region inside the hexagon but outside the sectors is shaded as shown What is the area of the shaded region?

## Problem 13
*Tags: hexagon-decomposition, isoceles-triangle*

Equilateral $\triangle ABC$ has side length $1$, and squares $ABDE$, $BCHI$, $CAFG$ lie outside the triangle. What is the area of hexagon $DEFGHI$?

_(Asymptote diagram omitted — see AoPS for image)_

$ \textbf{(A)}\ \dfrac{12+3\sqrt3}4\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \dfrac92\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 3+\sqrt3\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \dfrac{6+3\sqrt3}2\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 6 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Decompose hexagon $DEFGHI$.** It consists of:
- The central equilateral triangle $\triangle ABC$ (area $\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4}$).
- The $3$ outer squares (each area $1$).
- The $3$ "corner" triangles connecting adjacent squares at each vertex of $\triangle ABC$.

**Corner triangles.** At vertex $A$ (say), the two adjacent squares are $ABDE$ and $CAFG$; the corner triangle is $\triangle AEF$ with $AE = AF = 1$ and the interior angle at $A$ equal to $360^\circ - 60^\circ - 90^\circ - 90^\circ = 120^\circ$.

**Area of each corner triangle.** Using the formula $\tfrac{1}{2} a b \sin \theta$ with $a = b = 1$, $\theta = 120^\circ$:
$$\text{area} = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 \cdot 1 \cdot \sin 120^\circ = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4}.$$
Three corner triangles: $3 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4} = \tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{4}$.

**Total.**
$$\text{Hexagon area} = \underbrace{\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{4}}_{\triangle ABC} + \underbrace{3}_{\text{3 squares}} + \underbrace{\tfrac{3\sqrt{3}}{4}}_{\text{3 corners}} = 3 + \tfrac{\sqrt{3} + 3\sqrt{3}}{4} = 3 + \sqrt{3}.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 3 + \sqrt{3}}$.

</details>

## Problem 14
*Tags: perpendicular-lines, right-triangle-median*

The $y$-intercepts, $P$ and $Q$, of two perpendicular lines intersecting at the point $A(6,8)$ have a sum of zero. What is the area of $\triangle APQ$?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 45\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 48\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 54\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 60\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 72 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** Let $P = (0, p)$ and $Q = (0, q)$ with $p + q = 0$, so $q = -p$. Thus $P$ and $Q$ are symmetric about the origin on the $y$-axis.

**Key fact about right triangles.** $\triangle APQ$ has a right angle at $A$ (since the two lines are perpendicular, and the two intercepts plus $A$ form a right angle at $A$). So $PQ$ is the hypotenuse. The midpoint of $PQ$ is the origin $O = (0, 0)$, and the median from $A$ to $O$ equals half the hypotenuse:
$$AO = \tfrac{1}{2} |PQ|.$$

**Compute $AO$.** $A = (6, 8)$, $O = (0, 0)$, so $AO = \sqrt{36 + 64} = 10$. Hence $|PQ| = 20$.

**Area of right $\triangle APQ$.** Using $PQ$ as the base: the altitude from $A$ to line $PQ$ (the $y$-axis) is just the $x$-coordinate of $A$, which is $6$. So
$$\text{area} = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 20 \cdot 6 = 60.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } 60}$.

</details>

## Problem 15
*Tags: rate-time-distance, linear-equations*

David drives from his home to the airport to catch a flight.  He drives $35$ miles in the first hour, but realizes that he will be $1$ hour late if he continues at this speed.  He increases his speed by $15$ miles per hour for the rest of the way to the airport and arrives $30$ minutes early.  How many miles is the airport from his home?

$\textbf{(A) }140\qquad
\textbf{(B) }175\qquad
\textbf{(C) }210\qquad
\textbf{(D) }245\qquad
\textbf{(E) }280\qquad$

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** After the first hour David has gone $35$ miles. Let $d$ be the remaining distance. At $35$ mph he'd arrive $1$ hour late; at $50$ mph ($35 + 15$) he arrives $\tfrac{1}{2}$ hour early. The *difference* in travel time is $1.5$ hours.

**Time equation.**
$$\frac{d}{35} - \frac{d}{50} = 1.5.$$
Common denominator $350$:
$$\frac{10d - 7d}{350} = 1.5 \implies \frac{3d}{350} = 1.5 \implies d = \frac{1.5 \cdot 350}{3} = 175.$$

**Total distance.** First hour $35$ miles + remaining $175$ miles = $210$ miles.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(C) } 210}$.

</details>

## Problem 16
*Tags: coordinate-geometry, kite-area*

In rectangle $ABCD$, $AB=1$, $BC=2$, and points $E$, $F$, and $G$ are midpoints of $\overline{BC}$, $\overline{CD}$, and $\overline{AD}$, respectively. Point $H$ is the midpoint of $\overline{GE}$. What is the area of the shaded region?

_(Asymptote diagram omitted — see AoPS for image. The shaded region is the kite formed by segments $AF$, $CH$, $BF$, and $DH$ inside the rectangle.)_

$ \textbf{(A)}\ \dfrac1{12}\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \dfrac{\sqrt3}{18}\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \dfrac{\sqrt2}{12}\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \dfrac{\sqrt3}{12}\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \dfrac16 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Set coordinates.** $D = (0, 0), C = (1, 0), B = (1, 2), A = (0, 2)$. Midpoints:
- $E$ of $BC$: $(1, 1)$.
- $F$ of $CD$: $(\tfrac{1}{2}, 0)$.
- $G$ of $AD$: $(0, 1)$.
- $H$ of $GE$: $(\tfrac{1}{2}, 1)$.

**Line equations.**
- $AF$: from $(0, 2)$ to $(\tfrac{1}{2}, 0)$. Slope $-4$. Equation: $y = -4x + 2$.
- $DH$: from $(0, 0)$ to $(\tfrac{1}{2}, 1)$. Slope $2$. Equation: $y = 2x$.

**Intersection of $AF$ and $DH$** (one vertex of the kite):
$$-4x + 2 = 2x \implies 6x = 2 \implies x = \tfrac{1}{3}, \quad y = \tfrac{2}{3}.$$

So one vertex is at $(\tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{2}{3})$.

**By symmetry (reflection about the vertical line $x = \tfrac{1}{2}$),** the opposite vertex (intersection of $BF$ and $CH$) is at $(1 - \tfrac{1}{3}, \tfrac{2}{3}) = (\tfrac{2}{3}, \tfrac{2}{3})$.

**The kite has diagonals.** $FH$ is vertical from $(\tfrac{1}{2}, 0)$ to $(\tfrac{1}{2}, 1)$ — length $1$. The other diagonal (horizontal, connecting the two computed intersection points) has length $\tfrac{2}{3} - \tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{1}{3}$.

**Kite area** = $\tfrac{1}{2}$ (product of diagonals) = $\tfrac{1}{2} \cdot 1 \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{1}{6}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } \tfrac{1}{6}}$.

</details>

## Problem 17
*Tags: dice-probability, casework*

Three fair six-sided dice are rolled. What is the probability that the values shown on two of the dice sum to the value shown on the remaining die?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ \dfrac16\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ \dfrac{13}{72}\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ \dfrac7{36}\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ \dfrac5{24}\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ \dfrac29 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Event.** Two of the three dice sum to the third. Equivalently, some ordered pair of dice sums to the third. Let's count the number of ordered outcomes $(d_1, d_2, d_3)$ (out of $6^3 = 216$) where $d_i + d_j = d_k$ for some assignment.

**Count configurations where the third die equals the sum of the first two.** For each ordered pair of dice values $(a, b)$ with $a + b \le 6$, one specific value of the third die works. Values of $a + b$ range from $2$ to $6$:
- Sum $2$: $(1, 1)$. $1$ ordered pair.
- Sum $3$: $(1, 2), (2, 1)$. $2$.
- Sum $4$: $(1, 3), (3, 1), (2, 2)$. $3$.
- Sum $5$: $(1, 4), (4, 1), (2, 3), (3, 2)$. $4$.
- Sum $6$: $(1, 5), (5, 1), (2, 4), (4, 2), (3, 3)$. $5$.

Total: $1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 = 15$ ordered pairs.

For each, the third die is forced to one value. So the number of ordered triples with (die$_1$, die$_2$) summing to die$_3$ is $15$.

**Multiply by $3$ for which die is the "sum".** There are $3$ choices for which die plays the role of the sum. So total outcomes $= 3 \cdot 15 = 45$.

**Check no double-counting.** Could a triple simultaneously satisfy "die$_1$ + die$_2$ = die$_3$" and "die$_1$ + die$_3$ = die$_2$"? Then die$_2$ = die$_1$ + die$_3$ and die$_3$ = die$_1$ + die$_2$, which gives die$_2$ = die$_1$ + die$_1$ + die$_2$, so die$_1$ = $0$. Impossible on a die. So no overlap.

**Probability.** $\tfrac{45}{216} = \tfrac{5}{24}$.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(D) } \tfrac{5}{24}}$.

</details>

## Problem 18
*Tags: tilted-square, coordinate-geometry*

A square in the coordinate plane has vertices whose $y$-coordinates are $0$, $1$, $4$, and $5$. What is the area of the square?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ 16\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 17\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 25\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 26\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 27 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**Setup.** The vertices have $y$-coordinates $0, 1, 4, 5$. Going around the square, consecutive vertices have $y$-coordinate differences (in some order) following the side vectors.

**Key observation.** A square's four sides come in two perpendicular pairs. If one side has vertical rise $\Delta y$ and horizontal run $\Delta x$, the perpendicular side has rise $-\Delta x$ and run $\Delta y$ (or the opposite sign).

**Order the vertices by $y$-coordinate.** Label them $A$ (at $y = 0$), $B$ (at $y = 1$), $C$ (at $y = 4$), $D$ (at $y = 5$). By symmetry, $A$ and $D$ are opposite vertices (diagonal endpoints), as are $B$ and $C$. The square is a rotated square where opposite corners have $y$-differences $5 - 0 = 5$ and $4 - 1 = 3$.

**Side length via Pythagorean.** Consider the side from $A$ to $B$: $\Delta y = 1$, and by the square's geometry, the perpendicular side (from $B$ to $C$) has $\Delta y = 4 - 1 = 3$. These two sides are perpendicular and equal length (side of square), so:
- Side from $A$ to $B$: $(\Delta x_1, 1)$ with length $s = \sqrt{\Delta x_1^2 + 1}$.
- Side from $B$ to $C$: $(\Delta x_2, 3)$ with length $s = \sqrt{\Delta x_2^2 + 9}$.
- Perpendicularity: $\Delta x_1 \Delta x_2 + 1 \cdot 3 = 0$, so $\Delta x_1 \Delta x_2 = -3$.

Equal lengths: $\Delta x_1^2 + 1 = \Delta x_2^2 + 9$, so $\Delta x_1^2 - \Delta x_2^2 = 8$.

Let $u = \Delta x_1$, $v = \Delta x_2$. Then $uv = -3$ and $u^2 - v^2 = 8$. From $v = -3/u$: $u^2 - 9/u^2 = 8$, so $u^4 - 8u^2 - 9 = 0$. Quadratic in $u^2$: $u^2 = \tfrac{8 \pm \sqrt{64 + 36}}{2} = \tfrac{8 \pm 10}{2}$. Positive root: $u^2 = 9$, so $u = \pm 3$, giving $v = \mp 1$.

**Side length.** $s^2 = u^2 + 1 = 9 + 1 = 10$? Wait — recheck: $s = \sqrt{\Delta x_1^2 + 1}$ with $\Delta x_1^2 = 9$: $s^2 = 10$? Hmm, let me reverify.

Actually, let me reconsider: the side $AB$ has $\Delta y = 1$. So $s^2 = \Delta x_1^2 + 1$. If $u^2 = 9$, then $s^2 = 10$.

But I also need to check that $B$ and $C$ are adjacent (not diagonal). The square has $4$ vertices, so given $\{A, B, C, D\}$ ordered by $y$, the adjacency pattern depends on the square's orientation. Let me try the pattern where $A$ and $C$ are adjacent (instead of $A, B$ adjacent).

Actually, retry: for a tilted square with $y$-coords $\{0, 1, 4, 5\}$, the correct adjacency (by standard configuration) is $A-B-D-C$ cyclic, or equivalently, going around the square we see $y$-differences of $1, 4, 1, -4$ (rotated). ## Problem 22
*Tags: rectangle, 30-60-90-triangle, tan-15*

In rectangle $ABCD$, $\overline{AB}=20$ and $\overline{BC}=10$. Let $E$ be a point on $\overline{CD}$ such that $\angle CBE=15^\circ$. What is $\overline{AE}$?

$ \textbf{(A)}\ \dfrac{20\sqrt3}3\qquad\textbf{(B)}\ 10\sqrt3\qquad\textbf{(C)}\ 18\qquad\textbf{(D)}\ 11\sqrt3\qquad\textbf{(E)}\ 20 $

<details><summary>Solution</summary>

**$\angle CBE = 15^\circ$.** From $B = (0, 10)$, the angle between ray $BC$ (pointing to $C = (0, 0)$, i.e., downward) and ray $BE$ (to $E = (e, 0)$) equals $15^\circ$.

**Compute $e$.** The tangent of $\angle CBE$ (in right triangle $CBE$ with right angle at $C$) is
$$\tan(\angle CBE) = \frac{CE}{BC} = \frac{e}{10} = \tan 15^\circ.$$

**Compute $\tan 15^\circ$.** Using $\tan(45^\circ - 30^\circ)$:
$$\tan 15^\circ = \frac{\tan 45^\circ - \tan 30^\circ}{1 + \tan 45^\circ \tan 30^\circ} = \frac{1 - \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}}{1 + \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}} = \frac{\sqrt{3} - 1}{\sqrt{3} + 1} = \frac{(\sqrt{3} - 1)^2}{2} = \frac{4 - 2\sqrt{3}}{2} = 2 - \sqrt{3}.$$

**Find $DE$ and $AE$.** $DE = 20 - e = 10\sqrt{3}$. In right triangle $ADE$ (right angle at $D$, since $AD$ vertical and $DE$ horizontal):
$$AE = \sqrt{AD^2 + DE^2} = \sqrt{10^2 + (10\sqrt{3})^2} = \sqrt{100 + 300} = \sqrt{400} = 20.$$

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(E) } 20}$.

## Problem 25

**Each "triple-slot" interval gives one valid pair $(m, n)$.** So there are $279$ valid pairs.

The answer is $\boxed{\textbf{(B) } 279}$.
